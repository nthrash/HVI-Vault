Scalar Waveforms — Overview

The three scalars (pressure, flow, volume) plotted against time. Understanding normal appearance in each mode is the foundation of waveform interpretation.

Pressure-Time Waveform

The pressure scalar is the workhorse. It reveals airway resistance (PIP−Pplat), lung compliance (Pplat−PEEP), and dyssynchrony patterns like flow starvation.

Normal Pressure Waveform (ACVC) Linear ramp rise to PIP, plateau pressure after inspiratory hold, return to PEEP. Key landmarks labeled. Deranged Physiology
High vs Normal Airway Resistance High PIP with normal Pplat = increased PIP−Pplat gap = airway resistance problem (secretions, bronchospasm, kinked ETT). Pplat unchanged because compliance is preserved. Deranged Physiology
Decreased Compliance — Pressure Waveform Both PIP and Pplat rise together = compliance problem (ARDS, pulmonary edema, pneumothorax). PIP−Pplat gap stays normal because resistance is unchanged. Deranged Physiology

Flow-Time Waveform

The flow waveform is the most sensitive detector of auto-PEEP (expiratory flow not returning to zero) and secretions (saw-tooth pattern).

Normal Flow Waveform Square inspiratory flow (ACVC) above the baseline, passive exponential expiratory flow below. Flow returns to zero before the next breath — no air trapping. Deranged Physiology
ACPC — Flow Decelerates to Zero In pressure control, inspiratory flow decelerates and reaches zero at the end of Ti — the lung is fully filled. This is the expected pattern; if it doesn't reach zero, Ti may be too short. Deranged Physiology
Insufficient Inspiratory Time (ACPC) Flow is cut off before reaching zero — Ti is too short. The lung hasn't finished filling. This results in reduced tidal volume delivery. Deranged Physiology
Air Trapping / Auto-PEEP + Increased Airway Resistance Expiratory flow does not return to zero before the next breath. This is the gold-standard waveform sign of auto-PEEP. Also shows prolonged expiratory flow pattern with increased resistance. Deranged Physiology

Pressure-Volume (PV) Loops

PV loops reveal lung compliance (slope), overdistension (beaking at UIP), atelectasis (LIP), and work of breathing (loop area). Traced counterclockwise.

PV Loop — Spontaneous Breathing PV loop in a spontaneously breathing patient. Note the work of breathing is represented by the leftward deflection below the trigger point (patient effort). Deranged Physiology
Anatomy of the PV Loop Labeled components: inspiratory limb, expiratory limb, compliance slope, hysteresis area, lower inflection point (LIP), upper inflection point (UIP/beaking). Deranged Physiology
Normal PV Loop — Volume Control (CMV/ACVC) Ideal PV loop in volume-controlled ventilation. Note the characteristic shape — pressure rises as volume is delivered, forming a closed loop with normal hysteresis. Deranged Physiology
Normal PV Loop — Pressure Control (PCV/ACPC) PV loop in pressure-controlled ventilation. The pressure rises rapidly to the set level, then volume fills in — different shape from volume control. Deranged Physiology
PV Loop — SIMV / PRVC PV loop appearance in SIMV and PRVC modes. Note how PRVC loops may vary breath-to-breath as the ventilator auto-adjusts pressure to achieve target tidal volume. Deranged Physiology
PV Loops Summary — Key Patterns Summary diagram comparing normal PV loop against abnormal patterns. Quick reference for compliance changes, overdistension (beaking), and air trapping (loop not closing). Deranged Physiology
PV Loops in Disease States PV loop changes across pathologic states: decreased compliance (ARDS, pulmonary edema), increased resistance (bronchospasm), air trapping. Loops shift right and widen with disease. Deranged Physiology

Flow-Volume (FV) Loops

FV loops reveal airway obstruction (scooped expiratory limb), bronchodilator response, and air trapping (loop doesn't close).

Flow-Volume Loop Patterns Normal FV loop vs obstructive pattern (scooped expiratory limb), restrictive pattern (small compressed loop), and air trapping (truncated expiratory limb). Key patterns for bedside interpretation. Deranged Physiology
Flow-Volume Loop — LITFL LITFL's FV loop diagram showing normal loop anatomy with inspiratory (upper) and expiratory (lower) limbs labeled. Foundation for recognizing obstructive vs restrictive patterns. LITFL

Patient-Ventilator Dyssynchrony

Dyssynchrony affects 25–50% of ventilated patients. These waveform patterns allow early detection — often before clinical signs are obvious.

Ineffective Triggering — Mandatory Mode Wasted patient effort in a mandatory ventilation mode. Small flow/pressure deflections that don't result in a triggered breath. Cause: trigger too insensitive or significant auto-PEEP. Deranged Physiology
Ineffective Triggering — Trigger Sensitivity Too High Patient effort is visible but not reaching the trigger threshold. Patient is working hard to breathe but the ventilator isn't responding. Fix: increase trigger sensitivity (lower threshold). Deranged Physiology
Flow Starvation — Pressure Scalloping (ACVC) The normally convex/linear pressure rise becomes concave ("scooped" or "scalloped") during inspiration. Patient demand exceeds set flow rate. Fix: increase flow to 60–80 L/min or switch to ACPC. Deranged Physiology
Auto-PEEP — Increased Respiratory Effort Auto-PEEP forces the patient to generate extra inspiratory effort just to overcome intrinsic PEEP before reaching trigger threshold. Results in wasted efforts and respiratory distress. Especially dangerous in RV failure and LVAD patients. Deranged Physiology
Auto-Triggering — Cardiac Oscillations Cardiac contractions transmit pressure/flow oscillations into the circuit, which can be misinterpreted by the ventilator as patient effort, triggering auto-triggered breaths. Fix: reduce trigger sensitivity. Deranged Physiology
Double Triggering & Premature Breath Termination Two consecutive breaths fire without an expiratory pause — the patient's neural inspiration outlasts the ventilator's Ti. Results in stacked tidal volumes (up to 2× Vt). Dangerous in ARDS. Fix: lengthen Ti, switch to ACPC, or deepen sedation. Deranged Physiology
