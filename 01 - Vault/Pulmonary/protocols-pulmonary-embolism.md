# Pulmonary Embolism: Diagnosis & Management

## 1. Overview & Classification

### Temporal Pattern

| Category | Description |
|---|---|
| **Acute** | Symptoms develop immediately after pulmonary vascular obstruction |
| **Subacute** | Symptoms emerge over days to weeks following the initial event |
| **Chronic** | Progressive pulmonary hypertension over years — Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension (CTEPH) |

### Hemodynamic Stratification (IBCC Framework)

| Category | Definition | Disposition |
|---|---|---|
| **Low-Risk PE** | Normal hemodynamics, no RV dysfunction, low biomarkers | Outpatient or short observation |
| **Low-Risk Submassive** | RV dilation present but hemodynamically stable, low mortality risk | Ward admission; monitor |
| **High-Risk Submassive** | Hemodynamically stable but elevated mortality; RV dysfunction + biomarker elevation | ICU admission; consider advanced therapy |
| **Non-Crashing Massive** | Hypotension stabilizing on low-dose vasopressor | ICU; advanced therapy indicated |
| **Crashing Massive** | Persistent hemodynamic instability/collapse; highest immediate mortality risk | ICU/emergent intervention |

### Anatomical Location

- **Saddle** — Lodged at bifurcation of main pulmonary artery; extends into R and L main PAs. Occurs in ~3–6% of PE. Often hemodynamically catastrophic.
- **Lobar** — Within lobar pulmonary arteries
- **Segmental** — Within segmental branches
- **Subsegmental** — Distal; clinical significance debated; anticoagulation decision individualized

## 2. Pathophysiology & Etiology

### Virchow's Triad

All PE pathogenesis relates to one or more components:

1. **Venous Stasis** — immobility, heart failure, compression, obesity
2. **Endothelial Injury** — trauma, surgery, indwelling catheters/leads
3. **Hypercoagulable State** — inherited thrombophilia, malignancy, estrogen, pregnancy

### Source of Emboli
- Majority originate from the **deep veins of the lower extremities** — most commonly the **common femoral vein** and popliteal vein (proximal DVT carries highest embolization risk)
- Upper extremity DVT (axillary/subclavian): increasing in frequency due to PICC lines, pacemaker leads, and LVAD hardware
- Pelvic veins (post-surgical or obstetric)

### Genetic Risk Factors
More than 50 genetic risk factors for VTE have been identified, including:
- **Factor V Leiden** (most common inherited thrombophilia; 5% of general population)
- **Prothrombin gene mutation G20210A**
- Protein C/S deficiency, antithrombin deficiency
- Antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (acquired hypercoagulable state)

## 3. Diagnosis

### Clinical Presentation

**Symptoms**
- Dyspnea (most common), pleuritic chest pain, cough, hemoptysis
- Syncope or pre-syncope (suggests massive PE)
- Sense of **impending doom** — a clinically significant symptom; take seriously

**Physical Exam**
- Tachycardia (most sensitive sign)
- Tachypnea — initially compensatory; when patient can no longer sustain work of breathing → respiratory failure/crash
- Hypoxemia (severity proportional to clot burden)
- Hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg) or >40 mmHg drop from baseline → massive PE
- Elevated JVP, RV heave, loud P2 — signs of acute RV strain
- Lower extremity swelling/asymmetry (concurrent DVT)

### Diagnostic Workup

| Test | Finding | Clinical Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **D-Dimer** | Elevated (>500 ng/mL or age-adjusted) | Sensitive, not specific; used to rule out in low-probability patients. Near-universal elevation in HF patients — skip D-dimer and go to imaging |
| **Troponin I** | >0.4 ng/mL | RV myocardial injury; elevated = worse prognosis |
| **Troponin T** | >0.1 ng/mL | Same significance as above |
| **BNP/NT-proBNP** | BNP >90 pg/mL | RV pressure overload; nearly all HF patients elevated at baseline |
| **ABG** | ↓PaO2, ↓PaCO2, respiratory alkalosis | Hypoxemia + hyperventilation → compensatory alkalosis early; CO2 rises late (ominous) |
| **ECG** | Sinus tach; S1Q3T3; new RBBB; RV strain pattern | S1Q3T3 classic but nonspecific; new RBBB concerning |
| **CXR** | Hampton's hump, Westermark sign, cardiomegaly | Often normal; primarily used to exclude other diagnoses |
| **CTA-PE** | Filling defect in pulmonary arteries | Gold standard; also assess RV/LV ratio and hepatic reflux |
| **Echo (TTE/TEE)** | RV dilation, septal flattening (D-sign), McConnell's sign | RV assessment; bedside echo essential in unstable patients |
| **V/Q Scan** | High probability mismatch | Use when CT contraindicated (contrast allergy, renal failure) |
| **Lower extremity duplex** | Proximal DVT | Supports diagnosis; may negate need for CT if clinical probability high |

### CT Findings Suggesting RV Strain/Failure
- **RV/LV diameter ratio >0.9** on 4-chamber view → RV dilation
- **Contrast reflux into hepatic veins or IVC** — highly suggestive of elevated right-sided pressures and RV dysfunction
- Main pulmonary artery dilation

### Echo Findings
- **RV/LV diameter ratio >0.9** in apical 4-chamber view
- **D-sign** — septal flattening in parasternal short axis (interventricular dependence)
- **McConnell's sign** — RV free wall hypokinesis with preserved apical motion (relatively specific for PE)
- Estimated RVSP elevation via TR jet
- Absence of RV hypertrophy favors acute (not chronic) RV strain

## 4. Risk Stratification

### sPESI Score (Simplified PESI) — Preferred for ICU Triage

| Variable | Points |
|---|---|
| Age >80 years | 1 |
| Cancer | 1 |
| Chronic cardiopulmonary disease | 1 |
| HR ≥110 bpm | 1 |
| SBP <100 mmHg | 1 |
| SpO2 <90% | 1 |

**Score 0** = Low risk (~1% 30-day mortality) — may consider outpatient management
**Score ≥1** = Elevated risk — admit; further risk stratification required

> sPESI has 97–99% sensitivity for mortality; preferred for rapid ICU triage. Full PESI (11 variables, >120 = high-risk) offers better discrimination but is more complex.

### BOVA Score — Best for Submassive PE Risk Stratification

| Variable | Points |
|---|---|
| HR ≥110 bpm | 1 |
| SBP 90–100 mmHg | 2 |
| Elevated troponin | 2 |
| RV dysfunction on echo or CT | 2 |

| Score | Risk Class | 30-Day Complication Rate |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 | Stage I (Low) | 3.5% |
| 3–4 | Stage II (Intermediate) | 10.7% |
| ≥5 | Stage III (High) | 24.3% |

> BOVA better identifies submassive PE patients at high risk of deterioration who merit advanced therapy consideration.

### Geneva Score
Used primarily for pre-test clinical probability assessment in the outpatient/ED setting before imaging. Less relevant once PE is confirmed on CT.

## 5. RV Strain vs. RV Failure — Key Distinction

Understanding where the patient sits on this spectrum determines urgency of intervention.

| Feature | RV Strain / Dysfunction | RV Failure |
|---|---|---|
| **Hemodynamics** | SBP ≥90 mmHg; no vasopressor | SBP <90 mmHg OR vasopressor-dependent |
| **RV/LV Ratio (CT or Echo)** | 0.9–1.0 | >1.0 (severe dilation) |
| **Septal Motion** | Mild flattening | D-sign; paradoxical septal motion |
| **Biomarkers** | Troponin/BNP elevated | Troponin/BNP markedly elevated; rising lactate |
| **Mental Status** | Alert | Altered; signs of low output |
| **RV Hypokinesis** | Mild-moderate | Severe; may see McConnell's sign |
| **Tricuspid Regurgitation** | Moderate elevation of RVSP | TR may be severe; RVSP may be low (indicates severe failure) |
| **Clinical Significance** | High-risk submassive | Massive PE; emergent intervention |

> **Key Pearl:** In acute massive PE, RV has not had time to hypertrophy. A thin, dilated, hypokinetic RV = acute. A thick, hypertrophied RV suggests chronic underlying pulmonary hypertension on top of acute event. This changes prognosis and management.

> **Key Pearl:** Falling RVSP on echo in the setting of worsening hemodynamics is an ominous sign — it indicates the RV is failing and can no longer generate pressure, not that the PE is resolving.

## 6. Hemodynamic Management

### Vasopressors — Order of Preference

| Agent | Role | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Norepinephrine** | **First-line** (0.05–2 mcg/kg/min) | Maintains systemic vascular resistance and RV coronary perfusion pressure; supports MAP ≥65 mmHg |
| **Vasopressin** | Second-line adjunct | May augment MAP without further tachycardia |
| **Dopamine** | Alternative second-line | Tachycardia risk; can worsen RV demand |
| **Dobutamine** | Low-dose for RV inotropic support | Use if hypoperfusion persists despite adequate MAP; risk of vasodilation |
| **Epinephrine** | Bridge in cardiac arrest or peri-arrest | Push-dose epinephrine 10–20 mcg boluses for peri-intubation hemodynamic support |

> ⚠️ **Avoid pure alpha-1 agonists (phenylephrine)** — increases RV afterload without improving contractility; can precipitate RV failure.

### Fluid Resuscitation
- RV is **preload-dependent but afterload-sensitive**
- Modest fluid bolus (250–500 mL) may be trialed if CVP is low
- **Avoid aggressive fluid resuscitation** — RV distension worsens septal shift → reduces LV filling → worsens systemic output → death spiral
- If CVP already elevated, additional fluids are harmful
- Use bedside echo to guide fluid responsiveness (IVC collapsibility, septal position)

### Inhaled Pulmonary Vasodilators
- **Inhaled nitric oxide (iNO)** or **inhaled epoprostenol** — reduces pulmonary vascular resistance acutely
- Can be administered via HFNC or ventilator circuit
- Consider early in patients with evidence of severe RV afterload elevation
- Useful bridge while awaiting definitive therapy

## 7. Respiratory Management

### Avoid Intubation if at All Possible

Intubation in massive PE dramatically increases risk of cardiac arrest:
- Sedatives → ↓ SVR → hypotension
- Positive pressure → ↓ RV preload → worsens RV output
- Overdistension → compresses pulmonary capillaries → ↑ PVR → worsens RV afterload

**Preferred approach:**
- **High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC):** Start at 40–60 L/min, FiO2 100%; titrate to SpO2 >92% and patient comfort
- **BiPAP/CPAP:** Acceptable if no contraindications; use cautiously — positive pressure still carries hemodynamic risk in severe RV failure

### If Intubation is Unavoidable

1. **Pre-optimize hemodynamics** — target SBP ~130–140 mmHg before induction, typically with epinephrine infusion
2. **Push-dose epinephrine ready** — 10–20 mcg IV boluses for hypotension or bradycardia peri-intubation
3. **Inhaled pulmonary vasodilator at bedside** — administer through vent circuit immediately after intubation
4. **Preoxygenation + apneic oxygenation** — maximize margin for desaturation; use most experienced operator
5. **Hemodynamically stable induction agent** — **Ketamine** preferred (maintains sympathetic tone); avoid propofol/etomidate if hypotensive
6. **Post-intubation ventilation** — avoid overdistension; use low tidal volumes (6 mL/kg IBW); avoid breath-stacking; target plateau pressure <30 cmH2O
7. **Watch closely for first 10 minutes** — highest-risk period post-intubation; have defibrillator, code cart, and ECMO team on standby if available

> **Key Pearl:** The most dangerous moment is often ~5 minutes post-intubation — initial stabilization gives false reassurance while the patient is sliding into hemodynamic collapse.

## 8. Treatment by Risk Stratification

### Low-Risk PE
- Anticoagulation alone
- DOACs preferred (apixaban or rivaroxaban) — see Section 9
- Consider outpatient treatment if sPESI = 0 and reliable follow-up

### Submassive PE (High-Risk)
- **Anticoagulation** — UFH infusion (80 units/kg bolus, then 18 units/kg/hr; target aPTT 60–100 sec)
- **ICU admission** and continuous monitoring
- Reassess RV function every 6–12 hours
- **PERT activation** (if available) — multidisciplinary team for advanced therapy decision
- Consider advanced therapy (CDT, mechanical thrombectomy) if clinical deterioration or high BOVA score (≥5)
- **Systemic thrombolysis** if rapidly deteriorating toward massive (see below)

### Massive PE
- **UFH** immediately upon diagnosis
- **Advanced therapy** — one of the following (PERT decision):
  - Systemic thrombolysis (if no contraindications)
  - Catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT/EKOS)
  - Mechanical thrombectomy (FlowTriever, Indigo)
  - Surgical embolectomy
  - VA-ECMO as bridge to above
- Hemodynamic support per Section 6
- Avoid intubation if possible (Section 7)

## 9. Anticoagulation

### Agent Selection

| Scenario | Preferred Agent |
|---|---|
| Massive PE / pre-intervention | UFH IV (reversible, titratable, short half-life) |
| Submassive PE (stable) | UFH IV or LMWH |
| Hemodynamically stable, no intervention planned | DOAC (apixaban or rivaroxaban preferred) |
| Renal failure (CrCl <30 mL/min) | UFH IV (avoid LMWH and most DOACs) |
| HIT or heparin contraindication | Argatroban (direct thrombin inhibitor) |
| Pregnancy | LMWH (enoxaparin) |
| Active malignancy | LMWH or DOAC (rivaroxaban/apixaban preferred over warfarin) |

### Dosing

**UFH:**
- Bolus: 80 units/kg IV
- Infusion: 18 units/kg/hr
- Target aPTT: 60–100 seconds (2.0–2.5× control)
- Use weight-based nomogram; check aPTT every 6 hours until therapeutic, then q24h

**LMWH (Enoxaparin):**
- 1 mg/kg SQ q12h OR 1.5 mg/kg SQ daily
- Reduce to 1 mg/kg SQ daily if CrCl 15–30 mL/min
- Avoid if CrCl <15 mL/min

**DOACs:**

| Drug | PE Dosing |
|---|---|
| Apixaban (Eliquis) | 10 mg PO BID × 7 days → 5 mg PO BID (no bridging) |
| Rivaroxaban (Xarelto) | 15 mg PO BID × 21 days → 20 mg PO daily with evening meal (no bridging) |
| Dabigatran (Pradaxa) | Requires 5–10 days parenteral anticoagulation first, then 150 mg PO BID |

> DOACs are preferred for hemodynamically stable PE with no anticipated interventions. Apixaban and rivaroxaban can be initiated directly without heparin bridge.

## 10. Advanced Therapies

### Systemic Thrombolysis (tPA)

**Indications:**
- Massive PE with hemodynamic collapse (strongest indication — potential benefit > risk)
- Submassive PE rapidly deteriorating toward hemodynamic collapse despite anticoagulation (individualize)

**Dosing:**
- **Alteplase 100 mg IV over 2 hours** (standard dose)
- If cardiac arrest: **0.6 mg/kg IV** (max 50 mg) over 15 minutes (accelerated protocol)
- Hold heparin during infusion; restart without bolus once aPTT <80 sec

**Absolute Contraindications:**
- Active internal bleeding
- Prior intracranial hemorrhage
- Ischemic stroke within 3 months
- Intracranial or spinal surgery/trauma within 3 months
- Intracranial neoplasm, AVM, or aneurysm
- Significant closed-head or facial trauma within 3 months

**Relative Contraindications:**
- SBP >180 mmHg or DBP >110 mmHg
- Recent major surgery (within 10 days)
- Prolonged CPR (>10 minutes)
- Pregnancy
- Active peptic ulcer disease
- LVAD (increased hemorrhagic risk — see Section 12)

**Evidence:** PEITHO trial (submassive PE) — tenecteplase reduced hemodynamic collapse/death (2.2% vs 5.6%, p=0.04) but increased major bleeding (5.0% vs 2.4%) including intracranial hemorrhage. Reserve systemic lysis for massive PE; consider carefully in submassive.

### Catheter-Directed Thrombolysis (CDT) / EKOS

- Catheter placed directly into clot; low-dose tPA infused locally
- **EKOS (EkoSonic):** Ultrasound-assisted CDT — ultrasound energy disperses fibrin matrix, allowing lower tPA dose penetration
- Standard EKOS dose: **20 mg tPA total over 15–24 hours** (split across bilateral catheters if bilateral PE)
- ULTIMA trial: EKOS significantly reduced RV/LV ratio vs UFH alone with only 2% major bleeding
- Advantage over systemic lysis: Lower systemic tPA exposure → reduced ICH/major bleeding risk
- Preferred over systemic lysis in submassive PE or patients with relative contraindications to systemic thrombolysis

### Mechanical Thrombectomy

**FlowTriever (Inari Medical):**
- Large-bore aspiration catheter — mechanically removes clot without thrombolytics
- FLARE trial: >90% technical success; RV/LV ratio improved from 1.08 → 0.87; 6.7% major adverse events
- No lytic drugs → lowest bleeding risk of all advanced therapies (~2–3%)
- Increasingly first-line for massive PE or patients with contraindications to thrombolytics

**Indigo (Penumbra):**
- Aspiration-based thrombectomy; 75–85% technical success
- Alternative to FlowTriever; manual suction aspiration

**Advantage:** Faster than CDT (30–60 minutes); no systemic lytic exposure; preferred in high bleeding risk patients, post-surgical, LVAD.

### Surgical Pulmonary Embolectomy

**Indications:**
- Massive PE with hemodynamic collapse refractory to medical therapy
- Contraindication to thrombolysis with massive PE
- Failed catheter-based intervention
- Central clot (saddle PE) amenable to surgical extraction
- Massive PE with intracardiac thrombus or patent foramen ovale (paradoxical embolism)

**Outcomes:**
- Hospital mortality 20–30% in contemporary series (vs ~50% in untreated massive PE)
- Best outcomes at experienced centers with rapid team activation

### VA-ECMO

- Indicated for hemodynamic collapse unresponsive to vasopressors (cardiogenic shock from massive PE)
- Use as **bridge** to definitive therapy (thrombectomy, CDT, or surgical embolectomy) — not as standalone treatment
- Femoral-femoral cannulation typical for rapid deployment
- Complications: Major bleeding (30%), limb ischemia (5–15%), LV distension (consider LV venting)
- ECMO circuit can pull clot from proximal vessels — especially with large iliac/IVC clot burden
- If LVAD patient on VA-ECMO: consider circuit exchange risk; monitor pump flows

> **Key Clinical Pearl (from direct ICU experience):** In patients with large clot burden involving the IVC/iliac veins, ECMO can pull additional thrombus into the circuit — be prepared for circuit exchange. This has been observed on multiple occasions in massive PE on ECMO.

### PE Response Team (PERT)

- Multidisciplinary team: cardiology, pulmonology, CT/IR, cardiac surgery, hematology, ICU
- Activated for any high-risk submassive or massive PE
- **Outcomes:** PERT activation associated with 20–40% increase in advanced therapy use and reduced 30-day mortality in observational series; faster time-to-intervention
- Contact interventional cardiology/IR and cardiac surgery early — do not wait for deterioration

## 11. PE in Heart Failure Patients

### Why This Matters
- HF patients have **2–5× increased risk of VTE** compared to general population
- Risk increases as LV systolic function worsens
- Mortality from PE is approximately **7% higher** in HF patients vs non-HF
- Diagnosis is challenging — symptom and biomarker overlap is significant

### Risk Factors Specific to HF
- Reduced cardiac output → venous stasis
- Atrial fibrillation → thromboembolic risk
- Indwelling catheters, PICC lines, pacemaker/ICD leads, LVAD hardware → endothelial injury and upper extremity DVT
- Advanced age, immobility, frequent hospitalizations

### Diagnostic Challenges in HF
- **D-dimer:** Nearly universally elevated in acutely decompensated HF — skip D-dimer and proceed directly to CT-PE in hospitalized HF patients
- **Troponin:** Chronic troponin elevation in advanced HF complicates interpretation — look for acute rise above baseline
- **BNP/NT-proBNP:** Chronically elevated; look for acute disproportionate rise
- **Dyspnea/hypoxemia out of proportion** to degree of pulmonary congestion on exam or CXR → strong trigger for PE evaluation
- **New or worsening RVF** (↑JVP, hepatomegaly, edema) **without significant pulmonary rales** → subacute/chronic PE until proven otherwise
- **New RV dysfunction on echo** in a known HF patient with elevated biomarkers → evaluate for PE before attributing to HF progression

### Management Considerations in HF + PE
- Inpatient HF patients should proceed **directly to CT-PE imaging** — do not use D-dimer to rule out
- Echo is essential to differentiate new RV dysfunction from chronic
- **Lower threshold for advanced therapy** in submassive PE with HF — combined hemodynamic compromise is higher risk
- UFH preferred if renal dysfunction is present; DOACs acceptable if renal function adequate
- Volume management critically important — avoid fluids that worsen RV dilation

## 12. LVAD Patients with PE

### Unique Challenges
- LVAD patients are already on anticoagulation (warfarin ± aspirin) — yet PE can still occur
- Upper extremity DVT (from device leads/lines) is a more common source than in non-LVAD patients
- LVAD flow dynamics alter pulmonary circulation — PE may be clinically silent until severe

### Key Management Questions

**RPM Adjustments:**
- Some expert opinion supports **modest RPM reduction** if PE is causing severe RV strain — allows RV to handle reduced forward flow without further RV-LV competition
- Alternatively, **RPM increase** may augment overall flow in select patients
- No RCT data; individualize based on hemodynamics and echo guidance; involve LVAD team

**Thrombolysis in LVAD Patients:**
- Systemic tPA carries elevated hemorrhagic risk:
  - LVAD patients have high rates of **GI arteriovenous malformations (AVMs)** → GI bleeding risk
  - Risk of **intracranial hemorrhage** higher than general population (baseline anticoagulation + LVAD-associated cerebrovascular changes)
- If thrombolysis considered: weigh against bleeding risk; **catheter-directed or mechanical thrombectomy preferred** to minimize systemic lytic exposure
- Do not reflexively thrombolyze simply because hemodynamics are poor — explore mechanical options first

**ECMO in LVAD + PE:**
- VA-ECMO can be used as bridge; complex hemodynamics with coexisting LVAD
- Risk of clot being pulled into ECMO circuit (large DVT burden → IVC/iliac → circuit)
- ECMO + EKOS combination reported in severe cases

### Clinical Pearls for HF/LVAD PE
- Dyspnea and hypoxemia out of proportion to pulmonary vascular congestion → evaluate for PE
- New/worsened RVF (↑JVP, hepatomegaly, edema) without pulmonary edema → subacute/chronic PE
- All hospitalized HF patients with suspected PE: go directly to CTA-PE — skip D-dimer
- Echo essential to detect new RV dysfunction when biomarkers are chronically elevated
- RV dysfunction on echo or CT RV enlargement identifies high-risk for adverse events including early mortality
