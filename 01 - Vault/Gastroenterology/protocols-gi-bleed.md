## 1. Overview

**Acute gastrointestinal hemorrhage** is one of the most common life-threatening emergencies encountered in the ICU, accounting for ~500,000 hospital admissions and an overall mortality of 2–10% (higher in variceal hemorrhage and in patients with major comorbidity). GI bleeding is conventionally divided by anatomic origin:

- **Upper GI bleed (UGIB, ~75%)** — proximal to the ligament of Treitz
- **Small bowel bleed (~4%)** — between ligament of Treitz and ileocecal valve ("middle GI")
- **Lower GI bleed (LGIB, ~20–30%)** — distal to the ileocecal valve

> [!tip] **Bottom Line**
> The first priorities in any GI bleed are (1) airway/hemodynamic resuscitation, (2) identification of *active* vs. *stopped* bleeding, (3) rapid differentiation of upper vs. lower source, and (4) early involvement of the right proceduralist — GI, IR, or surgery. Transfuse to a hemoglobin target of **>7 g/dL** in most patients, reverse coagulopathy thoughtfully, and resist the reflex to over-resuscitate variceal bleeders.

## 2. Etiology & Classification

### Upper GI Bleed (proximal to ligament of Treitz)

| Category                | Common Causes                                                          |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Mucosal/ulcerative**  | Peptic ulcer disease (most common), esophagitis, gastritis, duodenitis |
| **Mechanical**          | Mallory-Weiss tear, hiatal hernia/Cameron lesions, NG tube irritation  |
| **Portal hypertension** | Esophageal varices, gastric varices, portal hypertensive gastropathy   |
| **Vascular**            | Dieulafoy lesion, AVMs, GAVE (gastric antral vascular ectasia)         |
| **Neoplastic**          | Upper GI malignancy                                                    |
| **Fistulous**           | Aortoenteric fistula (history of AAA repair!)                          |
| **Pancreaticobiliary**  | Hemobilia (liver trauma/biopsy/abscess), hemosuccus pancreaticus       |
| **Medication**          | SSRI, NSAID                                                            |

### Small Bowel Bleed (by age)

| Age | Most Common Cause |
|---|---|
| **>40–50 yr** | Vascular malformations (AVMs) |
| **25–40 yr** | Neoplasms (leiomyoma, adenocarcinoma, lymphoma, carcinoid) |
| **<25 yr** | Meckel diverticulum |

Other: Crohn disease, NSAID/ischemic/vasculitic ulcers, infections (CMV, TB, histoplasmosis).

### Lower GI Bleed (by age)

| Age | Most Common Cause |
|---|---|
| **>50 yr** | Diverticulosis, AVMs, malignancy, ischemic colitis |
| **<50 yr** | Infectious colitis, anorectal disease, IBD |

Additional categories:
- **Vascular:** Diverticular bleed, AVMs, colorectal varices
- **Colitis:** Ischemic, IBD, infectious (bacterial/CMV), NSAID-induced, radiation
- **Neoplastic / post-procedural:** Colon cancer, post-polypectomy bleeding
- **Anorectal:** Hemorrhoids, stercoral ulcer

### Risk Factors
NSAID / antiplatelet / anticoagulant use, *H. pylori* infection, alcohol use, cirrhosis or portal hypertension, prior GI bleed, recent endoscopy or polypectomy, AAA repair (think aortoenteric fistula), gastric bypass, advanced age.

## 3. Pathophysiology

The clinical syndrome of GI bleeding is the product of three interlocking mechanisms:

1. **Loss of vascular volume → hemorrhagic shock.** Arterial sources (peptic ulcer, Dieulafoy) bleed rapidly and under pressure. Venous sources (varices, hemorrhoids) bleed slower but can be massive. Tachycardia and narrowed pulse pressure appear first; hypotension is a late sign (20–30% blood volume loss).
2. **Loss of red cell mass → tissue hypoxia.** Hemoglobin lags behind acute volume loss by hours; a *normal* Hb in a shocked patient implies a very brisk bleed.
3. **Secondary physiologic consequences:** Swallowed blood → nitrogen load → **hepatic encephalopathy** in cirrhotics; gastric distension → **aspiration risk**; coagulopathy (from dilution, consumption, or underlying cirrhosis) → ongoing bleeding.

**Variceal hemorrhage** deserves special emphasis: bleeding is driven by the hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG). Aggressive crystalloid or blood resuscitation raises portal pressure and perpetuates the bleed — the classic "vicious cycle" of over-resuscitation. Permissive (relative) hypotension and *restrictive* transfusion are the cornerstones.

## 4. Clinical Presentation

### Manifestations & What They Mean

| Finding                        | Most Likely Source   | Notes                                                       |
| ------------------------------ | -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Hematemesis** (bright red)   | UGIB — active        | Nearly 100% specific; most worrisome finding                |
| **Coffee-ground emesis**       | UGIB — slower        | Blood congealed in stomach; less active                     |
| **Melena** (black/tarry, foul) | UGIB (90%)           | Slow right-colon bleed also possible; iron/bismuth mimic    |
| **Hematochezia** (BRBPR)       | LGIB (~85%)          | ~10–15% are massive UGIB w/ rapid transit → usually shocked |
| **Clots per rectum**           | LGIB (very strongly) | LR ~20 for lower source                                     |

### History Pearls

- NSAID / aspirin / anticoagulant use — **last dose taken?**
- Alcohol, chronic liver disease, prior varices, prior EGD/colonoscopy findings
- Prior GI bleed or high-risk lesion on prior endoscopy
- **AAA repair within 5 years** → aortoenteric fistula until proven otherwise
- Gastric bypass (altered anatomy)
- Retching → Mallory-Weiss
- Prior weight loss / anemia → malignancy
- Difficult airway history

### Physical Exam

- **Vitals + shock index (HR/SBP)** — SI >0.9 suggests significant bleed
- Orthostatic vitals (if clinically stable)
- **Stigmata of chronic liver disease** — spider angiomata, caput medusae, ascites, splenomegaly, jaundice, palmar erythema
- **Abdominal exam** — pain (PUD, ischemia, perforation), tenderness, peritoneal signs, pulsatile mass
- **Rectal exam** — *mandatory* — character and color of stool, masses, hemorrhoids, rectal tone
- **Skin/mucosa** — telangiectasias (HHT), hyperpigmented lips (Peutz-Jeghers), epistaxis source

### Red Flags 🚩

> [!warning] **Red Flags Demanding Immediate Action**
> - Ongoing hematemesis or bright-red hematochezia with instability
> - Shock index ≥1.0, SBP <90, or vasopressor requirement
> - Altered mental status (airway risk + hepatic encephalopathy)
> - Known varices, cirrhosis, or portal hypertension
> - History of AAA repair with any GI bleed → **CT angio first**
> - Exsanguinating ulcer bleed on CTA → consider direct to IR

## 5. Initial Evaluation & POCUS

### Hemodynamic POCUS

- **IVC**: collapsed, hyperdynamic LV → consistent with hemorrhagic shock; *distended IVC in shock argues against hemorrhage* as primary cause — **don't activate MTP reflexively**.
- Screen for **PH, severe AS, RV failure** — these alter resuscitation targets.

### FAST / Ascites

- RUQ + LUQ views: ascites suggests cirrhosis.
- If moderate–large ascites in a cirrhotic with UGIB → **diagnostic/therapeutic paracentesis** to exclude SBP and lower portal pressure.
- Rarely: hemoperitoneum (splenic rupture from portal hypertension, trivial trauma).

### Gastric Ultrasound

- **Collapsed stomach** → argues against active variceal/UGIB.
- **Distended stomach without recent meal** → suggests active UGIB; plan NG suction / erythromycin pre-EGD.
- Predicts aspiration risk for intubation or EGD.

## 6. Diagnostic Workup

### GI Bleed Lab Panel

| Lab | Purpose |
|---|---|
| CBC | Baseline Hb; trend every 4–6 hr in active bleed |
| BMP/CMP | BUN/Cr ratio (↑BUN:Cr >30 suggests UGIB); LFTs; electrolytes |
| INR, PTT, Fibrinogen | Coagulopathy assessment |
| **TEG / ROTEM** | Cirrhosis, DIC, suspected complex coagulopathy |
| Type & Crossmatch | Prepare for ≥2 U PRBC; activate MTP if massive |
| Lactate | Tissue perfusion marker |
| Iron studies | Chronic vs. acute blood loss |
| VBG | Rapid Hb, acid-base |

> [!tip] **BUN:Cr >30** has a positive LR of ~7.5 for UGIB (high specificity ~89%), but **sensitivity is only ~39%** — a normal ratio does not exclude an upper source. A lower threshold of ≥22 may optimize overall diagnostic performance (sensitivity 66%, specificity 71%). Digested blood = protein load absorbed in small bowel → disproportionate BUN rise

### Imaging / Procedures by Scenario

| Scenario | First-Line Test |
|---|---|
| Hematemesis / coffee grounds | **Emergent EGD** (within 24 hr; <12 hr if variceal or unstable) |
| Hematochezia + instability | **NG lavage + CT angiography** → EGD if UGIB suspected |
| Hematochezia + stable, active | **CT angiography** (STAT, while bleeding) |
| Hematochezia + stopped bleeding | Observation; repeat CTA if re-bleed; urgent colonoscopy within 24 hr |
| Prior AAA repair + any GI bleed | **CT angiography FIRST** — rule out aortoenteric fistula |
| Active extravasation on CTA | → **Interventional radiology** for embolization |
| Obscure/intermittent bleed | Capsule endoscopy, deep enteroscopy, provocative angiography |

**NG lavage**: sensitivity ~28-44% (does not exclude UGIB), specificity very high when bloody. Useful to triage EGD vs CTA in hematochezia patient — **not** routine in every bleed.

**Tagged RBC scan**: slightly more sensitive than CTA but *non-specific* and does not guide IR or surgery. Largely supplanted by multi-detector CT angiography.

## 7. Risk Stratification

### Validated Scores

| Score | Use | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Glasgow-Blatchford (GBS)** | UGIB — need for intervention | Pre-endoscopy; ≤1 = safe discharge |
| **Rockall** | UGIB — rebleeding/mortality | Pre- and post-endoscopy components |
| **AIMS65** | UGIB — inpatient mortality | Albumin, INR, Mental status, SBP, age ≥65 |
| **Oakland** | LGIB — safe discharge | Age, sex, prior admissions, vitals, Hb, PR blood |

### Clinical Red Features

| Favorable | Unfavorable |
|---|---|
| Stable hemodynamics | Shock index >1.0, SBP <90, vasopressor use |
| Profound chronic anemia (subacute bleed) | Normal Hb with shock (brisk acute bleed) |
| Recent clean EGD | Known varices or cirrhosis |
| IVC full, antrum empty | IVC empty, antrum distended |
| Reversible coagulopathy (warfarin) | Irreversible coagulopathy (DOAC without andexanet/idarucizumab) |
| No signs of ongoing bleeding | Active hematemesis + hematochezia (massive UGIB w/ rapid transit) |

> [!tip] **Profound anemia + stable vitals ≠ emergency transfusion.** These patients have been bleeding for days, are volume-replete (isovolemic anemia), and are at risk of **TACO** if rapidly transfused. Gentle transfusion, often with diuresis, is appropriate.

## 8. General Management — All GI Bleeds

### Airway & Access

- **Two 18-gauge PIVs** minimum; consider RIC if difficult access.
- A-line + large-bore central access for MTP/unstable patients.
- **Intubate** for altered mental status, severe hematemesis, inability to protect airway. Intubation is NOT benign in GI bleed — plan carefully:
  - Pre-resuscitate; consider vasopressor infusion started *before* RSI
  - NG suction to decompress stomach (varices are NOT a contraindication)
  - Gastric POCUS to assess aspiration risk
  - Erythromycin 250 mg IV or metoclopramide 10 mg IV ~30–90 min before

### Transfusion Strategy

| Clinical Scenario | Hb Target |
|---|---|
| Stable UGIB / LGIB (most patients) | **>7 g/dL** |
| Acute coronary syndrome | **>8 g/dL** |
| Massive bleed with hemodynamic instability | Activate **MTP** (1:1:1) |
| Variceal bleed | **>7 g/dL** *strict* — over-transfusion worsens bleeding |

**Platelets**: target >50 × 10⁹/L (>100 × 10⁹/L if CNS bleed).
**Fibrinogen**: cryoprecipitate if <150 mg/dL (cirrhosis) or <200 mg/dL (obstetric).
**DDAVP 0.3 mcg/kg**: consider in uremia or antiplatelet-induced bleeding.

### Coagulopathy Reversal

| Agent on Board | Reversal |
|---|---|
| Warfarin | **4-factor PCC** 25–50 units/kg + Vitamin K 10 mg IV |
| Dabigatran | **Idarucizumab** 5 g IV |
| Factor Xa inhibitors (apixaban, rivaroxaban) | **Andexanet alfa** or 4F-PCC 50 units/kg |
| Heparin | Protamine 1 mg per 100 units heparin (max 50 mg) |
| LMWH | Protamine (partial reversal only) |
| Antiplatelets | DDAVP ± platelet transfusion (evidence weak) |
| Cirrhosis | See cirrhotic coagulopathy protocol — *avoid reflexive FFP* |

### Core Pharmacology (see §12 for details)

- **PPI**: Pantoprazole 80 mg IV bolus, then 8 mg/hr infusion *or* 40 mg IV q12hr (equivalent) if UGIB suspected.
- **Octreotide**: 50 mcg IV bolus, then 50 mcg/hr infusion × 2–5 days if cirrhosis/varices suspected.
- **Ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily**: standard of care in cirrhotic UGIB (guidelines); recent meta-analyses suggest no mortality benefit, but continues to be recommended.
- **Erythromycin 250 mg IV**: prokinetic to clear stomach before EGD (contraindicated in QTc prolongation).
- **Tranexamic acid**: **NOT recommended** — HALT-IT trial showed no benefit and increased venous thromboembolism and increased seizures (did not affect arterial).

### Medications to Stop

- DVT prophylaxis, therapeutic anticoagulants
- Antihypertensives (until hemodynamically stable)
- Non-essential antiplatelets (cardiac consult if recent stent)

## 9. Upper GI Bleed — Nonvariceal

### Sequence of Care

1. **Resuscitate** → airway, access, labs, T&C, transfuse to Hb >7.
2. **Initiate PPI** (empiric, pre-endoscopy).
3. **NPO**, NG suction if massive bleeding or distended stomach.
4. **GI consult for EGD within 24 hours** (within 12 hr if unstable or variceal).
5. **Prokinetic** (erythromycin) ~30–90 min pre-EGD.
6. **Risk stratify** post-EGD using Forrest classification.

### Exceptions to EGD-First

| Scenario | First Step |
|---|---|
| AAA repair within ~5 yr + GI bleed | CT angiography (r/o aortoenteric fistula) |
| Exsanguinating duodenal ulcer on CTA | Direct to IR (posterior ulcers hard to manage endoscopically) |
| Failed EGD hemostasis | Interventional radiology embolization |
| Failed EGD + IR | Surgery (rare with modern endoscopic/IR options) |

### Forrest Classification & Rebleed Risk

Rebleed rates depend heavily on whether hemostasis was successfully achieved. Older literature quoted untreated rates; modern management uses *post-hemostasis* rates. Both are shown below for context.

| Class | Lesion | Untreated Rebleed | Post-Hemostasis Rebleed (Jensen 2017) | Management |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ia** | Active spurting | ~55% (up to ~59% at 30 d per de Groot 2014) | ~22.5% (highest single category) | Endoscopic Rx + PPI, ICU |
| **Ib** | Active oozing | Historically grouped with Ia (combined ~22–55%; older pooled 81% for Forrest I) | ~4.9% (questionably "high-risk") | Endoscopic Rx + PPI |
| **IIa** | Non-bleeding visible vessel | ~43% (older untreated estimates as high as 50%) | ~11.3% | Endoscopic Rx + PPI |
| **IIb** | Adherent clot | ~22% | ~17.6% | Consider dislodgement + Rx |
| **IIc** | Flat pigmented spot | ~7–10% | Low | PO PPI, ward/home |
| **III** | Clean ulcer base | ~3–5% | Very low | PO PPI, early discharge |

> [!tip] The commonly cited "90% rebleed for Ia" and "50% for IIa" figures come from heterogeneous older data and conflate treated and untreated cohorts. Jensen 2017 post-hemostasis data and the de Groot 2014 reassessment give a more accurate modern picture. Forrest Ia remains the highest-risk lesion; Forrest Ib post-hemostasis behaves more like an intermediate-risk lesion.

### PPI Strategy Post-EGD

- High-risk lesions (Forrest Ia–IIb): continuous PPI infusion × 72 hr (or equivalent intermittent dosing — evidence shows equivalence; SACHAR 2014 meta-analysis).
- Transition to PO PPI for 4–8 weeks.

### *H. pylori*

- Test and treat after any PUD-related bleed.
- Acute test sensitivity is reduced in active bleeding — if negative, retest after 4–6 weeks.

## 10. Variceal Hemorrhage

> [!warning] **Variceal hemorrhage has a 30-day mortality of 15–20%.** This is the highest-mortality form of GI bleeding. The goal is **low portal venous pressure** — resist over-resuscitation.

### Immediate Pharmacotherapy

1. **Octreotide** 50 mcg IV bolus → 50 mcg/hr infusion × 2–5 days
2. **PPI** (pantoprazole 40 mg IV q12hr)
3. **Ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily — up to 5 days** (AASLD 2024 portal hypertension guidance). Discontinue once bleeding is controlled and no active infection is identified. Older AASLD 2021 ascites guidance allowed up to 7 days.
   - Recent meta-analysis (Prosty 2025) shows *no* mortality benefit; still recommended by current guidelines, but be willing to stop at 48–72 hr if no infection identified.
4. **Lactulose** (± rifaximin) early if AMS or high risk — UGIB is a major precipitant of hepatic encephalopathy.

### Transfusion & Fluid Strategy

- **Hb target: 7 g/dL strict** — do *not* over-transfuse.
- Avoid large-volume crystalloid — raises portal pressure and worsens bleeding.
- Permissive SBP 80–90 mm Hg is often acceptable in chronic cirrhotics.
- **Avoid MTP reflex** unless exsanguinating; focus on source control.

### Coagulopathy in Cirrhosis

- Check **fibrinogen** (low → cryoprecipitate to >150 mg/dL).
- **Do NOT give FFP** to "correct" INR — cirrhosis INR does not reflect true coagulopathy; FFP raises portal pressure.
- TEG/ROTEM preferred over INR for guided resuscitation.
- Platelet target >50 × 10⁹/L.

### Procedural Options

| Varix Type | First Line | Second Line |
|---|---|---|
| **Esophageal** | Endoscopic band ligation | **TIPS** — early TIPS <72 hr for **CTP class B with score >7 (i.e., 8–9) and active bleeding on endoscopy** *or* **CTP class C with score 10–13** (AASLD 2024) |
| **Gastric** | Glue (cyanoacrylate) injection | **TIPS** or **BRTO** (if gastrorenal shunt) |
| **Refractory** | Blakemore/Minnesota tube as bridge | TIPS emergent |

### TIPS vs. BRTO Complication Profile

| | TIPS | BRTO |
|---|---|---|
| Hepatic encephalopathy | **Worsens** | Does not worsen |
| Ascites | Improves | May worsen |
| Esophageal varices | Improves | May worsen |
| Availability | Widely available | Technically challenging, limited centers |

### Blakemore / Minnesota Tube (bridge only)

1. Insert via mouth to 50 cm (patient should be intubated).
2. Confirm gastric position (POCUS or aspiration).
3. Inflate **gastric** balloon with 50 mL air → confirm placement (CXR or POCUS).
4. Inflate gastric balloon to total **250 mL** air.
5. Apply traction (pulley + saline bag, or ETT securement).
6. Gastric balloon alone is often sufficient.
7. If persistent bleeding → inflate **esophageal** balloon to 30 mmHg; reduce as soon as possible to prevent esophageal necrosis.
8. Wean after ~12 hr of stability.

> [!warning] Blakemore is a **high-risk rescue**. Major complications: esophageal rupture, aspiration, airway obstruction, necrosis. Use only as bridge to definitive therapy (TIPS).

### Anticipate Hepatic Encephalopathy

- Swallowed blood = massive nitrogen load → HE.
- Start **lactulose** titrated to 3 soft stools/day.
- Add **rifaximin 550 mg BID** if overt HE or recurrent.

## 11. Lower GI Bleed / Severe Hematochezia

### General Approach

1. **Resuscitate** as above.
2. **Assess for rapid transit UGIB** — ~10–15% of hematochezia is massive UGIB. Features favoring upper source: hemodynamic instability, melena component, coffee grounds, BUN:Cr >30, known PUD.
3. **NG lavage**: bloody → EGD; bilious without blood → likely lower source (but does not exclude UGIB).
4. **CT angiography** if actively bleeding — guides IR and excludes aortoenteric fistula, ischemic colitis, etc.
5. **Colonoscopy** within 24 hr if stable and bleeding has stopped (urgent colonoscopy in active bleed rarely useful — buried in blood and stool).
6. **IR embolization** for active extravasation on CTA — technical success ~85–95% (series-dependent); complications (bowel ischemia, ulcer formation) rare.
7. **Surgery** reserved for refractory bleeding with well-localized source.

### CT Angiography Notes

- *Must* be protocolled as CTA, **not** a contrast CT abdomen/pelvis — three timed acquisitions.
- Detects extravasation at rates ≥0.3 mL/min.
- Must be obtained **while actively bleeding** — lower GI bleeds stop and start unpredictably.

### Provocative Angiography

- Used for recurrent/obscure bleeding when standard workup unrevealing.
- **Heparin** (preferred over thrombolytics — more reversible) to provoke bleeding, followed by CTA and intervention.
- Requires multidisciplinary (GI + IR) planning.

### Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Consulting GI and waiting** for lower GI bleed — GI rarely intervenes urgently for hematochezia. If NG lavage is negative and the patient is bleeding actively, **order CTA without delay**.
- **Ordering a tagged RBC scan** instead of CTA — less specific, doesn't guide intervention.
- **Over-transfusion** in stable profound anemia — causes TACO.
- **Empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics** in lower GI bleed without infection signs.

## 12. Pharmacology Deep-Dive

### Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs)

| Drug | SUP Dose | GI Bleed Dose |
|---|---|---|
| Pantoprazole | 40 mg IV/PO daily | 80 mg IV bolus → 8 mg/hr *or* 40 mg IV q12hr |
| Esomeprazole | 40 mg IV/PO daily | 40 mg IV q12hr |
| Omeprazole | 40 mg IV/PO daily | 40 mg IV q12hr |
| Lansoprazole | 30 mg PO daily | — |

**Mechanism**: Irreversible inhibition of gastric H⁺/K⁺ ATPase → 80–95% reduction in gastric acidity; duration of action 24–48 hr despite plasma t½ of 0.5–3 hr (irreversible pump inactivation).

**Key drug interactions**: reduced absorption of azoles, atazanavir/protease inhibitors, TKIs (erlotinib), mycophenolate; **increases** digoxin absorption. Omeprazole inhibits CYP2C19 (affects clopidogrel activation — clinically modest). Pantoprazole has the lowest CYP interaction profile.

**Safety**: extensive RCT data (SUP-ICU, REVISE, PEPTIC) show PPIs do NOT cause C. difficile, pneumonia, or excess mortality in critically ill patients. No renal dose adjustment.

**PPI infusion vs. intermittent**: Sachar 2014 meta-analysis demonstrated *equivalence* — intermittent high-dose PPI is perfectly acceptable post-endoscopy.

### H2 Receptor Antagonists (Famotidine)

| Renal Function | Famotidine Dose (SUP) |
|---|---|
| GFR >50 | 20 mg IV/PO q12hr |
| GFR 30–50 | 20 mg IV/PO q24hr |
| GFR <30 or HD | 10 mg IV/PO q24hr (not removed by HD — may accumulate) |

**Mechanism**: reversible competitive antagonism of H2 receptors on parietal cells → decreased acid secretion.

**Key caveats**: Tachyphylaxis over days; **on Beers list** for elderly (delirium risk); renal adjustment required. **2nd line** to PPIs for SUP.

### Octreotide

- **Variceal bleed**: 50 mcg IV bolus → 50 mcg/hr IV × 2–5 days.
- **Sulfonylurea hypoglycemia**: 100 mcg IV × 1, then 50 mcg SC q6hr.
- **No renal adjustment.**

**Mechanism**: Octapeptide somatostatin analog → reduces splanchnic blood flow and portal venous pressure.

**Adverse effects**: gallstones/biliary sludge, bradycardia/AV block, QT prolongation, dysglycemia (inhibits insulin AND glucagon), pancreatic exocrine insufficiency.

**Contraindications**: severe bradycardia/AV block, brittle diabetes, recurrent gallstones.

### Sucralfate

- **Dose**: 1 g PO QID on empty stomach.
- **Mechanism**: Forms viscous ulcer-adherent complex; inhibits pepsin locally.
- **Contraindications**: GFR <10 mL/min (aluminum accumulation).
- **Key caveat**: Reduces absorption of *many* oral medications — separate dosing; little role when PPI already prescribed.

### Tranexamic Acid (TXA)

- **Not recommended for GI bleeding.** The HALT-IT trial (2020; n=12,009) showed no mortality benefit and increased risk of VTE and seizures. Guidelines do not recommend TXA for acute GI hemorrhage.

### Erythromycin (prokinetic for EGD)

- **Dose**: 250 mg IV over 5–30 min, ideally 20–90 min pre-EGD.
- **Benefit**: Reduces need for repeat endoscopy and shortens LOS (2021 ACG guidelines).
- **Contraindicated**: QTc prolongation.
- **Alternative**: Metoclopramide 10 mg IV.

## 13. Stress Ulcer Prophylaxis (SUP)

Based on **2024 SCCM/ASHP guidelines**.

### Indication (both required)

1. **Truly critically ill** (not merely boarding in ICU; mechanical ventilation alone is NOT sufficient).
2. **PLUS any one** of:
   - Shock
   - Coagulopathy (platelets <50, INR >1.5, PTT >2× control)
   - Chronic liver disease
   - Neurocritical care patient (particularly elevated ICP)

### Preferred Agent

- **2024 SCCM/ASHP guideline (Recommendation 8)** suggests using **either a PPI *or* an H2RA as first-line** — the two classes are treated as equivalent first-line options.
- Network meta-analysis: PPIs reduce clinically important upper GI bleeding more than H2RAs (RR 0.53), but are associated with a *possible* small increase in mortality (RR 1.05, 95% CI 1.00–1.10) — hence the guideline's neutrality.
- A 2026 retrospective septic-shock cohort (n=15,102) found PPIs associated with lower UGIB (OR 0.78) without mortality difference, but this is observational only.
- Dosing:
   - Pantoprazole / esomeprazole / omeprazole **40 mg IV or PO daily** (lansoprazole 30 mg PO daily)
   - Famotidine **20 mg IV/PO q12hr** (renal-adjusted — see §12)
- Practical considerations that may still favor a PPI at the bedside:
   - No renal adjustment required
   - No tachyphylaxis
   - Famotidine is on the **Beers list** in the elderly (delirium, confusion) — this is an independent concern, not a guideline-cited reason for PPI preference.

### Documentation & Cessation

- Document indication in the chart (e.g., *"PPI for SUP — shock"*).
- **Discontinue** when indication resolves — avoid inappropriate outpatient continuation.

### Early Enteral Nutrition

- Recommended to reduce clinically significant stress ulceration (and for many other reasons).

## 14. Clinical Pearls

> [!tip] **High-Yield Pearls**
>
> 1. **BUN:Cr >30 in a bleeding patient** = upper GI source until proven otherwise. Digested blood is a protein load absorbed in small bowel.
> 2. **Normal hemoglobin + shock** = brisk acute bleed; Hb hasn't had time to fall. **Profound anemia + stable vitals** = subacute bleed — *do not over-transfuse*.
> 3. In **variceal bleeding**, restraint is a therapy. Transfuse to 7 g/dL strict. Every extra unit of blood and every liter of crystalloid increases portal pressure and perpetuates bleeding.
> 4. **Do NOT give FFP to "correct" INR in cirrhosis.** The INR doesn't reflect true hemostasis in liver disease; FFP raises portal pressure without improving bleeding.
> 5. **AAA repair + any GI bleed = CT angiography FIRST.** Aortoenteric fistula kills quickly and is missed on EGD.
> 6. **Order erythromycin early** (~250 mg IV) before EGD — it takes pharmacy time to mix, and a clear stomach dramatically improves endoscopic yield.
> 7. **CT angiography is a *protocol*, not a CT with contrast.** Make sure IR/radiology knows what you're asking for.
> 8. **Tagged RBC scan is rarely useful** — it's sensitive but non-specific, and doesn't give IR a map.
> 9. **Ceftriaxone in cirrhotic UGIB**: current guidelines still recommend it, but recent evidence (Prosty 2025) questions a mortality benefit. Give it, but **stop at 48–72 hr** if no infection.
> 10. **Intubation for GI bleed is high-risk.** Pre-load with vasopressor, decompress the stomach, and use erythromycin/metoclopramide to reduce aspiration.
> 11. **Restrictive transfusion (Hb >7) is standard** — the Villanueva trial (NEJM 2013) showed lower mortality vs liberal strategy in acute UGIB.
> 12. **Early TIPS (<72 hr)** in high-risk variceal bleeders — **CTP class B score >7 (8–9) with active bleeding on endoscopy** or **CTP class C 10–13** (AASLD 2024) — reduces rebleeding and mortality (García-Pagán 2010).

## 15. Evidence & Landmark Trials

### Transfusion Strategy

**Villanueva 2013 — NEJM Restrictive Transfusion in UGIB**
- 921 patients with acute UGIB; Hb <7 vs <9 transfusion thresholds.
- 6-week mortality: 5% restrictive vs 9% liberal (HR 0.55, p=0.02).
- Rebleeding and complications also lower in the restrictive arm.
- **Established Hb >7 g/dL as standard target.**

### PPI Dosing & Strategy

**Sachar 2014 — JAMA IM Intermittent vs Continuous PPI**
- Meta-analysis of 13 RCTs, 1691 patients with high-risk bleeding ulcers post-endoscopy.
- Intermittent PPI non-inferior to continuous infusion for rebleeding, need for surgery, and mortality.
- **Established intermittent PPI as acceptable alternative to infusion.**

### Stress Ulcer Prophylaxis

**SUP-ICU 2018 — NEJM Pantoprazole in ICU**
- 3298 adult ICU patients; pantoprazole 40 mg IV daily vs placebo.
- No difference in mortality or clinically important events; reduced GI bleeding.
- **Supports PPI safety but questions routine use.**

**PEPTIC 2020 — JAMA PPI vs H2RA for SUP**
- Cluster-randomized crossover trial, 26,828 ventilated ICU patients.
- PPI associated with lower GI bleeding rate vs H2RA (1.3% vs 1.8%); hospital mortality similar (signal toward slightly higher 90-day mortality with PPI did not reach significance).
- Informs — but does not definitively establish — PPI preference; the 2024 SCCM/ASHP guideline ultimately classed PPIs and H2RAs as equivalent first-line options.

**REVISE 2024 — NEJM Pantoprazole in Mechanically Ventilated Adults**
- 4821 ventilated ICU patients; pantoprazole vs placebo.
- Reduced clinically important upper GI bleeding (1.0% vs 3.5%); no increase in C. diff or pneumonia.
- **Reaffirmed PPI safety and effectiveness.**

### Tranexamic Acid

**HALT-IT 2020 — Lancet TXA in Acute GI Bleed**
- 12,009 patients with significant upper or lower GI bleed; TXA vs placebo.
- No reduction in death due to bleeding (4% vs 4%); increased VTE and seizures.
- **TXA NOT recommended for GI hemorrhage.**

### Variceal Bleed

**García-Pagán 2010 — NEJM Early TIPS in Variceal Bleeding**
- 63 patients with high-risk variceal bleeding (Child B active bleed or Child C).
- Early TIPS (<72 hr) vs standard therapy: 1-year survival 86% vs 61% (p<0.001).
- **Established early TIPS for high-risk variceal bleeding.**

**Baveno VII 2022 Consensus** — current variceal guidelines; risk stratification, pre-emptive TIPS, antibiotics, NSBB as secondary prophylaxis.

### Upper GI Bleed Guidelines

**ACG 2021 — Laine et al., Am J Gastroenterol** (PMID 33929377)
- Recommends restrictive transfusion (Hb >7), early EGD (within 24 hr), pre-endoscopy erythromycin, intermittent high-dose PPI for high-risk lesions.

### Current Evidence (Recent Publications)

- **Prosty 2025** — Meta-analysis of antibiotics in cirrhotic UGIB showing *no mortality benefit* and no reduction in rebleeding; trends toward less benefit in modern era. Challenges reflexive ceftriaxone use.
- **Long & Gottlieb 2024, *Am J Emerg Med*** — EM update on UGIB; emphasizes restrictive transfusion, early EGD, and restrained use of MTP.
- **Martyak, Soult, Britt 2025, *J Trauma Acute Care Surg*** — comprehensive update on diagnosis and management of GI hemorrhage; expanded role of CT angiography.
- **Orpen-Palmer & Stanley 2022, *BMJ Med*** — UGIB management update; pragmatic indications for intubation.
- **Tokar & Higa 2022, *Ann Intern Med* (In the Clinic)** — practical clinical overview of acute GI bleeding.
