## 1. Overview
-  **Definition:** Acute cholangitis is a clinical syndrome characterized by fever, jaundice, and abdominal pain that develops as a result of stasis and infection in the biliary tract.
-  **Epidemiology:** Those without bile duct stents: Biliary calculi (28 to 70 percent), benign biliary stricture (5 to 28 percent), and malignancy (10 to 57 percent).
-  **Pathophysiology:** Primarily caused by bacterial infection with biliary obstruction. Organisms typically ascend from the duodenum; hematogenous spread from the portal vein is a rare source of infection.
	- Mechanisms that usually prevent bacteria from entering the biliary tract are interrupted. These mechanisms include the sphincter of Oddi (a circular muscle located at the end of the common bile duct and pancreatic duct, where they join to form the ampulla of Vater that leads into the duodenum), which acts as an effective mechanical barrior to duodenal reflux and ascending bacterial infection.
	- Usually mixed growth of gram negative and gram positive. Common bacteria include _E. coli_ is the major gram-negative bacterium isolated (25 to 50%), followed by _Klebsiella_ (15 to 20%) and _Enterobacter_ species (5 to 10%). The most common gram-positive bacteria are _Enterococcus_ species (10 to 20%). Anaerobes, such as _Bacteroides_ and Clostridia, are usually present as part of a mixed infection, but their frequency is underestimated by standard culture techniques

## 2. Clinical Presentation
-  **Symptoms:** Classic presentation of acute cholangitis is fever, abdominal pain, and jaundice (Charcot's triad), although only 50 to 75% of patients with acute cholangitis have all three findings. Jaundice is seen in 60-70% of patients
- Acute cholangitis should be suspected in patients with fever, abd pain, and jaundice

## 3. Risk Factors
| Risk Factor              | Description                                                      |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Biliary calculi          | Common cause of obstruction                                      |
| Benign biliary stricture | Congenital, post-infectious, or inflammatory                     |
| Malignancy               | Tumors in gallbladder, bile duct, ampulla, duodenum, or pancreas |
| Post-ERCP                | Particularly after therapeutic procedures                        |
| Postoperative            | Bile duct injury or strictured biliary-enteric anastomosis       |

## 4. Differential Diagnosis
- Acute Cholecystitis
- Biliary Leak
- Acute pancreatitis
- Liver Abscess
- Pylonephritis
- PID

## 6. Diagnostic Criteria/Scoring Systems
- [MDCalc Tokyo Guidelines Calculator](https://www.mdcalc.com/calc/10142/tokyo-guidelines-acute-cholangitis-2018#evidence)
- Diagnosis of acute cholangitis diagnosis made if the patient has evidence of systemic inflammation with one of the following
	- Fever/chills
	- Lab evidence of inflammation (ERCP/WBC/NLR)
	  **AND BOTH** of the following:
	- **Evidence of cholestatis**: Bilirubin ≥2 mg/dL or abnormal liver chemistries (elevated alkaline phosphatase, gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase, alanine aminotransferase, or aspartate aminotransferase, to >1.5 times the upper limit of normal).
	- **Imaging with biliary dilation** or evidence of the underlying etiology (eg, a stricture, stone, or stent).
## 7. Severity/Risk Stratification
**Severe (suppurative) cholangitis** — Acute cholangitis is considered severe if it is associated with the onset of dysfunction in at least any one of the following organs/systems:
- Cardiovascular dysfunction – Hypotension requiring dopamine ≥5 micrograms/kg per min, or any dose of norepinephrine
- Neurological dysfunction – Disturbance of consciousness
- Respiratory dysfunction – PaO2/FiO2 ratio <300
- Renal dysfunction – Oliguria, serum creatinine >2.0 mg/dl
- Hepatic dysfunction – Prothrombin time-international normalized ratio >1.5
- Hematological dysfunction – Platelet count <100,000/mm

**Moderate acute cholangitis** — Acute cholangitis is defined as moderate if it is associated with any two of the following:
- Abnormal WBC count (>12,000/mm3, <4,000/mm3)
- Fever 39°C (102.2°F)
- Age (≥75 years)
- Hyperbilirubinemia (total bilirubin ≥5 mg/dl)
- Hypoalbuminemia

**Mild acute cholangitis** — Mild acute cholangitis does not meet the criteria for moderate or severe cholangitis at initial diagnosis.

## 8. Management
**Initial Management**
• Hospital admission, IV hydration, electrolyte correction, pain control.

**Medical Management**
- **Antibiotics:** Empiric regimens targeting enteric streptococci, coliforms, and anaerobes. Tailor based on culture results.
	- Typically Zosyn in septic patients requiring ICU admission as front line therapy. If high risk ESBL can consider meropenem
	- 70-80% of patients respond with abx therapy. However, biliary drainage in patients with mild to moderate should be performed in 24-48hrs
- Those with mild to moderate who fail conservative therapy or those with severe required urgent (24 hours) biliary decompression
- Refer to cholecystitis antibiotic guide for regimen selection

**Procedural/Surgical Management**
- **Endoscopic sphincterotomy:** Stone extraction and/or stent insertion.  ERCP or EUS-guided biliary drainage. ERCP first line.
	- Successful in 90 to 95%. Associated with significantly lower overall rates of mortality and morbidity compared with surgical decompression (mortality rates of 4.7 to 10 percent versus 10 to 50 percent)
	- Alternative to ERCP would be the EUS in patients at high risk for complications with ERCP or where ERCP has failed due to altered surgical anatomy or obstructing duodenal or ampullary tumors.
- **Percutaneous drainage:** If endoscopic methods fail.
	- Is more invasive than ERCP due to transhepatically injecting contrast into the biliary system
- **Surgical drainage:** Reserved for cases where other methods fail.
	- Rare in contemporary practice that acute cholangitis requires surgical intervention
	- May be warranted for gangrene/necrosis or perforation

**Special Populations**
• **Pregnancy:** Antibiotic choices should consider fetal toxicity; use fetal shielding during fluoroscopy.

## 9. Complications
-  Hepatic abscess, sepsis, MODS, shock

## 10. Prognosis & Follow-Up
• **Prognosis:** Mortality rates have decreased with advances in treatment; severe cases still have high mortality.
• **Follow-up recommendations:** Elective cholecystectomy after resolution to prevent recurrence.
