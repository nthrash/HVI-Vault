# Interpreting TEG and ROTEM: Viscoelastic Hemostatic Assays in Clinical Practice

## Overview

Thromboelastography (TEG) and rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM) are **point-of-care viscoelastic hemostatic assays (VHAs)** that measure the mechanical properties of a whole-blood clot as it forms, strengthens, and lyses in real time. Unlike conventional coagulation tests (PT, aPTT, fibrinogen, platelet count), which measure isolated steps of the clotting cascade in plasma, VHAs evaluate the entire hemostatic process — from initial thrombin generation through clot propagation to fibrinolysis — in a single dynamic readout. The result is a graphical trace whose shape encodes the functional adequacy of clot formation.

> [!tip] **Why This Matters**
> In hemorrhagic emergencies — trauma, obstetric hemorrhage, cardiac surgery, liver transplantation, and massive GI bleeding — waiting for conventional labs delays targeted therapy. VHAs produce actionable results in 15–30 minutes and allow clinicians to identify *which component* of hemostasis is failing, enabling goal-directed blood product administration rather than empiric "cookbook" transfusion.

---

## The Physics: How It Works

Both TEG and ROTEM exploit the same principle: a small whole-blood sample (~0.36 mL) is placed in a cup, and a pin is suspended in the sample. As a clot forms, its mechanical strength transmits torsional force between the pin and the cup. The resulting movement is transduced into an electrical signal and plotted over time, generating the characteristic spindle-shaped waveform.

- **TEG (Haemonetics)**: The *cup* oscillates back and forth 4°45' while the pin remains stationary. Clot formation couples cup motion to pin motion.
- **ROTEM (Instrumentation Laboratory / Werfen)**: The *pin* rotates while the cup is stationary. Clot formation resists pin rotation.

The graphical output looks the same — a symmetric spindle — and the parameters measured are conceptually equivalent, though the names differ between systems.

## Parameters: TEG vs. ROTEM Side-by-Side

Understanding both nomenclatures is essential, as institutions may use either system. The table below maps equivalent parameters.

| **What Is Measured** | **TEG Parameter** | **TEG Normal** | **ROTEM Parameter** | **ROTEM Normal** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time to initial clot formation (2mm amplitude) | **R time** (Reaction time) | 5–10 min | **CT** (Clotting Time) | 100–240 sec (EXTEM) |
| Time from initial clot to defined clot strength | **K time** (Kinetics) | 1–3 min | **CFT** (Clot Formation Time) | 30–110 sec (EXTEM) |
| Rate of clot development (angle of tangent) | **Alpha (α) angle** | 53–72° | **Alpha (α) angle** | 63–83° (EXTEM) |
| Maximum clot strength | **MA** (Maximum Amplitude) | 50–70 mm | **MCF** (Maximum Clot Firmness) | 50–72 mm (EXTEM) |
| Clot lysis at 30 min post-MA | **LY30** | 0–8% | **LI30** (Lysis Index at 30 min) | >85% (i.e., <15% lysis) |
| Clot lysis at 60 min | **LY60** | 0–15% | **LI60** | >85% |
| Estimated percent lysis | — | — | **ML** (Maximum Lysis) | <15% |
| Coagulation Index (composite) | **CI** | -3 to +3 | — | — |

> [!warning] **Normal ranges vary by reagent/assay channel.** ROTEM has four primary channels (EXTEM, INTEM, FIBTEM, APTEM) each with different reference ranges. Always reference your institution's specific reagent normals.

## ROTEM Assay Channels Explained

ROTEM uses different activators in each channel to interrogate specific aspects of hemostasis:

| **Channel** | **Activator** | **Pathway Tested** | **Key Use** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **EXTEM** | Tissue factor (TF) | Extrinsic (TF) pathway + platelets + fibrinogen | Overall clot quality; first-line screen |
| **INTEM** | Ellagic acid (contact) | Intrinsic pathway + platelets + fibrinogen | Detects intrinsic pathway defects, heparin effect |
| **FIBTEM** | TF + cytochalasin D (platelet inhibitor) | Fibrinogen contribution only | Isolates fibrinogen from platelet contribution to clot strength |
| **APTEM** | TF + aprotinin (fibrinolysis inhibitor) | Extrinsic pathway, fibrinolysis blocked | Confirms fibrinolysis when EXTEM shows lysis |
| **HEPTEM** | Ellagic acid + heparinase | Intrinsic pathway with heparin neutralized | Isolates heparin effect from true factor deficiency |

**How to use EXTEM + FIBTEM together:** The difference between EXTEM MCF and FIBTEM MCF reflects the **platelet contribution** to clot strength. A low EXTEM MCF with a normal FIBTEM MCF indicates platelet dysfunction or thrombocytopenia. A low FIBTEM MCF indicates hypofibrinogenemia regardless of platelet count.

---

## TEG Assay Channels

TEG uses a similar multi-channel approach:

| **Channel** | **Activator** | **Use** |
|---|---|---|
| **Kaolin TEG (CK)** | Kaolin (contact activator) | Standard whole-blood clotting assessment |
| **Kaolin + heparinase (CKH)** | Kaolin + heparinase | Neutralizes heparin; compare to CK to assess heparin effect |
| **Rapid TEG (rTEG)** | Tissue factor + kaolin | Faster results (~15 min); used in trauma |
| **Functional Fibrinogen (FF)** | Tissue factor + platelet inhibitor (abciximab) | Fibrinogen contribution only (analogous to FIBTEM) |

## Reading the Trace: Parameter-by-Parameter

### R Time / CT — "Time to First Clot"

This reflects the **initiation phase** of coagulation: thrombin generation. It is predominantly influenced by clotting factor activity.

- **Prolonged R / CT**: Factor deficiency (II, V, VII, X for extrinsic; VIII, IX, XI for intrinsic), anticoagulant effect (heparin, warfarin, DOACs), or severe hypothermia/acidosis impairing enzyme function.
- **Shortened R / CT**: Hypercoagulable state, factor VIII elevation (acute phase), thrombosis risk.

> [!tip] **Heparin effect pearl**: If INTEM CT is prolonged but HEPTEM CT is normal, the culprit is heparin. EXTEM is largely unaffected by unfractionated heparin at therapeutic doses.

### K Time / CFT — "Speed of Clot Development"

This reflects the **amplification phase**: fibrin cross-linking and early platelet interaction. It is influenced by fibrinogen level and platelet function.

- **Prolonged K / CFT**: Hypofibrinogenemia, platelet dysfunction, antiplatelet drugs, heparin.
- **Shortened K / CFT**: Hypercoagulable state, elevated fibrinogen.

### Alpha Angle — "Rate of Clot Propagation"

The angle of the tangent drawn from the point of initial clot formation to the steepest portion of the trace. It is closely related to K time — both reflect **fibrinogen** and **platelet** activity.

- **Decreased alpha angle**: Hypofibrinogenemia, thrombocytopenia/dysfunction.
- **Increased alpha angle**: Hypercoagulability.

### MA / MCF — "Maximum Clot Strength"

This is the **most clinically actionable parameter**. It reflects the absolute maximum clot strength, driven approximately:
- **80% by platelets** (platelet-fibrin interaction via GPIIb/IIIa)
- **20% by fibrinogen** cross-linking

Comparing EXTEM MCF to FIBTEM MCF isolates these contributions:

```
Platelet contribution = EXTEM MCF − FIBTEM MCF
Fibrinogen contribution = FIBTEM MCF
```

- **Low MA / MCF**: Thrombocytopenia, platelet dysfunction (GPIIb/IIIa inhibitors), hypofibrinogenemia, factor XIII deficiency.
- **High MA / MCF**: Hypercoagulable state, elevated fibrinogen (acute phase reactant), heparin-induced thrombocytopenia with thrombosis (HITT).

### LY30 / LI30 — "Fibrinolysis Assessment"

Measured as the percentage reduction in clot amplitude 30 minutes after MA is reached.

- **TEG LY30 > 8%** or **ROTEM LI30 < 85%**: Suggests **clinically significant fibrinolysis**.
- **LY30 > 3%** is the treatment threshold used in many trauma protocols (some use >3%, others >8% — know your institution's cutoff).
- **Hyperfibrinolysis (LY30 > 30%)**: Life-threatening fibrinolytic crisis; requires immediate antifibrinolytic therapy (TXA or aminocaproic acid).

> [!warning] **APTEM confirmation**: If EXTEM shows lysis (reduced LI30/ML), repeat with APTEM channel. If lysis disappears in APTEM, fibrinolysis is confirmed. If both show lysis, consider other causes (artifact, retraction).

## Recognizing Pattern Signatures

The real power of VHA is pattern recognition. Knowing the gestalt of a trace is faster than computing each parameter individually.

### Pattern 1: Normal Trace
- R/CT normal, alpha normal, MA/MCF normal, LY30/LI30 normal
- Symmetric spindle shape, maximal amplitude reached by 30–40 min

### Pattern 2: Factor Deficiency (Prolonged R/CT, otherwise normal or slightly narrow)
- Prolonged R (TEG) or CT (ROTEM)
- Alpha angle and MA may be mildly reduced if severe
- **Think**: Factor deficiency, anticoagulant effect (heparin, warfarin, DOAC), hypothermia, acidosis
- **Treat**: FFP, factor concentrates (PCC for warfarin/factor deficiency), or address heparin effect

### Pattern 3: Fibrinogen Deficiency (Normal R/CT, prolonged K/CFT, reduced alpha, reduced MA — but FIBTEM MCF low)
- Normal initiation but impaired clot propagation
- FIBTEM MCF characteristically < 8–10 mm in severe hypofibrinogenemia
- **Think**: Dilutional coagulopathy, DIC consumption, liver disease, post-major hemorrhage
- **Treat**: Cryoprecipitate (10 units raises fibrinogen ~50–75 mg/dL) or fibrinogen concentrate (4g)

### Pattern 4: Platelet Deficiency/Dysfunction (Low MA/MCF with normal FIBTEM MCF)
- EXTEM MCF low, FIBTEM MCF normal → platelet problem
- **Think**: Thrombocytopenia, GPIIb/IIIa inhibitors (aspirin, clopidogrel, tirofiban), uremia, hypothermia
- **Treat**: Platelet transfusion, DDAVP (for uremic/aspirin dysfunction), consider cessation of antiplatelet agents

### Pattern 5: Fibrinolysis (Normal initial trace, then clot lyses — "washout" appearance)
- MA/MCF reached normally, then amplitude decreases progressively
- LY30 > 3–8% (TEG) or LI30 < 85% (ROTEM)
- Hyperfibrinolysis: rapid clot dissolution, near-complete lysis within 30 min
- **Think**: Trauma-induced coagulopathy (TIC), post-thrombolytics, liver disease, DIC fibrinolytic phase, prostate surgery
- **Treat**: Tranexamic acid (TXA) 1g IV over 10 min + 1g over 8h (trauma), epsilon-aminocaproic acid

### Pattern 6: Hypercoagulable Trace ("Cigar-shaped" — narrow, tall, fast)
- Shortened R/CT, steep alpha, increased MA/MCF
- **Think**: Post-op state, acute phase response, VTE risk, post-splenectomy, antiphospholipid syndrome
- **Clinical action**: Does not necessarily require anticoagulation; correlate with clinical context and VTE risk

### Pattern 7: Global Coagulopathy / DIC (All parameters abnormal)
- Prolonged R/CT, low alpha, low MA, significant lysis
- The "flat" or nearly flat trace in fulminant DIC represents near-complete failure of hemostasis
- **Think**: Sepsis-associated DIC, amniotic fluid embolism, acute hemolytic transfusion reaction, massive hemorrhage
- **Treat**: Address underlying cause; goal-directed FFP, cryoprecipitate, platelets, TXA if fibrinolysis present

## Clinical Applications

### 1. Trauma and Massive Hemorrhage
VHAs have become foundational in trauma resuscitation, allowing early identification of:
- **Fibrinogen depletion** (first coagulation factor to reach critical levels in hemorrhage)
- **Platelet dysfunction** (independent of count — traumatic brain injury causes platelet dysfunction even with normal counts)
- **Hyperfibrinolysis** (a major driver of early trauma death; TXA is most effective within 3 hours of injury)
- **Factor deficiency** (particularly in patients on anticoagulants)

### 2. Cardiac Surgery (Post-CPB)
Cardiopulmonary bypass causes predictable coagulopathy through hemodilution, platelet dysfunction, residual heparin, hypothermia, and fibrinolysis. VHA-guided transfusion after CPB reduces blood product use and re-exploration rates versus algorithm-based or empiric transfusion.

### 3. Liver Disease / Liver Transplantation
Conventional tests (PT/INR) are notoriously unreliable in liver disease — the INR was not designed to assess bleeding risk in cirrhosis, as it does not account for concurrent decreases in anticoagulant proteins (protein C, S, antithrombin). VHA better reflects the rebalanced hemostasis of cirrhosis and predicts procedural bleeding risk more accurately.

### 4. Obstetric Hemorrhage
Postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) is a leading cause of maternal mortality. Fibrinogen drops early and is the key clotting factor to replace. FIBTEM MCF < 12 mm in the context of PPH predicts the need for massive transfusion and guides early cryoprecipitate/fibrinogen concentrate use.

### 5. Anticoagulant Effect and Reversal
- **Heparin**: INTEM CT prolonged; HEPTEM CT normal → confirm heparin effect → protamine
- **Warfarin**: EXTEM CT prolonged; INTEM CT prolonged → PCC or FFP
- **DOACs**: VHA may detect anticoagulant effect but is not sensitive enough to rule out dabigatran or factor Xa inhibitor levels — use specific assays (anti-Xa, dTT) in addition

## Limitations of VHAs

> [!warning] **Know the blind spots**

- **VHAs do not detect thrombocytopenia per se** — MA reflects platelet *function*, not count alone. A patient with 20k platelets from ITP may have a near-normal MA if functional. Conversely, a uremic patient with 200k dysfunctional platelets may have a low MA.
- **VHAs cannot assess the vascular endothelium** — von Willebrand disease (especially type 1) may not be reliably detected.
- **Temperature**: Tests run at 37°C regardless of patient temperature; hypothermia-induced coagulopathy may be underestimated.
- **DOACs**: Factor Xa inhibitors (rivaroxaban, apixaban) may not reliably prolong CT/R at lower therapeutic levels.
- **Sensitivity for mild disorders**: VHAs are designed for acute, significant derangements — mild congenital bleeding disorders (mild hemophilia A, VWD type 1) may not be detected.
- **Operator variability**: Air bubbles, poor technique, delay in sample processing all affect results.
- **Reference ranges**: Normal values vary by reagent lot, activator, and institution — do not apply one lab's normals to another.

## Clinical Pearls

> [!tip] **Clinical Pearls**
>
> **1. Fibrinogen first** — In acute hemorrhage, fibrinogen is consumed before all other coagulation proteins. A low FIBTEM MCF (< 10 mm) or functional fibrinogen < 1.5–2.0 g/L is an early and sensitive signal to give cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate — often before the PT/aPTT even budges.
>
> **2. The EXTEM–FIBTEM subtraction** — `EXTEM MCF − FIBTEM MCF = platelet contribution`. This is the most clinically elegant calculation in VHA interpretation. If EXTEM MCF is 38mm and FIBTEM MCF is 9mm, the platelet contribution is 29mm (low) — give platelets. If EXTEM MCF is 38mm and FIBTEM MCF is 6mm, fibrinogen is the limiting factor — give cryoprecipitate first, then reassess.
>
> **3. TXA window in trauma** — Hyperfibrinolysis on TEG/ROTEM is treatable, but the evidence strongly favors giving TXA within 3 hours of injury (CRASH-2 trial). After 3 hours, TXA may worsen outcomes by blocking physiologic fibrinolysis and promoting organ microthrombosis. Don't let VHA results delay TXA in early trauma — give empirically, then confirm fibrinolysis on the trace.
>
> **4. The "flat line" trace** — A completely flat or near-flat trace means one of two things: (1) no clot is forming at all (fulminant coagulopathy, catastrophic DIC), or (2) technical error (air bubble, clotted sample, wrong cup). Always verify technique before calling a flat trace pathological.
>
> **5. VHA in liver disease** — The PT/INR in a cirrhotic patient does NOT reliably predict bleeding. Cirrhosis simultaneously decreases pro- and anticoagulant factors, resulting in a rebalanced but fragile hemostasis. VHA — particularly the ROTEM — better reflects this balance. A normal or near-normal MCF in a cirrhotic patient does not mean they can't bleed; it means their clot-forming capacity is adequate at baseline. Platelet dysfunction and portal hypertension-related bleeding are not captured by VHA.
>
> **6. Comparing INTEM and HEPTEM** — If INTEM CT is prolonged but HEPTEM CT normalizes, unfractionated heparin is on board. If both remain prolonged, the patient has an intrinsic pathway factor deficiency (or is on a DOAC). This comparison is the fastest way to differentiate residual heparin from factor deficiency at the bedside after cardiac surgery.
>
> **7. Don't treat the number, treat the patient** — VHA results must always be interpreted in clinical context. A slightly prolonged R time in a patient with no bleeding may need no intervention. The same value in a patient hemorrhaging after trauma demands FFP and factor concentrates. VHA guides therapy — it does not replace clinical judgment.
>
> **8. Hypercoagulable trace ≠ treat** — A narrow, tall TEG trace (short R, steep alpha, high MA) indicates hypercoagulability, but this is common post-operatively, in pregnancy, and in the acute phase response. This finding alone does not mandate anticoagulation unless there is additional thrombotic risk. Document and monitor, but resist reflexive treatment.

## Evidence and Landmark Trials

### Landmark Trials

**CRASH-2 Trial (2010)**
- *Population*: 20,211 trauma patients with or at risk of significant bleeding in 40 countries
- *Intervention*: TXA (1g IV over 10 min, then 1g over 8h) vs. placebo within 8 hours of injury
- *Primary outcome*: All-cause mortality at 4 weeks; TXA reduced mortality (14.5% vs. 16.0%; RR 0.91, p=0.0035), with greatest benefit within 3 hours
- *Clinical impact*: Established TXA as standard of care in trauma hemorrhage; confirmed the critical importance of early antifibrinolytic therapy — now driven by VHA fibrinolysis detection

**PROPPR Trial (2015)**
- *Population*: 680 severely injured trauma patients with hemorrhagic shock at Level I trauma centers
- *Intervention*: 1:1:1 (plasma:platelets:RBC) vs. 1:1:2 transfusion ratio
- *Primary outcome*: 24-hour and 30-day mortality; no difference in 30-day mortality, but 1:1:1 achieved hemostasis more often and had improved 24-hour survival
- *Clinical impact*: Supported balanced resuscitation strategies; VHA-guided protocols complement ratio-based approaches by identifying specific component deficits

**ITACTIC Trial (2021)**
- *Population*: 396 trauma patients at major trauma centers (European multicenter RCT)
- *Intervention*: VHA-guided resuscitation (ROTEM or TEG) vs. standard coagulation test (CCT)-guided resuscitation
- *Primary outcome*: 28-day survival with good neurological outcome; no significant difference in primary endpoint
- *Clinical impact*: Challenged early enthusiasm for VHA in undifferentiated trauma; subgroup analysis suggested benefit in TBI patients; debate continues about optimal patient selection and protocol implementation

**Systematic Review — Wikkelso et al. (Cochrane, 2016)**
- *Population*: 8 RCTs, 757 patients undergoing surgery with transfusion guided by TEG or ROTEM
- *Outcome*: VHA-guided transfusion reduced all-cause mortality (RR 0.52), reduced RBC transfusion, and reduced FFP and platelet use vs. standard care
- *Clinical impact*: Strongest evidence base for VHA in surgical (especially cardiac) settings; widely cited to support perioperative protocols

**FIBRES Trial (2019)**
- *Population*: 735 cardiac surgery patients with post-CPB bleeding in Canada
- *Intervention*: ROTEM-guided fibrinogen concentrate vs. cryoprecipitate for FIBTEM MCF < 22 mm
- *Primary outcome*: Units of allogeneic blood products transfused within 24 hours; fibrinogen concentrate was non-inferior to cryoprecipitate
- *Clinical impact*: Established fibrinogen concentrate as an acceptable alternative to cryoprecipitate in cardiac surgery when ROTEM identifies fibrinogen deficiency

## Key Guidelines

**European Trauma Guidelines (STOP THE BLEED / ESTES, 5th Edition, 2019 — Spahn et al., *Critical Care*)**
- Recommend VHA-based monitoring for all major hemorrhage (1C)
- FIBTEM MCF < 10 mm: give fibrinogen concentrate or cryoprecipitate (target fibrinogen ≥ 1.5–2.0 g/L)
- EXTEM CT > 90s (INTEM CT > 240s): consider FFP or PCCs
- VHA-detected hyperfibrinolysis: TXA

**Society of Thoracic Surgeons / Society of Cardiovascular Anesthesiologists (STS/SCA) Blood Conservation Guidelines (2021 Update)**
- Recommend VHA to guide transfusion in cardiac surgery (Class I)
- Endorse ROTEM FIBTEM to target fibrinogen replacement
- Support use of VHA to reduce unnecessary FFP and platelet transfusion

**AABB (formerly American Association of Blood Banks) — Transfusion Threshold Guidelines**
- Acknowledge VHA as an emerging standard for goal-directed transfusion
- Note that VHA-guided protocols reduce overall blood product utilization in surgical settings

**Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG) — Postpartum Haemorrhage Guideline (2016, updated 2023)**
- Recommend ROTEM/TEG when available in PPH ≥ 1000 mL
- FIBTEM MCF < 12 mm triggers early fibrinogen replacement

## Quick Reference: Therapeutic Targets

| **VHA Finding** | **Component Deficient** | **Treatment** | **Target** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prolonged R/CT (EXTEM) | Clotting factors (extrinsic) | FFP 10–15 mL/kg or PCC 25–50 IU/kg | Normalize CT |
| Prolonged INTEM CT, normal HEPTEM CT | Heparin effect | Protamine 1 mg per 100U UFH | Normalize INTEM |
| Low FIBTEM MCF (< 8–10 mm) | Fibrinogen | Cryoprecipitate 10 units OR fibrinogen concentrate 4g | FIBTEM MCF > 10–12 mm; fibrinogen ≥ 2 g/L |
| Low EXTEM MCF with normal FIBTEM MCF | Platelets | Platelet transfusion 1 apheresis unit | Platelet contribution > 40 mm |
| LY30 > 3–8% / LI30 < 85% | Fibrinolysis | TXA 1g IV over 10 min | Cessation of lysis |
| All parameters deranged | Global coagulopathy / DIC | Treat underlying cause + balanced resuscitation | Clinical hemostasis |
