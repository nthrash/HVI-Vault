## **Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA)**

**Overview**

_S. aureus_ (methicillin-susceptible) is one of the most common and virulent pathogens in the ICU. It produces numerous toxins and enzymes that facilitate tissue invasion and immune evasion. MSSA is fully susceptible to beta-lactam antibiotics.

**Characteristics**

| Feature                    | Detail                                                                                                                 |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Gram Stain                 | Positive                                                                                                               |
| Metabolism                 | Facultative anaerobe                                                                                                   |
| Arrangement                | Clusters ("bunch of grapes")                                                                                           |
| First-Line Antibiotics     | **Cefazolin (preferred)** or Nafcillin/Oxacillin                                                                       |
| Second-Line Antibiotics    | Vancomycin (if beta-lactam intolerant), Daptomycin                                                                     |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Skin/soft tissue, bloodstream (bacteremia), endocarditis, pneumonia, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, epidural abscess |

**Pathogenicity**
- Produces **coagulase** — key virulence factor distinguishing it from coagulase-negative staph
- **Toxins:** Protein A (immune evasion), alpha-toxin (membrane pore formation), Panton-Valentine Leukocidin (PVL, community-acquired strains — tissue necrosis), TSST-1 (toxic shock syndrome)
- **Biofilm formation** on indwelling devices (central lines, prosthetic valves, joint replacements)
- Right-sided endocarditis classically seen in IV drug users; septic pulmonary emboli common
- Source control is critical — bacteremia without a removable source requires prolonged IV therapy (≥4–6 weeks for endocarditis/osteomyelitis)

**Antibiotic Selection**
- **Cefazolin is now preferred over antistaphylococcal penicillins** for MSSA bacteremia based on recent evidence showing lower 30-day mortality (RR 0.70) and significantly less nephrotoxicity (RR 0.36) compared to nafcillin/oxacillin [1-4]
- Standard dosing: Cefazolin 2 g IV every 8 hours; increase to 2 g every 6 hours for critically ill patients or endocarditis [1]
- Nafcillin/oxacillin remain acceptable alternatives when cefazolin is unavailable or contraindicated
- Vancomycin should be reserved for severe beta-lactam allergies; it is inferior to beta-lactams for MSSA

## **Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA)**

**Overview**
Methicillin-resistant _S. aureus_ carries the _mecA_ gene encoding PBP2a, conferring resistance to all beta-lactams except ceftaroline and ceftobiprole. MRSA is a leading cause of healthcare-associated infections and carries significant morbidity and mortality in the ICU.

**Characteristics**

|Feature|Detail|
|---|---|
|Gram Stain|Positive|
|Metabolism|Facultative anaerobe|
|Arrangement|Clusters|
|First-Line Antibiotics|Vancomycin (AUC-guided dosing, target AUC 400–600); Daptomycin 8–10 mg/kg (non-pulmonary)|
|Second-Line Antibiotics|Linezolid, Ceftaroline, Ceftobiprole, Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (skin/soft tissue)|
|Typical Sites of Infection|Bloodstream, skin/soft tissue, pneumonia (especially post-influenza), endocarditis, osteomyelitis, prosthetic device infections|

**Pathogenicity**
- Same virulence factors as MSSA plus intrinsic beta-lactam resistance
- **HA-MRSA** (healthcare-associated): typically multidrug resistant; associated with indwelling lines and post-surgical infections
- **CA-MRSA** (community-acquired): often carries PVL; aggressive necrotizing skin infections and necrotizing pneumonia
- **Daptomycin** is inactivated by pulmonary surfactant — do NOT use for MRSA pneumonia; use Vancomycin or Linezolid
- For bacteremia/endocarditis, higher daptomycin doses (8–10 mg/kg) are preferred over the FDA-approved 6 mg/kg due to concentration-dependent killing [1]

**Vancomycin Dosing and Monitoring**
- **AUC-guided dosing is now the standard of care** — target AUC/MIC 400–600 mg·h/L (assuming MIC ≤1 mg/L) [5-6]
- **Trough-only monitoring (15–20 mg/L) is NO LONGER RECOMMENDED** due to association with increased nephrotoxicity without improved efficacy [5][7]
- AUC monitoring requires either: (1) two-level PK sampling (peak at 1–2 hours post-infusion + trough), or (2) Bayesian software with at least one trough level (preferred method) [5]
- Loading dose: 20–35 mg/kg (max 3 g), then maintenance 15–20 mg/kg every 12 hours based on renal function [1][5]
- Target AUC should be achieved within 24–48 hours of therapy initiation [5]

### Staphylococcus epidermidis

**Overview**
A coagulase-negative staphylococcus and normal skin flora that is the most common cause of healthcare-associated bloodstream infections related to indwelling devices. Often considered a contaminant in a single blood culture but clinically significant in prosthetic valve endocarditis or central line infections.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Positive |
| Metabolism | Facultative anaerobe |
| Arrangement | Clusters |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Vancomycin (most clinical strains are methicillin-resistant) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Daptomycin, Linezolid, Rifampin (adjunct for biofilm) |
| Typical Sites of Infection | CLABSI, prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), prosthetic joint infections, CSF shunt infections |

**Pathogenicity**
- Forms robust **biofilms** on prosthetic materials — hallmark of its pathogenicity
- Rarely invasive in immunocompetent hosts without foreign material
- Two or more positive blood cultures from separate draws increase likelihood of true infection vs. contamination
- Rifampin added as adjunct for biofilm-associated infections but never used as monotherapy (rapid resistance development)

### Staphylococcus lugdunensis

**Overview**
A coagulase-negative staphylococcus that behaves more like *S. aureus* in terms of virulence. Unlike other CoNS, *S. lugdunensis* causes aggressive native valve endocarditis and should never be dismissed as a contaminant in blood cultures.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Positive |
| Metabolism | Facultative anaerobe |
| Arrangement | Clusters |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Penicillin G or Nafcillin/Oxacillin (if susceptible — most strains are penicillin-sensitive) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Vancomycin (if resistant) |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Native valve endocarditis (aggressive, destructive), skin/soft tissue, wound infections |

**Pathogenicity**
- Produces **SLUSH (S. lugdunensis synergistic hemolysis)** and a clumping factor — contributes to aggressive endocarditis
- Unlike most CoNS, native valve endocarditis due to *S. lugdunensis* frequently requires valve surgery
- Often susceptible to penicillin — always test; if susceptible, penicillin is preferred over vancomycin

### Streptococcus pneumoniae

**Overview**
The leading cause of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), bacterial meningitis in adults, and otitis media. A major ICU pathogen due to its capacity to cause severe invasive disease. Encapsulated organism; vaccine-preventable.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Positive |
| Metabolism | Facultative anaerobe |
| Arrangement | Lancet-shaped diplococci; short chains |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Penicillin G (if susceptible); Ceftriaxone (for meningitis or resistant strains) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Vancomycin + Ceftriaxone (empiric meningitis); Respiratory fluoroquinolone (CAP) |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Pneumonia, meningitis, bacteremia, sinusitis, otitis media, septic arthritis |

**Pathogenicity**
- **Polysaccharide capsule** — primary virulence factor; antiphagocytic; basis for PCV and PPSV vaccines
- **Pneumolysin** — pore-forming toxin causing cell lysis and inflammation
- Penicillin resistance is increasing — always check susceptibilities; empiric meningitis coverage requires Vancomycin + Ceftriaxone until sensitivities return
- Asplenic patients at highest risk for overwhelming pneumococcal sepsis (OPSI)

### Streptococcus anginosus Group

**Overview**
A subgroup of viridans streptococci (*S. anginosus*, *S. intermedius*, *S. constellatus*) known for their propensity to form abscesses. Unlike other streptococci, they are characteristically purulent.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Positive |
| Metabolism | Facultative anaerobe |
| Arrangement | Chains |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Penicillin G or Ampicillin |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Ceftriaxone, Clindamycin |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Brain abscesses, liver abscesses, intra-abdominal abscesses, pleural empyema, dental/periodontal abscesses |

**Pathogenicity**
- Strongly associated with **abscess formation** — whenever *S. anginosus* group is isolated, look for occult abscess
- Produce hyaluronidase and other enzymes facilitating tissue invasion
- Often mixed infections with anaerobes
- Source control (drainage) is essential alongside antibiotics

### Group A Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes)

**Overview**
Causes a wide spectrum of disease from superficial pharyngitis and impetigo to life-threatening necrotizing fasciitis and streptococcal toxic shock syndrome (STSS). One of the most important causes of rapidly progressive soft tissue infection in the ICU.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Positive |
| Metabolism | Facultative anaerobe |
| Arrangement | Chains |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Penicillin G (IV); add Clindamycin for toxin suppression in severe/invasive disease |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Ampicillin, Cephalosporins; Vancomycin if penicillin-allergic |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Pharyngitis, cellulitis, impetigo, erysipelas, necrotizing fasciitis, myositis, STSS, bacteremia |

**Pathogenicity**
- **M protein** — major virulence factor; antiphagocytic; basis for serotyping
- **Streptolysins S and O** — tissue destruction; ASO titer used for post-streptococcal disease diagnosis
- **Pyrogenic exotoxins (SPE-A, B, C)** — superantigens causing STSS
- Clindamycin added in severe invasive disease to suppress toxin production (ribosomal inhibitor halts toxin synthesis even without killing the organism)
- Penicillin remains fully effective — no documented resistance in GAS

### Group B Streptococcus (Streptococcus agalactiae)

**Overview**
The leading cause of neonatal sepsis and meningitis. In adults, causes invasive infections in elderly, diabetic, immunocompromised patients, and pregnant women. Increasingly recognized as a cause of bacteremia and endocarditis in non-neonatal populations.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Positive |
| Metabolism | Facultative anaerobe |
| Arrangement | Chains |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Penicillin G or Ampicillin |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Ceftriaxone, Vancomycin (penicillin allergy) |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Neonatal sepsis/meningitis, maternal chorioamnionitis, adult bacteremia, skin/soft tissue infections, osteomyelitis, endocarditis (elderly/diabetics) |

**Pathogenicity**
- **CAMP factor** — enhances RBC lysis; used for laboratory identification
- **Polysaccharide capsule** — antiphagocytic
- Colonizes rectovaginal area of ~25% of pregnant women; screening and intrapartum prophylaxis prevents neonatal transmission
- Adult invasive GBS disease increasing — associated with diabetes, malignancy, liver disease

### ## **Enterococcus faecalis**

**Overview**
Normal GI flora that has emerged as a major nosocomial pathogen. Intrinsically resistant to many antibiotics. _E. faecalis_ accounts for ~80–90% of enterococcal infections and is generally more susceptible than _E. faecium_.

**Characteristics**

|Feature|Detail|
|---|---|
|Gram Stain|Positive|
|Metabolism|Facultative anaerobe|
|Arrangement|Pairs and short chains|
|First-Line Antibiotics|Ampicillin (UTI, uncomplicated infections); **Ampicillin + Ceftriaxone (preferred for endocarditis)**|
|Second-Line Antibiotics|Ampicillin + Gentamicin (endocarditis, if ceftriaxone unavailable); Vancomycin, Linezolid, Daptomycin|
|Typical Sites of Infection|UTI, bacteremia, endocarditis, intra-abdominal infections, wound infections, biliary infections|

**Pathogenicity**
- Intrinsically resistant to cephalosporins, clindamycin, and low-level aminoglycosides
- **Endocarditis** requires synergistic bactericidal therapy
- Gelatinase and cytolysin contribute to virulence
- Can acquire VRE resistance (less common in _E. faecalis_ than _E. faecium_)

**Endocarditis Treatment**
- **Ampicillin + Ceftriaxone is now preferred over Ampicillin + Gentamicin** for _E. faecalis_ endocarditis (both native and prosthetic valve) [8-9]
- Ampicillin 2 g IV every 4 hours + Ceftriaxone 2 g IV every 12 hours × 6 weeks [9]
- Advantages: Non-inferior efficacy, significantly lower nephrotoxicity (OR 0.45), fewer drug withdrawals due to adverse events (OR 0.11), no need for aminoglycoside level monitoring [9-10]
- **Ampicillin + Gentamicin remains acceptable** when ceftriaxone is unavailable or for aminoglycoside-susceptible, high-level gentamicin-resistant strains that are streptomycin-susceptible [9]
- For high-level aminoglycoside resistance: Ampicillin + Ceftriaxone is the regimen of choice

### Enterococcus faecium

**Overview**
Accounts for ~10–20% of enterococcal infections but is far more antibiotic-resistant than *E. faecalis*. Vancomycin-resistant *E. faecium* (VRE) is a major challenge in ICU settings, particularly in immunocompromised and transplant patients.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Positive |
| Metabolism | Facultative anaerobe |
| Arrangement | Pairs and short chains |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Vancomycin (if susceptible); Linezolid or Daptomycin (VRE) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Tigecycline, Quinupristin-dalfopristin (not for *E. faecalis*) |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Bacteremia, UTI, endocarditis, intra-abdominal infections, wound infections |

**Pathogenicity**
- High intrinsic ampicillin resistance distinguishes from *E. faecalis*
- **VanA** and **VanB** resistance genes encode altered peptidoglycan precursors that don't bind vancomycin
- VRE colonization precedes infection — contact precautions essential
- Linezolid preferred for VRE; Daptomycin used for bacteremia/endocarditis (higher doses, 8–12 mg/kg)

### Lactobacillus Species

**Overview**
Normal flora of the GI tract, vagina, and oral cavity. Rarely pathogenic except in severely immunocompromised or critically ill patients. Notable because it is intrinsically resistant to vancomycin, which can cause diagnostic confusion.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Positive |
| Metabolism | Facultative anaerobe (some obligate anaerobes) |
| Arrangement | Rods; pairs or chains |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Penicillin G or Ampicillin ± Gentamicin |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Clindamycin, Erythromycin |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Bacteremia (rare, in immunocompromised), endocarditis (rare), liver abscess |

**Pathogenicity**
- **Intrinsic vancomycin resistance** — important to recognize in the lab; does not indicate VRE
- Pathogenesis in critically ill patients related to intestinal barrier breakdown and bacterial translocation
- Probiotic *Lactobacillus* strains have rarely caused bacteremia in ICU patients receiving supplemental probiotics — use with caution in critically ill

### Listeria monocytogenes

**Overview**
A foodborne pathogen causing serious invasive disease in pregnant women, neonates, elderly, and immunocompromised patients (especially those on steroids or with defects in cell-mediated immunity). Notably resistant to cephalosporins, which do not provide coverage.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Positive |
| Metabolism | Facultative anaerobe |
| Arrangement | Short rods; may form chains |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Ampicillin ± Gentamicin (synergy for meningitis/bacteremia) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Meningitis/meningoencephalitis, bacteremia, neonatal sepsis, spontaneous abortion, gastroenteritis (immunocompetent) |

**Pathogenicity**
- Uses **actin-based motility** (ActA protein) to spread cell-to-cell, evading humoral immunity
- **Listeriolysin O (LLO)** — pore-forming toxin enabling escape from phagocytic vacuoles
- Cell-mediated immunity (T cells) is primary defense — hence risk in pregnancy, steroid use, HIV, transplant
- **Cephalosporins have no activity** — empiric meningitis coverage in high-risk patients (elderly, immunocompromised, pregnant) must include Ampicillin

### ## **Clostridium difficile** _(Clostridioides difficile)_

**Overview**
The leading cause of antibiotic-associated diarrhea and colitis in healthcare settings. Spore-forming organism that survives on environmental surfaces and is not killed by alcohol-based hand sanitizers. Produces toxins A and B causing mucosal inflammation and pseudomembranous colitis.

**Characteristics**

|Feature|Detail|
|---|---|
|Gram Stain|Positive|
|Metabolism|Obligate anaerobe|
|Arrangement|Rods (spore-forming)|
|First-Line Antibiotics|**Fidaxomicin 200 mg PO BID × 10 days (preferred)**; Vancomycin 125 mg PO QID × 10 days (acceptable alternative)|
|Second-Line Antibiotics|Bezlotoxumab (mAb for recurrence prevention — **currently unavailable due to drug shortage**); Fecal Microbiota Transplant (FMT) for recurrent CDI|
|Typical Sites of Infection|Colon (pseudomembranous colitis), fulminant colitis, toxic megacolon|

**Pathogenicity**
- **Toxin A** (enterotoxin) — mucosal disruption, fluid secretion
- **Toxin B** (cytotoxin) — more potent; cytoskeletal disruption; primary driver of disease
- **Binary toxin (CDT)** — produced by hypervirulent NAP1/BI/027 strain; associated with more severe disease
- Spores resist alcohol-based hand sanitizers — **hand washing with soap and water required**

**Treatment Recommendations**
- **Fidaxomicin is now preferred over vancomycin** for initial CDI episodes due to similar cure rates but significantly lower recurrence rates (13% vs 27%, p=0.02) [11-12]
- Fidaxomicin increases sustained clinical response (RR 1.16) without increasing adverse events [12]
- Vancomycin remains an acceptable alternative, particularly when cost is a limiting factor [11-12]
- **Metronidazole is NO LONGER RECOMMENDED** for initial CDI treatment (inferior to vancomycin) [11]
- **Fulminant CDI** (hypotension, ileus, megacolon, WBC >35,000, lactate >5): Vancomycin 500 mg PO/NG QID + Metronidazole 500 mg IV every 8 hours; early surgical consultation [11]
- **Bezlotoxumab** (monoclonal antibody against toxin B) is currently experiencing a drug shortage and is unavailable in the United States

### ## **Clostridium botulinum**

**Overview**
Produces botulinum toxin, the most potent biological toxin known. Causes [botulism](https://www.openevidence.com/rare-disease/botulism) — a descending flaccid paralysis — through foodborne, wound, or infant intestinal mechanisms. An ICU condition requiring mechanical ventilation in severe cases.

**Characteristics**

|Feature|Detail|
|---|---|
|Gram Stain|Positive|
|Metabolism|Obligate anaerobe|
|Arrangement|Rods (spore-forming)|
|First-Line Treatment|**Heptavalent Botulinum Antitoxin (HBAT/BAT)** — call CDC (770-488-7100) or state health department 24/7; supportive care / mechanical ventilation|
|Second-Line|Penicillin G or Metronidazole (wound botulism — to eradicate organism); **Surgical debridement (wound botulism — essential for source control)**|
|Typical Sites of Infection|GI (foodborne), wounds (wound botulism — especially IV drug use), infant intestinal colonization|

**Pathogenicity**
- **Botulinum neurotoxin (BoNT)** — cleaves SNARE proteins at the neuromuscular junction, preventing acetylcholine release → flaccid paralysis
- Descending paralysis: cranial nerve palsies (diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia) → descending limb/trunk weakness → respiratory failure
- Antitoxin must be given **early** — neutralizes only unbound toxin; does not reverse established paralysis
- Recovery occurs over weeks to months via sprouting of new nerve terminals

**Treatment Timing and Priorities**
- **Antitoxin should be administered as soon as botulism is suspected** — do NOT wait for laboratory confirmation [13-15]
- **Early treatment (≤2 days of symptom onset) is associated with:**
    - Shorter hospital stay (15 vs 25 days, p<0.01) [14]
    - Shorter ICU stay (10 vs 17 days, p=0.04) [14]
    - Shorter duration of mechanical ventilation (6 vs 14.5 days) [15]
    - Reduced mortality [16]
- **[Wound botulism](https://www.openevidence.com/rare-disease/wound-botulism) management:**
    - Antitoxin administration is the **highest priority** and should be given immediately upon clinical suspicion [13-14][17]
    - **Surgical debridement is essential** for source control and should NOT be delayed [13][17-18]
    - Ideally, antitoxin is given before debridement when immediately available, but **do not delay necessary surgical debridement** waiting for antitoxin [13]
    - Antibiotics (Penicillin G or Metronidazole) should be administered to eradicate _C. botulinum_ from the wound [13][17]
- Antitoxin is available 24/7 through state health departments in coordination with CDC

### Mycobacterium tuberculosis

**Overview**
The causative agent of tuberculosis (TB), one of the leading infectious causes of death worldwide. In the ICU, presents as severe pulmonary TB, miliary TB, or tuberculous meningitis. Requires airborne precautions (N95 mask, negative pressure room).

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Not applicable — acid-fast bacillus (Ziehl-Neelsen or auramine-rhodamine stain) |
| Metabolism | Obligate aerobe |
| Arrangement | Slender rods; may form cords ("serpentine cords") |
| First-Line Antibiotics | **Standard 6-month:** RIPE (2HRZE/4HR) — Rifampin + Isoniazid + Pyrazinamide + Ethambutol × 2 months, then Rifampin + Isoniazid × 4 months. **New 4-month option:** 2HPZM/2HPM (rifapentine-moxifloxacin regimen, conditionally recommended ≥12 years, drug-susceptible pulmonary TB) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Fluoroquinolones (Levofloxacin/Moxifloxacin), Linezolid, Bedaquiline (MDR/XDR-TB) |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Pulmonary (primary), miliary (hematogenous spread), lymph nodes (scrofula), meningitis, vertebral (Pott's disease), pericarditis, peritonitis |

**Pathogenicity**
- **Waxy mycolic acid cell wall** — responsible for acid-fast staining and resistance to desiccation and many disinfectants
- Survives intracellularly within macrophages by preventing phagolysosome fusion (via ManLAM)
- **Latent TB** — contained by granuloma formation; reactivation risk with immunosuppression (steroids, TNF-alpha inhibitors, HIV)
- Pyridoxine (B6) supplementation required with Isoniazid to prevent peripheral neuropathy
- Airborne transmission — sputum AFB smear and culture required for diagnosis; GeneXpert MTB/RIF for rapid detection and rifampin resistance

**Treatment Regimens:**
| Regimen | Drugs | Duration | Indication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Standard RIPE (2HRZE/4HR)** | Rifampin + Isoniazid + Pyrazinamide + Ethambutol → Rifampin + Isoniazid | 6 months (extend to 9 months if cavitation on CXR AND positive culture at 2 months) | Drug-susceptible pulmonary TB; all ages |
| **4-month rifapentine-moxifloxacin (2HPZM/2HPM)** | Rifapentine + Isoniazid + Pyrazinamide + Moxifloxacin → Rifapentine + Isoniazid + Moxifloxacin | 4 months | Drug-susceptible pulmonary TB; age ≥12 years; conditionally recommended (WHO/ATS 2022; moderate certainty) |
| **Pediatric 4-month (2HRZE/2HR)** | Rifampin + Isoniazid + Pyrazinamide + Ethambutol → Rifampin + Isoniazid | 4 months | Nonsevere TB; ages 3 months–16 years; **strongly recommended** (replaces 6-month standard for nonsevere disease) |
| **MDR-TB** | Bedaquiline + Linezolid + Levofloxacin/Moxifloxacin ± other agents | 6–18 months (WHO 2022 BPaL regimens) | RIF or INH resistance confirmed |

### Escherichia coli

**Overview**
The most common cause of urinary tract infections and gram-negative bacteremia. A major component of the normal GI flora that becomes pathogenic when it gains access to sterile sites. Extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) producing strains are an increasing concern in ICU settings.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Negative |
| Metabolism | Facultative anaerobe |
| Arrangement | Rods |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Ceftriaxone (uncomplicated); Piperacillin-tazobactam (moderate severity); Meropenem (ESBL or severe sepsis) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Ciprofloxacin (if susceptible), TMP-SMX (UTI), Ceftazidime-avibactam (carbapenem-resistant) |
| Typical Sites of Infection | UTI/pyelonephritis, bacteremia, intra-abdominal infections, pneumonia (HAP), meningitis (neonates), CLABSI |

**Pathogenicity**
- **Type 1 and P fimbriae** — mediate adhesion to uroepithelium
- **Shiga toxin** (STEC/EHEC) — inhibits protein synthesis; causes HUS (hemolytic uremic syndrome)
- **Heat-labile and heat-stable enterotoxins** (ETEC) — traveler's diarrhea
- ESBL strains: resistant to all penicillins and cephalosporins; Meropenem preferred
- KPC (Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemase) can also occur in *E. coli* — treat with Ceftazidime-avibactam

### Klebsiella pneumoniae

**Overview**
An encapsulated gram-negative rod causing healthcare-associated pneumonia, bacteremia, UTI, and liver abscesses (hypervirulent strains). Carbapenem-resistant *Klebsiella* (CRKP) carrying KPC enzymes is a critical threat organism in ICUs.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Negative |
| Metabolism | Facultative anaerobe |
| Arrangement | Rods |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Ceftriaxone (susceptible strains); Meropenem (ESBL or moderate-severe); Ceftazidime-avibactam (KPC) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Imipenem-cilastatin-relebactam, Cefiderocol (pandrug-resistant) |
| Typical Sites of Infection | HAP/VAP, UTI, bacteremia, liver abscess (hypervirulent), endophthalmitis, meningitis (neonates) |

**Pathogenicity**
- **Thick polysaccharide capsule** — "string test" positive in hypervirulent strains; antiphagocytic
- Classic: "currant jelly" sputum in alcoholic/immunocompromised patients with lobar pneumonia
- **Hypervirulent Klebsiella** (hvKP) — causes metastatic infections (liver abscess + endophthalmitis) in otherwise healthy patients, especially in Southeast Asia
- ESBL and KPC enzyme production rapidly spreading in healthcare settings — always check susceptibilities

### Pseudomonas aeruginosa

**Overview**
An obligate aerobe and major opportunistic pathogen with intrinsic resistance to many antibiotics. One of the most feared ICU pathogens due to its resistance mechanisms, biofilm formation, and ability to cause fulminant VAP and bacteremia. Requires antipseudomonal coverage in empiric therapy for HAP/VAP.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Negative |
| Metabolism | Obligate aerobe |
| Arrangement | Rods |
| First-Line Antibiotics | **Empiric:** Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5g IV q6h (extended infusion preferred) **OR** Cefepime 2g IV q8h (extended infusion preferred) **OR** Meropenem 1–2g IV q8h (high local resistance or prior antipseudomonal exposure) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Ceftazidime, Aztreonam, Ciprofloxacin; **MDR/DTR strains:** Ceftolozane-tazobactam, Ceftazidime-avibactam, Imipenem-cilastatin-relebactam |
| Typical Sites of Infection | VAP, HAP, bacteremia, UTI (catheter-associated), wound infections (burn patients), external otitis malignant, ecthyma gangrenosum |

**Pathogenicity**
- **Alginate** — biofilm component; protects from antibiotics and phagocytes (especially in CF patients)
- **Exotoxin A** — inhibits EF-2 (same mechanism as diphtheria toxin); major virulence factor causing tissue necrosis
- **Exoenzymes (ExoS, ExoT, ExoU)** — injected via Type III secretion system; ExoU is most cytotoxic
- **Pyocyanin** — blue-green pigment; generates reactive oxygen species
- Characteristic blue-green pus and sweet/grape-like odor
- **Ecthyma gangrenosum** — pathognomonic skin lesion (necrotic black ulcer) in bacteremic patients
- Rapidly develops resistance on therapy — avoid monotherapy with fluoroquinolones; combination debated but often used for severe infection
- **Aminoglycoside monotherapy is CONTRAINDICATED** for Pseudomonas pneumonia/VAP — poor lung penetration makes monotherapy inadequate; aminoglycosides may be used as part of combination therapy only (IDSA/ATS HAP/VAP Guidelines 2016; PMID 31823149)
- Extended-infusion beta-lactams (e.g., cefepime 2g over 3–4 hours q8h) optimize pharmacodynamic target attainment for susceptible strains
- Repeat susceptibility testing recommended — resistance can develop rapidly on therapy

**Definitive Therapy by Susceptibility:**
| Phenotype | Preferred Agent(s) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Susceptible | Pip-tazo, ceftazidime, cefepime, or meropenem (monotherapy acceptable) | Prefer traditional beta-lactams over carbapenems when susceptible to preserve carbapenem spectrum |
| Carbapenem-resistant, traditional BL-susceptible | Cefepime or pip-tazo extended infusion | PK/PD optimization over 3–4 hour infusions |
| MDR (resistant to ≥3 drug classes) | Ceftolozane-tazobactam, ceftazidime-avibactam | Check for MBL (metallo-beta-lactamase) — ceftazidime-avibactam inactive vs. MBL |
| DTR (difficult-to-treat resistant) | Imipenem-cilastatin-relebactam, cefiderocol | Last-resort agents; ID consult mandatory |

### Acinetobacter Species

**Overview**
A non-fermentative gram-negative coccobacillus notorious for its ability to acquire and develop multidrug resistance (MDR), extensively drug-resistant (XDR), and pandrug-resistant (PDR) phenotypes. A leading cause of VAP in ICUs, particularly in trauma and burn units and during wartime conflicts.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Negative |
| Metabolism | Obligate aerobe |
| Arrangement | Coccobacilli (diplococci on some media — can be mistaken for *Neisseria*) |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Meropenem (if susceptible); Ampicillin-sulbactam (sulbactam has intrinsic activity vs. *Acinetobacter*) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Colistin / Polymyxin B (last resort); Cefiderocol; Tigecycline (adjunct) |
| Typical Sites of Infection | VAP, HAP, bacteremia, wound infections, meningitis (post-neurosurgical), UTI |

**Pathogenicity**
- Remarkable ability to survive on dry surfaces for weeks — environmental contamination and horizontal transmission in ICUs
- OXA-carbapenemases (OXA-23, OXA-24/40) are the primary mechanism of carbapenem resistance
- Sulbactam-based regimens (Ampicillin-sulbactam or Cefoperazone-sulbactam) used because sulbactam has direct bactericidal activity against *Acinetobacter*, independent of the beta-lactam partner
- Colistin nephrotoxicity is significant — monitor renal function closely; loading dose required

### Enterobacter Species

**Overview**
A gram-negative rod that is part of the normal gut flora but causes opportunistic infections in ICU patients. Notable for its ability to develop resistance to cephalosporins through **inducible AmpC beta-lactamases** — third-generation cephalosporins should be avoided even if initially susceptible.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Negative |
| Metabolism | Facultative anaerobe |
| Arrangement | Rods |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Cefepime (stable to AmpC) or Meropenem (severe) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Ciprofloxacin (if susceptible), Piperacillin-tazobactam |
| Typical Sites of Infection | HAP/VAP, UTI, bacteremia, wound infections, intra-abdominal infections |

**Pathogenicity**
- **Inducible AmpC beta-lactamase** — can be de-repressed during therapy, causing treatment failure with cephalosporins despite initial susceptibility ("inoculum effect")
- Rule: **Never use 3rd-generation cephalosporins for serious Enterobacter infections** — use Cefepime or carbapenem
- Part of the **ESKAPE** organisms (Enterococcus, Staph aureus, Klebsiella, Acinetobacter, Pseudomonas, Enterobacter) — major causes of drug-resistant hospital infections

### Serratia marcescens

**Overview**
A gram-negative rod known for its characteristic red/pink pigment (prodigiosin) on some colonies. A nosocomial pathogen associated with contaminated medical equipment, IV fluids, and respiratory therapy equipment.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Negative |
| Metabolism | Facultative anaerobe |
| Arrangement | Rods |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Ceftriaxone or Cefepime (susceptible strains); Meropenem (resistant) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Ciprofloxacin, TMP-SMX |
| Typical Sites of Infection | UTI, bacteremia, VAP, wound infections, endocarditis (IV drug use), keratitis |

**Pathogenicity**

### Proteus mirabilis

**Overview**
A common cause of complicated UTIs, particularly in patients with urinary catheters or structural urologic abnormalities. Produces urease, which alkalinizes urine and promotes struvite (staghorn) kidney stone formation.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Negative |
| Metabolism | Facultative anaerobe |
| Arrangement | Rods (highly motile — "swarming" on agar) |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Ampicillin (if susceptible); Ceftriaxone; TMP-SMX (UTI) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Ciprofloxacin, Piperacillin-tazobactam |
| Typical Sites of Infection | UTI (including catheter-associated), pyelonephritis, struvite urolithiasis, wound infections, bacteremia |

**Pathogenicity**
- **Urease** — hydrolyzes urea to ammonia → urine alkalinization → struvite stone formation → obstruction and biofilm reservoir
- **Flagella** and **fimbriae** — facilitate colonization of urinary tract and catheters
- Intrinsically resistant to tetracycline and nitrofurantoin
- Stone removal often necessary to clear infection in struvite nephrolithiasis

### Haemophilus influenzae

**Overview**
A small gram-negative coccobacillus that is a major cause of respiratory infections. Encapsulated type b (Hib) strain was historically the leading cause of bacterial meningitis in children — now vaccine-preventable. Non-typeable strains cause the majority of adult respiratory infections.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Negative |
| Metabolism | Facultative anaerobe |
| Arrangement | Coccobacilli (pleomorphic) |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Amoxicillin-clavulanate (outpatient); **Ceftriaxone 2g IV q12h** (IV/meningitis — empiric regardless of beta-lactamase status) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Azithromycin, TMP-SMX, Fluoroquinolone; Meropenem (BLNAR meningitis) |
| Typical Sites of Infection | COPD exacerbations, pneumonia, sinusitis, otitis media, meningitis (Hib in unvaccinated), epiglottitis (Hib) |

**Pathogenicity**
- **Requires X (hemin) and V (NAD) factors** — only grows on blood agar with satellite colonies near *S. aureus*
- ~30–40% produce **beta-lactamase** — test susceptibility; amoxicillin alone may fail
- **BLNAR (beta-lactamase negative, ampicillin-resistant)** strains — mediated by PBP3 mutations; require meropenem at high doses for CNS infections; prevalence rising in some regions
- Type b capsule (Hib) — antiphagocytic; Hib vaccine dramatically reduced invasive disease
- Non-typeable *H. influenzae* (NTHi) — most common cause of adult respiratory infections; colonizes COPD airways

**Treatment by Scenario:**
| Scenario | Treatment | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Non-CNS infection, beta-lactamase negative | Amoxicillin or ampicillin (once susceptibility confirmed) | Only after susceptibility testing |
| Non-CNS infection, beta-lactamase positive | Ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, or fluoroquinolone | Empiric choice |
| Meningitis (any strain) | **Ceftriaxone 2g IV q12h** empirically | Always use ceftriaxone — do not use ampicillin empirically even if beta-lactamase negative due to BLNAR risk |
| BLNAR meningitis (confirmed) | Meropenem (high dose) | Ceftriaxone resistance in BLNAR; meropenem monotherapy preferred |

### Moraxella catarrhalis

**Overview**
An obligate aerobe and common colonizer of the upper respiratory tract. A frequent but underappreciated cause of COPD exacerbations, community-acquired pneumonia, and otitis media. Almost universally beta-lactamase producing.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Negative |
| Metabolism | Obligate aerobe |
| Arrangement | Diplococci (kidney bean-shaped) |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Amoxicillin-clavulanate; Azithromycin |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | TMP-SMX, Fluoroquinolones, Ceftriaxone |
| Typical Sites of Infection | COPD exacerbations, community-acquired pneumonia, otitis media, sinusitis |

**Pathogenicity**
- >95% of strains produce **BRO beta-lactamase** — amoxicillin alone is ineffective
- **UspA proteins** — facilitate complement evasion and respiratory epithelial adhesion
- Rarely causes invasive disease in immunocompetent adults; can cause bacteremia in immunocompromised

### Neisseria gonorrhoeae

**Overview**
The causative agent of gonorrhea, the second most common bacterial sexually transmitted infection. In the ICU, disseminated gonococcal infection (DGI) — presenting as septic arthritis, tenosynovitis, and dermatitis — is the most relevant presentation. Increasing antimicrobial resistance is a major public health concern.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Negative |
| Metabolism | Obligate aerobe |
| Arrangement | Diplococci (intracellular within PMNs on Gram stain of urethral discharge) |
| First-Line Antibiotics | **Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM × 1** (uncomplicated urogenital/rectal/pharyngeal); **Ceftriaxone 1g IV/IM q24h** (DGI/arthritis-dermatitis); **Ceftriaxone 1–2g IV q24h** (meningitis/endocarditis) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Gentamicin 240 mg IM × 1 + Azithromycin 2g PO × 1 (cephalosporin allergy only); no reliable fluoroquinolone alternatives |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Urethritis, cervicitis, proctitis, pharyngitis, DGI (arthritis-dermatitis syndrome, septic arthritis, rarely endocarditis/meningitis) |

**Pathogenicity**
- **Pili (fimbriae)** — facilitate adhesion to mucosal epithelium; antigenically variable (phase variation evades immunity)
- **IgA1 protease** — cleaves secretory IgA on mucosal surfaces
- **Opa proteins** — mediate invasion of epithelial and phagocytic cells
- **Complement deficiency** (C5–C9) predisposes to recurrent DGI — screen with CH50
- Fluoroquinolone and penicillin resistance now widespread — ceftriaxone remains first-line
- **Azithromycin is NO LONGER recommended** as routine dual therapy — removed from CDC 2021 STI Treatment Guidelines due to rapidly rising resistance (nearly 5% of isolates with elevated MIC by 2018; PMID 35416971); ceftriaxone monotherapy is now standard of care

**Treatment by Scenario (CDC 2021 STI Guidelines):**
| Infection Type | Treatment | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Uncomplicated urogenital / rectal | Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM × 1 dose | Single dose |
| Uncomplicated pharyngeal | Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM × 1 dose | Single dose; test-of-cure recommended at 7–14 days |
| DGI — arthritis-dermatitis syndrome | Ceftriaxone 1g IV/IM q24h | Until 24–48h after clinical improvement, then guided oral therapy; total >7 days |
| Gonococcal meningitis | Ceftriaxone 1–2g IV q24h | 10–14 days |
| Gonococcal endocarditis | Ceftriaxone 1–2g IV q24h | ≥4 weeks |
| Cephalosporin allergy | Gentamicin 240 mg IM × 1 + Azithromycin 2g PO × 1 | Single dose (only if true cephalosporin allergy) |

### Neisseria meningitidis

**Overview**
The leading cause of bacterial meningitis in adolescents and young adults, and a cause of fulminant septicemia with purpura fulminans. A medical emergency — antibiotics must be given immediately upon clinical suspicion without waiting for confirmatory testing.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Negative |
| Metabolism | Obligate aerobe |
| Arrangement | Diplococci (intracellular within PMNs in CSF) |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Penicillin G (if susceptible); Ceftriaxone (empiric or penicillin-resistant) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Meropenem, Chloramphenicol (resource-limited settings) |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Meningitis, bacteremia, purpura fulminans/DIC, Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome (bilateral adrenal hemorrhage) |

**Pathogenicity**
- **Polysaccharide capsule** — major virulence factor; basis for ACWY and B vaccines (serogroups A, B, C, W, Y, X)
- **Lipooligosaccharide (LOS)** — potent endotoxin; triggers massive cytokine release → septic shock, DIC
- **Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome** — bilateral adrenal hemorrhage causing adrenal insufficiency; requires stress-dose steroids
- Droplet transmission — close contacts require prophylaxis (Rifampin, Ciprofloxacin, or Ceftriaxone × 1)
- Dexamethasone adjunct recommended with antibiotics for bacterial meningitis (reduces hearing loss)

### Helicobacter pylori

**Overview**
A microaerophilic gram-negative spiral rod that colonizes the gastric mucosa and causes peptic ulcer disease, gastritis, and is the strongest risk factor for gastric adenocarcinoma and MALT lymphoma. Relevant in ICU patients with upper GI bleeds.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Negative |
| Metabolism | Microaerophile |
| Arrangement | Curved/spiral rods with flagella |
| First-Line Antibiotics | **Bismuth quadruple therapy (BQT)** (PPI + Bismuth + Tetracycline + Metronidazole × 14 days) — preferred due to rising clarithromycin resistance; **OR Vonoprazan-amoxicillin dual therapy** (FDA-approved 2024); Clarithromycin triple therapy only if local resistance <15% and no prior macrolide exposure |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Levofloxacin-based triple therapy; Concomitant therapy (PPI + Clarithromycin + Amoxicillin + Metronidazole); Rifabutin-based therapy (salvage, multiple failures) |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Gastric/duodenal ulcers, gastric adenocarcinoma (long-term), MALT lymphoma |

**Pathogenicity**
- **Urease** — converts urea to ammonia; neutralizes gastric acid, enabling survival in the stomach; basis for urea breath test
- **CagA (cytotoxin-associated gene A)** — injected into gastric epithelium via Type IV secretion system; promotes inflammation and carcinogenesis
- **VacA (vacuolating cytotoxin)** — forms channels in epithelial cells; causes vacuolization and apoptosis
- Clarithromycin resistance now **≥25–30% in the US** (pediatric cohort data: 24.5%; regional/global data: 29–34%) — clarithromycin triple therapy should NOT be used as routine first-line in the US
- Test and treat recommended in all patients with documented peptic ulcer disease

**Treatment Regimens (Updated 2024–2026 Guidelines):**
| Regimen | Drugs & Doses | Duration | Eradication Rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Bismuth quadruple therapy (BQT)** *(preferred)* | PPI BID + Bismuth subcitrate 300 mg QID (or subsalicylate 525 mg QID) + Tetracycline 500 mg QID + Metronidazole 500 mg QID | 14 days | 85–90% | Not affected by clarithromycin resistance; preferred in US |
| **Vonoprazan-amoxicillin dual therapy** *(FDA-approved 2024)* | Vonoprazan 20 mg BID + Amoxicillin 1000 mg TID | 14 days | ~77% overall; 70% in clarithromycin-resistant strains | Superior acid suppression vs PPI; approved for treatment-naive adults |
| **Concomitant therapy** *(non-bismuth quadruple)* | PPI BID + Clarithromycin 500 mg BID + Amoxicillin 1000 mg BID + Metronidazole 500 mg BID | 10–14 days | ~91% | Acceptable if BQT unavailable and local clarithromycin resistance <15% |
| **Clarithromycin triple therapy** *(avoid in US)* | PPI BID + Clarithromycin 500 mg BID + Amoxicillin 1000 mg BID | 14 days | 70–85% (lower with resistance) | Only if local clarithromycin resistance <15% AND no prior macrolide exposure |
| **Salvage — levofloxacin triple** | PPI BID + Levofloxacin 500 mg daily + Amoxicillin 1000 mg BID | 14 days | 75–80% | If first-line fails |
| **Salvage — rifabutin triple** | PPI BID + Rifabutin 150 mg BID + Amoxicillin 1000 mg BID | 10 days | ~70% | Reserved for multiple treatment failures |

### ## **Legionella pneumophila**

**Overview**
An intracellular gram-negative rod that causes [Legionnaires' disease](https://www.openevidence.com/rare-disease/legionnaires-disease) — an atypical pneumonia that can progress to severe respiratory failure requiring ICU admission. Transmitted via aerosolized contaminated water (cooling towers, hospital water systems, hot tubs). No person-to-person transmission.

**Characteristics**

| Feature                    | Detail                                                                                                                        |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Gram Stain                 | Negative (stains poorly; better visualized with silver stain or DFA)                                                          |
| Metabolism                 | Obligate aerobe                                                                                                               |
| Arrangement                | Rods                                                                                                                          |
| First-Line Antibiotics     | **Levofloxacin 750 mg IV daily (preferred for severe/ICU disease)**; Azithromycin 500 mg IV/PO daily (acceptable alternative) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics    | Moxifloxacin, Doxycycline; Rifampin (adjunct in severe cases — not monotherapy)                                               |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Pneumonia (Legionnaires' disease), Pontiac fever (self-limited flu-like illness without pneumonia)                            |

**Pathogenicity**
- Survives and replicates **intracellularly within alveolar macrophages** by creating a specialized vacuole (LCV — _Legionella_-containing vacuole)
- **Dot/Icm Type IV secretion system** — injects >300 effector proteins into host cell to subvert intracellular trafficking
- Beta-lactams and aminoglycosides are not effective (poor intracellular penetration); use agents with intracellular activity (macrolides, fluoroquinolones)
- **Urine antigen test** — rapid, highly sensitive/specific for **serogroup 1** (most common, ~80–90% of cases); gold standard for acute diagnosis
- **Note:** Urine antigen has poor sensitivity for non-serogroup 1 strains — PCR or culture needed if clinical suspicion persists despite negative UAT
- Hyponatremia, elevated LFTs, and diarrhea are classic extrapulmonary features — triad should raise suspicion

**Treatment Recommendations**
- **For severe/ICU Legionnaires' disease, fluoroquinolones (levofloxacin) are preferred** over macrolides based on observational data [19-20]
- Levofloxacin 750 mg IV daily is recommended for optimal outcomes in severe disease [19]
- Azithromycin and levofloxacin have similar efficacy overall, but fluoroquinolones are bactericidal while macrolides are bacteriostatic [19-20]
- Meta-analysis shows no significant difference in mortality between fluoroquinolones and macrolides (OR 0.94, p=0.661), but fluoroquinolones may be preferred in critically ill patients [21-22]
- Duration: 5–10 days for levofloxacin; 3–5 days for azithromycin [19]
- Extended courses recommended for immunosuppressed patients, severe disease, or extrapulmonary infection [19]
- Combination therapy (fluoroquinolone + macrolide) has no proven benefit and is not routinely recommended

### Bacteroides fragilis

**Overview**
The most clinically significant obligate anaerobe and the most common anaerobic isolate from intra-abdominal infections. Despite being a minority of gut anaerobes, it accounts for the majority of anaerobic bacteremia. Its polysaccharide capsule and toxin production make it uniquely pathogenic.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Negative |
| Metabolism | Obligate anaerobe |
| Arrangement | Pleomorphic rods |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Metronidazole; Piperacillin-tazobactam |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Carbapenems (Meropenem/Ertapenem), Moxifloxacin (variable resistance) |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Intra-abdominal abscesses, peritonitis, bacteremia, pelvic infections, aspiration pneumonia (below diaphragm) |

**Pathogenicity**
- **Polysaccharide A (PSA) capsule** — induces abscess formation
- **Fragilysin (BFT — Bacteroides fragilis toxin)** — zinc-dependent metalloprotease; cleaves E-cadherin; causes diarrhea (enterotoxigenic *B. fragilis* / ETBF) and is associated with colorectal cancer
- Intrinsic **resistance to penicillin** via beta-lactamase production — always requires coverage with Metronidazole or beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor combination in intra-abdominal infections
- Always cover *B. fragilis* empirically in mixed intra-abdominal or aspiration (below diaphragm) infections

### Bordetella pertussis

**Overview**
The causative agent of whooping cough (pertussis), a highly contagious respiratory illness transmitted by respiratory droplets. In adults and adolescents, presents as a prolonged cough illness ("100-day cough"). In infants, can cause life-threatening apnea and respiratory failure. Droplet precautions required.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Negative |
| Metabolism | Obligate aerobe |
| Arrangement | Small coccobacilli |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Azithromycin × 5 days (treatment and post-exposure prophylaxis) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Clarithromycin, TMP-SMX |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Respiratory tract (bronchi/bronchioles); severe disease with pneumonia and pulmonary hypertension in infants |

**Pathogenicity**
- **Pertussis toxin (PT)** — ADP-ribosylates Gi proteins, disrupting cAMP regulation; causes lymphocytosis and inhibits phagocyte chemotaxis
- **Filamentous hemagglutinin (FHA)** and **pertactin** — adhesins mediating attachment to respiratory epithelium
- **Tracheal cytotoxin** — damages ciliated respiratory epithelium, impairing mucociliary clearance
- Macrolides are most effective early (catarrhal phase); by paroxysmal phase, antibiotics reduce contagiousness but not symptom duration
- **Infant pertussis** — can present with hyperleukocytosis (WBC >100,000); leukocytapheresis considered in severe cases; ECMO for refractory pulmonary hypertension

## Spirochetes & Atypical Organisms

---

### Leptospira interrogans

**Overview**
Causes leptospirosis, a zoonotic infection transmitted through contact with water or soil contaminated by urine of infected animals (rats, livestock). ICU-relevant in severe forms: **Weil's disease** (jaundice + renal failure + bleeding) and leptospiral pulmonary hemorrhage syndrome (LPHS).

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Not applicable — spirochete; visualized by darkfield microscopy or silver stain |
| Metabolism | Obligate aerobe |
| Arrangement | Tightly coiled spiral with hooked ends |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Penicillin G IV (severe disease); Doxycycline PO (mild disease and prophylaxis) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Ceftriaxone, Ampicillin |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Systemic (bacteremic phase): fever, myalgias, headache; Immune phase: meningitis, uveitis, hepatorenal failure (Weil's disease), pulmonary hemorrhage |

**Pathogenicity**
- **LipL32 (leptospiral lipoprotein)** — abundant outer membrane protein; contributes to host cell injury
- **Sphingomyelinase and phospholipases** — damage cell membranes, contributing to hepatic and renal injury
- Bifephasic illness: initial leptospiremic phase (7 days) → apparent recovery → immune phase (antibody-mediated organ damage)
- **LPHS** — massive pulmonary hemorrhage, high mortality; mechanism unclear but immune-mediated; ECMO may be required
- Diagnosis: PCR (early leptospiremic phase), serology/MAT (immune phase)

### Treponema pallidum

**Overview**
The causative agent of syphilis, a sexually transmitted infection with three classic stages: primary (chancre), secondary (systemic dissemination), and tertiary (cardiovascular and neurological disease). Neurosyphilis and cardiovascular syphilis are most ICU-relevant. Cannot be cultured in vitro.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Not applicable — too thin to visualize; darkfield microscopy or immunofluorescence |
| Metabolism | Microaerophile |
| Arrangement | Tightly coiled spiral |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Penicillin G (all stages); Benzathine PCN G (primary/secondary/early latent); Aqueous crystalline PCN G IV (neurosyphilis, cardiovascular) |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Doxycycline (penicillin allergy, non-neurosyphilis); Ceftriaxone (neurosyphilis in PCN allergy) |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Primary: painless genital chancre; Secondary: rash (palms/soles), condyloma lata, mucous patches, systemic; Tertiary: gummas, aortitis, aortic aneurysm, neurosyphilis (tabes dorsalis, general paresis) |

**Pathogenicity**
- Extremely thin outer membrane with very few surface proteins — partially explains immune evasion
- **Outer membrane proteins** facilitate adhesion to host cells
- Cardiovascular syphilis: aortitis → aneurysm of ascending aorta/aortic root → aortic regurgitation
- **Neurosyphilis** can occur at any stage; requires IV penicillin (CSF penetration inadequate with benzathine PCN)
- **Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction** — fever, chills, hypotension within hours of treatment (massive spirochete killing → cytokine release); manage supportively, do not stop antibiotics

### Mycoplasma pneumoniae

**Overview**
The most common cause of **atypical ("walking") pneumonia** in children and young adults. A unique organism with no cell wall — inherently resistant to all beta-lactam antibiotics. Transmitted via respiratory droplets; causes epidemics in closed communities.

**Characteristics**
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gram Stain | Not applicable — no cell wall; not visible on standard Gram stain |
| Metabolism | Facultative anaerobe |
| Arrangement | Pleomorphic (no fixed shape due to absent cell wall) |
| First-Line Antibiotics | Azithromycin or Doxycycline |
| Second-Line Antibiotics | Levofloxacin or Moxifloxacin (macrolide resistance emerging in some regions) |
| Typical Sites of Infection | Community-acquired atypical pneumonia, tracheobronchitis; Extrapulmonary: hemolytic anemia (cold agglutinins), encephalitis, myocarditis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, erythema multiforme/Stevens-Johnson |

**Pathogenicity**
- **P1 adhesin protein** — tip structure mediating attachment to respiratory epithelium; "gliding motility"
- **Hydrogen peroxide and superoxide** production — oxidative damage to respiratory epithelium
- **CARDS toxin** (Community-Acquired Respiratory Distress Syndrome toxin) — ADP-ribosylase activity causing vacuolization
- **Cold agglutinins** (IgM anti-I antibodies) — produced in ~75% of patients; can cause autoimmune hemolytic anemia; elevated cold agglutinin titer supports diagnosis
- Completely resistant to beta-lactams (no cell wall target) — never treat with penicillins or cephalosporins alone
- Severe CAP with mycoplasma: rare but reported; consider in young patients without comorbidities
