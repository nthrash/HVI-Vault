# Heart Transplant ICU Management
### From OR Arrival to ICU Discharge

## Overview

Orthotopic heart transplantation (OHT) is the gold-standard therapy for end-stage heart failure refractory to medical management and device therapy. Over 4,000 heart transplants are performed annually in the United States, with contemporary 1-year survival approaching 85–88% and median survival exceeding 13 years. Despite these advances, the immediate post-operative period remains the highest-risk phase of the transplant journey. The allograft must rapidly assume full cardiac output after an obligate period of ischemia, reperfusion injury, and denervation — all while the recipient's native physiology (often a scarred, remodeled mediastinum from prior CHF, LVADs, or sternotomies) presents formidable surgical and hemodynamic challenges.

The intensivist and APP caring for the heart transplant recipient must fluently manage primary graft dysfunction, right ventricular failure, vasoplegia, arrhythmias, bleeding, renal injury, and early rejection risk — often simultaneously, and often in a patient who is completely dependent on pharmacologic support. This document provides a systematic framework for ICU management from OR arrival through safe ICU discharge.

> [!tip] Why This Matters
> The first 24–72 hours after OHT carry the highest mortality risk of the entire post-transplant course. Primary graft dysfunction is the leading cause of 30-day mortality. Early recognition and aggressive hemodynamic support are the keys to survival.

## Immediate Post-Op Arrival: Whiteboard Protocol

On patient arrival from the OR, the APP should rapidly document the following for their post OP note, which serves as the team's operational reference:

### Operative Data
- **Patient arrival time** from OR
- **Total ischemic time** (donor cross-clamp to reperfusion) — target < 4–6 hours; > 6 hours significantly increases PGD risk
- **Cardiopulmonary bypass time** and cross-clamp time
- **Blood products administered** intraoperatively (pRBC, FFP, platelets, cryoprecipitate)
- **Colloids / crystalloid volumes**
- **Estimated blood loss** and urine output
- **Intraoperative complications** (difficult weaning from bypass, arrhythmias, bleeding)

### Immunosuppression & Infection Timing
- **Induction agent administered** (basiliximab or rATG) and time
- **Steroid dose and time** (e.g., methylprednisolone 500 mg given intraoperatively)
- **Antibiotic times** — perioperative antibacterial dosing
- **CMV serostatus**: Donor/Recipient (D+/R–, D+/R+, D–/R+, D–/R–)
- **Toxoplasma serostatus**: Donor/Recipient (if D+/R–, TMP-SMX cannot be deferred)

### Hemodynamic Baseline on Arrival
- **Ventilator settings**: mode, TV, RR, FiO₂, PEEP
- **Vital signs / hemodynamics**: HR, MAP, CVP, PA pressures (sPAP/dPAP/mPAP), PCWP, CO/CI
- **Active drips**: vasopressors, inotropes, pulmonary vasodilators, sedation/analgesia
- **Mechanical support**: if IABP or temporary VAD placed intraoperatively

### Lines and Drains
- Arterial line location
- Central venous catheter / pulmonary artery catheter (Swan-Ganz) confirmation and position
- Chest tubes — location and initial output
- Foley catheter, NGT/OGT

### Immediate Orders on Arrival
- **12-lead ECG** — baseline post-transplant rhythm (junctional or sinus expected; rate typically paced or supported)
- **Portable CXR** — confirm line placement, assess cardiac silhouette, bilateral pleural effusions, pulmonary edema
- **ABG** — confirm ventilation, pH, lactate
- **Labs**: CBC, BMP, ionized calcium, magnesium, phosphorus, coagulation (PT/INR, PTT, fibrinogen), TEG/ROTEM, tacrolimus level (once initiated)

## Pathophysiology of Early Graft Failure

Understanding the mechanism of early allograft dysfunction is essential to guide therapy.

**Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury (IRI):** The donor heart undergoes obligate warm ischemia at harvest and cold preservation during transport. Upon reperfusion, a cascade of oxidative stress, calcium overload, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammatory cytokine release (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) injures the myocardium. This produces a global state of myocardial stunning that may affect both ventricles.

**Denervation:** The transplanted heart lacks autonomic innervation. This produces a resting heart rate of 90–110 bpm (dependent on intrinsic SA nodal automaticity), a blunted chronotropic response to stress, and absence of the Bezold-Jarisch reflex. Importantly, digoxin and vagal maneuvers are ineffective; atropine has no effect on heart rate. Chronotropic agents like isoproterenol, dobutamine, or temporary pacing must be used to augment rate when needed.

**Pre-existing Recipient Pulmonary Hypertension:** Many recipients have chronic elevated pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) from longstanding heart failure. The donor RV, which has been operating at normal afterload, may fail acutely when it encounters the recipient's elevated PVR. This is the most common mechanism of isolated RV failure post-transplant.

**Vasoplegia:** Cardiopulmonary bypass activates complement, triggers release of inflammatory cytokines, and depletes arginine vasopressin stores — producing a vasodilatory state with profound distributive shock, often requiring multi-agent vasopressor therapy.

## Primary Graft Dysfunction (PGD)

**Definition:** PGD is new allograft dysfunction occurring within 24 hours of transplantation that is not attributable to acute rejection, pulmonary embolism, or primary recipient disease. It is the most common cause of 30-day mortality following OHT (~40% of early deaths).

> [!warning] PGD grading must be performed at the time of diagnosis and serially at 24, 48, and 72 hours post-transplant.

### ISHLT PGD Classification (2014 Consensus Conference)

**PGD — Left Ventricular (PGD LV):**

| Grade | Definition |
|-------|-----------|
| **Mild** | LVEF ≤ 40% on echo; or hemodynamic compromise (MAP < 70, RAP > 15, PCWP > 20, CI < 2.0) requiring low-dose inotrope support |
| **Moderate** | Above + requirement for ≥ 2 inotropes or any intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) support |
| **Severe** | LVEF ≤ 40% + need for ECMO, LVAD, or other durable MCS regardless of other parameters |

**PGD — Right Ventricular (PGD RV):**
- RAP > 15 mmHg
- CI < 2.0 L/min/m²
- PCWP ≤ 15 mmHg
- Requirement for ≥ 1 inotrope/pulmonary vasodilator, RVAD, or veno-arterial ECMO

**Risk Factors for PGD:**

| Donor Factors | Recipient Factors | Operative Factors |
|---------------|------------------|-------------------|
| Older age, male donor | Prior sternotomy, CHD | Prolonged ischemic time (> 6 hrs) |
| High-dose vasopressor use | Elevated PVR / PH | Prolonged CPB time |
| Hemodynamic instability | LVAD explant | Undersized graft |
| Donation after circulatory death (DCD) | BMI > 30 | Hypothermic preservation injury |
| Cocaine/methamphetamine use | IVIG use pre-transplant | Blood transfusions > 2 units pRBC |

## Hemodynamic Management

### Monitoring Requirements
All OHT recipients require a **pulmonary artery catheter (Swan-Ganz)** in the immediate post-operative period. This is the only modality that allows simultaneous continuous assessment of RV function (via CVP and PA pressures), LV preload (PCWP), and cardiac output. PA catheter-guided management is essential given the complexity and dynamic nature of early graft physiology.

**Serial bedside echocardiography** (every 4–8 hours initially, then daily) is the primary tool for assessing biventricular function, RV dilation, pericardial effusion, and response to interventions.

### Early Restrictive Physiology

Ischemia-reperfusion injury causes myocardial edema and reduced ventricular compliance from the moment of reperfusion, peaking in the first 24 hours and gradually resolving over weeks. This makes the allograft stiffer than a normal heart — it requires higher filling pressures to generate adequate stroke volume, and cardiac output is critically preload-dependent (especially with a fixed denervated heart rate). Ensure adequate filling early; a volume-depleted, stiff allograft is harder to rescue than one that is appropriately filled. The PA catheter is essential for navigating this — do not rely on CVP alone.

### Hemodynamic Targets

| Parameter          | Target                       |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------- |
| Heart Rate         | 100–120 bpm (sinus or paced) |
| MAP                | 65–80 mmHg                   |
| CVP (RAP)          | 8–15 mmHg                    |
| PCWP               | 15–20 mmHg                   |
| Cardiac Index (CI) | ≥ 2.2 L·min⁻¹·m⁻²            |
| SvO₂               | > 60%                        |
| Urine Output       | > 0.5 mL·kg⁻¹·hr⁻¹           |
| Lactate            | < 2 mmol/L (trending down)   |
| PVR                | < 3 Wood units               |

> [!tip] Heart Rate Management
> The denervated heart cannot mount a normal reflex tachycardia. Target HR 90–110 bpm in the early post-op period to optimize CO. Use isoproterenol or temporary epicardial pacing to augment heart rate if HR < 80. A slow HR significantly impairs cardiac output in the non-compliant allograft.

### Inotropic Support

| Agent | Mechanism | Dose | Key Notes |
|-------|-----------|------|-----------|
| **Dobutamine** | β1/β2 agonist | 2–10 mcg/kg/min | First-line inotrope; also reduces PVR |
| **Milrinone** | PDE-3 inhibitor | 0.125–0.5 mcg/kg/min | Inotropy + pulmonary vasodilation; avoid loading dose; reduce in AKI |
| **Isoproterenol** | Non-selective β agonist | 0.5–2 mcg/min | Chronotropy + inotropy; pulmonary vasodilator; first-line for bradycardia/AV block |
| **Epinephrine** | α1/β1/β2 agonist | 0.01–0.1 mcg/kg/min | High-output shock; use cautiously — increases PVR at high doses |
| **Levosimendan** | Calcium sensitizer | 0.05–0.2 mcg/kg/min | Limited US availability; evidence in refractory PGD; also vasodilates |

### Vasopressor Support

| Agent | Dose | Indication |
|-------|------|-----------|
| **Norepinephrine** | 0.05–0.5 mcg/kg/min | First-line vasopressor for distributive/vasoplegic shock |
| **Vasopressin** | 0.01–0.04 units/min | Adjunct vasopressor; NE-sparing; preferred in vasoplegia |
| **Phenylephrine** | 50–200 mcg/min | Pure α1; avoid if RV failure (increases PVR) |
| **Angiotensin II** | 2.5–80 ng/kg/min | Refractory vasoplegic shock; FDA approved |
| **Methylene Blue** | 1.5–2 mg/kg IV x 1 | Vasoplegia refractory to above; inhibits NO pathway |

> [!warning] Avoid Phenylephrine in RV Failure
> Pure alpha-1 agents increase PVR and will worsen RV dysfunction. Norepinephrine + milrinone or isoproterenol is preferred for RV failure with hypotension.

## Right Ventricular Failure

RV failure is the most feared early complication of OHT, occurring in up to 20–30% of recipients. The donor RV, accustomed to normal pulmonary pressures, is suddenly exposed to the recipient's potentially elevated PVR. RV dilation leads to interventricular septal shift (D-sign on echo), impaired LV filling, and a spiral into biventricular failure.

### Diagnosis
- **PA catheter**: CVP > 15, CI < 2.0, PCWP ≤ 15 (isolated RV failure), transpulmonary gradient (mPAP – PCWP) > 12 mmHg
- **Echo**: RV dilation, D-sign (septal flattening/shift), reduced TAPSE < 16 mm, McConnell's sign, TR
- **Clinical**: Rising CVP, worsening CI, metabolic acidosis, oliguria without LV dysfunction

### Management Strategy — Stepwise Approach

**Step 1 — Optimize Heart Rate & Rhythm**
- Target HR 90–100 via isoproterenol or pacing
- Restore and maintain sinus rhythm (RV is sensitive to loss of atrial kick)
- Treat atrial flutter/fibrillation aggressively

**Step 2 — Reduce RV Afterload (Lower PVR)**

| Agent | Route | Dose | Notes |
|-------|-------|------|-------|
| **Inhaled Nitric Oxide (iNO)** | Inhaled | 20–40 ppm | Selective pulmonary vasodilator; first-line for RV failure; no systemic hypotension |
| **Inhaled Epoprostenol (iEPO)** | Nebulized | 20–50 ng/kg/min | Alternative to iNO; less costly |
| **Milrinone** | IV | 0.125–0.5 mcg/kg/min | Pulmonary vasodilation + inotropy |
| **Isoproterenol** | IV | 0.5–2 mcg/min | Pulmonary vasodilation + chronotropy |
| **Sildenafil** | PO/NGT | 20–40 mg TID | Adjunct; transitions off iNO; caution if systemic hypotension |

**Step 3 — Optimize Preload**
- RV is preload-sensitive but volume-overloaded RV worsens dilation
- Target CVP 8–12; aggressive diuresis if CVP > 15 with adequate CI
- Avoid excessive volume resuscitation — RV is highly compliant and will dilate with excess fluid

**Step 4 — Maintain Systemic Perfusion**
- Norepinephrine to maintain coronary perfusion pressure (MAP – CVP) > 50 mmHg
- Coronary perfusion of the RV depends on maintaining adequate systemic diastolic pressure relative to RV filling pressure

**Step 5 — Escalate to MCS if Refractory**
- See Mechanical Circulatory Support section

> [!warning] The Death Spiral of RV Failure
> RV dilation → septal shift → LV underfilling → low CO → systemic hypotension → reduced RV coronary perfusion → further RV ischemia → worsening dilation. Break the cycle early with iNO + milrinone + norepinephrine, and escalate to MCS before irreversible injury.

## Vasoplegia Syndrome

**Definition:** Vasoplegia is distributive/vasodilatory shock occurring post-CPB, characterized by low SVR (< 800 dynes·sec·cm⁻⁵), preserved or elevated CI, normal-to-high filling pressures, and refractory hypotension (MAP < 65 despite norepinephrine ≥ 0.25 mcg/kg/min).

Vasoplegia occurs in 8–25% of cardiac surgery patients and is especially common after OHT due to prolonged CPB, pre-existing inflammatory state, and acute complement activation.

**Management:**
- Norepinephrine: titrate to MAP > 65 (first-line)
- Vasopressin: add 0.03–0.04 units/min early to spare norepinephrine
- Angiotensin II: add for persistent vasoplegia (MAP < 65 on NE + vasopressin)
- **Methylene blue** 1.5–2 mg/kg IV over 20 min: inhibits soluble guanylate cyclase (the NO signaling pathway); effective in refractory vasoplegia; may repeat in 4–6 hours if needed
- Hydrocortisone 50–100 mg IV q8h: addresses relative adrenal insufficiency from pre-transplant chronic corticosteroid use
- Ensure calcium repletion — ionized calcium > 1.1 mmol/L; hypocalcemia markedly impairs vascular tone

> [!tip] Methylene Blue Timing
> Earlier use of methylene blue (within the first 4–6 hours) is more effective than late rescue. If MAP remains < 65 despite NE ≥ 0.5 mcg/kg/min + vasopressin, give methylene blue promptly.

## Mechanical Ventilation

### Initial Post-Operative Settings

| Parameter | Target |
|-----------|--------|
| Mode | Volume-controlled (AC/VC) |
| Tidal Volume | 6–8 mL/kg IBW |
| Respiratory Rate | 12–18 breaths/min |
| FiO₂ | Titrate to SpO₂ > 95% |
| PEEP | 5 cmH₂O (start low) |
| Plateau Pressure | < 30 cmH₂O |

> [!warning] PEEP Caution
> High PEEP (> 8–10 cmH₂O) increases RV afterload by compressing alveolar capillaries, and reduces RV preload. In the presence of RV dysfunction, minimize PEEP while maintaining acceptable oxygenation. PEEP also raises CVP, complicating PA catheter interpretation.

### Weaning Strategy
Target early extubation (median 6–12 hours post-transplant in uncomplicated cases). Prolonged mechanical ventilation increases infection risk, delays early ambulation, and is independently associated with worse outcomes.

**Extubation Criteria:**
- Hemodynamically stable (MAP > 65, CI > 2.2 on ≤ 2 inotropes/vasopressors at low-moderate doses)
- FiO₂ ≤ 0.40, PEEP ≤ 5, SpO₂ > 95%
- Adequate respiratory effort, GCS ≥ 14, follows commands
- Chest tube output < 100 mL/hr and not air-leak
- No ongoing active bleeding
- Passed SBT (PS/CPAP 5/5 × 30–60 min with acceptable parameters)

## Arrhythmia Management

Arrhythmias occur in up to 50% of OHT recipients and carry very different clinical implications depending on **type** and **timing**. This is not standard post-cardiotomy arrhythmia management — in the transplanted heart, the arrhythmia itself may be the presenting sign of acute rejection, and failing to recognize this distinction can be fatal.

### Key Points About the Denervated Heart
- **Atropine has NO effect** on the transplanted heart — there is no vagal innervation to block
- **Digoxin has NO chronotropic effect** — its indirect mechanism via the vagus nerve is absent; it retains mild inotropy but is rarely used
- **Isoproterenol and temporary epicardial pacing** are the agents of choice for bradycardia
- **Direct-acting agents** (catecholamines, adenosine) retain their effect because the effector receptors on the myocardium are intact
- **Reinnervation** is partial and occurs over months to years — not clinically relevant in the ICU phase

### Timing Is Everything: Early vs. Late Arrhythmias

The cause and clinical significance of arrhythmias shift dramatically with time from transplant:

| Timing | Primary Cause | Rejection Concern? |
|--------|--------------|-------------------|
| **< 2–3 weeks post-op** | Surgical trauma, suture line inflammation, ischemia-reperfusion, pericarditis, electrolytes | Low — but do not ignore |
| **> 3 weeks post-op** | **Rejection until proven otherwise** | **High — biopsy indicated** |
| **Late (months–years)** | Cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV), chronic rejection, reinnervation | High for ventricular arrhythmias |

> [!warning] Arrhythmia = Rejection Until Proven Otherwise (Beyond 3 Weeks)
> In the late post-transplant period, new-onset atrial flutter or atrial tachycardia should be treated as a potential sign of acute allograft rejection and is an indication for urgent endomyocardial biopsy. Multiple studies confirm 69–75% of post-transplant atrial flutter episodes beyond the early post-op period are temporally associated with acute rejection.

---

### Sinus Bradycardia / Junctional Rhythm

**Timing:** Immediate post-op (first 24–72 hours). Very common.

**Cause:** Denervation, SA node preservation injury, residual cardioplegia effect.

**Management:**
- Isoproterenol infusion 0.5–2 mcg/min — first-line; increases HR and has mild pulmonary vasodilatory effect
- Temporary epicardial pacing (atrial or AV sequential) — use if isoproterenol insufficient or not tolerated
- Target HR 90–110 bpm in the early period — every beat per minute matters when CO is borderline
- Does **not** respond to atropine

> [!tip] Epicardial Pacing Leads
> Nearly all OHT recipients arrive from the OR with temporary epicardial pacing wires. Confirm capture and sensing thresholds on arrival and check daily. Keep accessible for the first 5–7 days — loss of pacing capability in a bradycardic denervated heart is a preventable emergency.

---

### Atrial Flutter

**The most important arrhythmia in the post-transplant patient.**

**Early (< 3 weeks):** May be related to surgical inflammation, suture line irritation at the bicaval or biatrial anastomosis, or atrial distension. Treat hemodynamic instability first; consider rejection if clinically borderline.

**Late (> 3 weeks) or Recurrent:** **Strongly associated with acute rejection.** In multiple studies, atrial flutter occurring beyond the early post-op period was associated with biopsy-proven acute rejection in 69–75% of episodes, most of which were moderate-to-severe (ISHLT Grade 2R or 3R). Atrial flutter is the single arrhythmia most closely linked to rejection in heart transplant recipients.

> [!warning] Atrial Flutter Beyond 3 Weeks = Biopsy Indication
> Any new episode of atrial flutter after the first 3 weeks post-transplant should prompt urgent endomyocardial biopsy. Do not simply rate-control and discharge. If there is hemodynamic compromise, initiate empiric pulse steroids (methylprednisolone 500–1000 mg IV) while awaiting biopsy — do not wait for pathology to treat if the patient is deteriorating.

**Acute Management:**
- If hemodynamically unstable: DC cardioversion (synchronized)
- Amiodarone IV for rate/rhythm control (consult team before initiating)
- Atrial overdrive pacing (via epicardial wires) can terminate typical isthmus-dependent flutter — effective and avoids antiarrhythmic drug toxicity in the early post-op period
- Treat underlying rejection if confirmed — rhythm often resolves with successful rejection treatment

---

### Atrial Fibrillation

**Timing:** Can occur early or late; incidence 20–40% of OHT recipients.

**Early (< 3 weeks):** Primarily driven by surgical trauma, pericardial inflammation, atrial distension, catecholamine excess, and electrolyte disturbances. Less strongly associated with rejection than atrial flutter in this phase.

**Late or Recurrent:** Still warrants evaluation for rejection, particularly if new-onset or refractory. Less specific than atrial flutter but should not be dismissed.

**Management:**
- Correct precipitants: electrolytes (K⁺ > 4.0, Mg²⁺ > 2.0), volume status, pain, fever
- Rate control: amiodarone IV preferred in hemodynamically compromised patients; metoprolol cautiously if CO adequate
- Rhythm control: amiodarone (discuss with team); electrical cardioversion if hemodynamically unstable
- Anticoagulation: individualized — high bleeding risk early post-op; team decision
- If late or unexplained → evaluate for rejection (biopsy) and CAV

---

### Atrial Tachycardia (Non-Flutter, Non-Fibrillation)

Focal atrial tachycardia occurs in 9–15% of OHT recipients and is **highly associated with acute rejection**, particularly in the weeks to months following transplant. Case reports and series document resolution of atrial tachycardia with pulse steroid treatment for confirmed rejection.

**Management:**
- Any new atrial tachycardia beyond the immediate post-op period: evaluate for rejection (biopsy)
- Amiodarone for acute rate/rhythm control
- Treat underlying rejection if confirmed — arrhythmia frequently resolves

---

### Complete Heart Block / AV Block

**Cause:** Surgical injury to the AV node or conduction system at the time of transplant; more common with bicaval technique disruption.

**Management:**
- Temporary epicardial AV sequential pacing first-line
- Permanent pacemaker (PPM) required in approximately 5–10% of OHT recipients
- PPM implantation typically deferred 5–7 days to allow for potential recovery of conduction

---

### Ventricular Arrhythmias

**Early post-op:** Ventricular ectopy and non-sustained VT are common and usually benign — often related to electrolyte disturbances, ischemia-reperfusion injury, or inotrope use. Correct reversible causes.

**Late (months–years post-transplant):** New or sustained ventricular arrhythmias are more strongly associated with **cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV)** than acute cellular rejection — CAV causes diffuse ischemia that creates the substrate for re-entrant VT and increases sudden cardiac death risk. VT/VF in a late post-transplant patient should prompt urgent evaluation for CAV (coronary angiography ± IVUS) in addition to rejection (biopsy).

**Sustained VT/VF:** ACLS applies — defibrillate, amiodarone, assess for reversible causes. Note that the denervated heart may not exhibit the normal autonomic responses to VF/VT (no diaphoresis, no chest pain angina equivalent).

> [!warning] No Anginal Equivalent in the Denervated Heart
> Patients with CAV-related ischemia will not experience chest pain usually. Ventricular arrhythmias, unexplained dyspnea, or hemodynamic deterioration may be the only presentation of significant ischemia or infarction in the transplanted heart.

## Renal & Metabolic Management

### Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)
AKI occurs in 40–70% of OHT recipients in the first 30 days, and ~5–10% require acute renal replacement therapy (RRT). Pre-transplant exposure to calcineurin inhibitors (CNI), low CO, and intraoperative hypoperfusion are all contributing factors.

**Management:**
- Optimize hemodynamics — low CO is the most important modifiable factor
- Avoid nephrotoxins: NSAIDs, contrast agents, aminoglycosides, IV acyclovir (use valacyclovir/valganciclovir instead)
- Hold or delay CNI initiation if Cr > 2.5–3.0 mg/dL (institution-dependent); use basiliximab/rATG induction to bridge
- Use renal-dose diuretics cautiously; avoid volume overload
- Early nephrology consultation for AKI stage 2–3 or oliguria unresponsive to hemodynamic optimization
- Continuous RRT preferred in hemodynamically unstable patients; intermittent HD once stable

### Electrolyte Management

| Electrolyte | Target | Notes |
|-------------|--------|-------|
| Potassium | 4.0–5.0 mEq/L | Hypokalemia promotes arrhythmias; avoid > 5.5 (especially with CNI) |
| Magnesium | > 2.0 mg/dL | Repletion reduces arrhythmia risk; also important with CNI-induced wasting |
| Calcium (ionized) | 1.1–1.3 mmol/L | Hypocalcemia worsens vasoplegia; replete aggressively |
| Phosphorus | > 2.5 mg/dL | Hypophosphatemia impairs myocardial function and respiratory weaning |
| Glucose | 140–180 mg/dL | High-dose steroids drive hyperglycemia; insulin infusion per protocol |

### Glycemic Control
High-dose perioperative corticosteroids predictably cause hyperglycemia, often requiring insulin infusion. Target glucose 140–180 mg/dL per ICU protocol. Tight control (< 110 mg/dL) is associated with increased hypoglycemia and does not improve outcomes.

## Coagulation & Bleeding

Post-operative bleeding is common following OHT, particularly in patients with:
- Prior sternotomy (redo surgery)
- Explanted LVAD (extensive adhesions, coagulopathy from device-related thrombus/anticoagulation)
- Pre-transplant warfarin/anticoagulation
- Pre-existing hepatic dysfunction (impaired clotting factor synthesis)

### Goals
- Chest tube output < 100 mL/hr after first hour; < 50 mL/hr by 4–6 hours
- Hemoglobin ≥ 8 g/dL (higher threshold, 9–10, if ongoing hemodynamic instability)
- INR < 1.5, fibrinogen > 200 mg/dL, platelets > 75,000

### Viscoelastic Testing (TEG/ROTEM)
Use **TEG or ROTEM** to guide targeted hemostatic resuscitation rather than empiric factor replacement. TEG/ROTEM differentiates clot formation defects, fibrinolysis, and platelet dysfunction.

| TEG Finding | Intervention |
|-------------|-------------|
| Prolonged R-time (clot formation ↓) | FFP |
| Reduced alpha-angle (fibrin polymerization ↓) | Cryoprecipitate |
| Reduced MA (platelet dysfunction) | Platelets |
| LY30 > 7.5% (fibrinolysis) | TXA (if not already given) |

> [!warning] Reoperation Threshold
> If chest tube output > 200 mL/hr × 2 hours despite correction of coagulopathy, or hemodynamic instability from suspected tamponade — emergent return to OR. Low threshold in redo-sternotomy or LVAD explant cases.

## Neurologic Monitoring

### Delirium
Delirium incidence is high (30–50%) in the early post-transplant period due to:
- Pre-existing cognitive vulnerability (chronic low CO, sleep deprivation, ICU exposure)
- Metabolic derangements (uremia, hyperglycemia, electrolyte abnormalities)
- CNI neurotoxicity (tacrolimus: tremors, encephalopathy, PRES)
- Opioid/benzodiazepine effects

**Prevention:** Minimize benzodiazepines (use propofol or dexmedetomidine for sedation), early liberation from mechanical ventilation, sleep hygiene protocols, early mobilization, CAM-ICU screening every shift.

### CNI Neurotoxicity / PRES
**Tacrolimus** can cause tremors, seizures, and **posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES)** — particularly with supratherapeutic levels, hypertension, or rapid dose escalation.

- Screen: new-onset tremor, headache, visual disturbances, altered mental status, seizures
- Diagnosis: MRI brain (FLAIR hyperintensities in posterior white matter)
- Management: Reduce or hold tacrolimus; substitute with basiliximab induction if needed; control blood pressure aggressively

## Nutritional Support

### Targets

| Parameter | Goal |
|-----------|------|
| Calories | 25–30 kcal/kg/day |
| Protein | 1.5–2.5 g·kg⁻¹·day⁻¹ |
| Route | Enteral preferred over parenteral |

**Strategy:**
- Initiate enteral nutrition within 24–48 hours of extubation or via nasoenteric tube if ventilated > 48 hours
- High-dose steroids increase protein catabolism; adequate protein intake is critical for wound healing and immune function
- Monitor glucose closely — insulin protocol per ICU standards
- Replete micronutrients: zinc, selenium, vitamins (particularly C and D)

## Extubation & ICU Liberation

**Criteria for Safe Extubation:**

| Criterion | Target |
|-----------|--------|
| Hemodynamics | MAP ≥ 65 mmHg, CI ≥ 2.2 on ≤ moderate inotrope support |
| Oxygenation | P/F ratio ≥ 250, FiO₂ ≤ 0.40, PEEP ≤ 5 |
| Ventilation | Respiratory rate < 30, adequate effort, no respiratory acidosis |
| Neurologic | Alert, follows commands, airway protective reflexes intact |
| Bleeding | Chest tube output < 50 mL/hr, coagulopathy corrected |
| Metabolic | Lactate < 2.0, pH > 7.30 |

**SBT:** CPAP 5 cmH₂O + PS 5 cmH₂O × 30–60 minutes with respiratory rate < 30, SpO₂ > 92%, HR and BP stable.

**ICU Discharge Criteria:**
- Extubated and maintaining adequate oxygenation on nasal cannula
- Off mechanical support (IABP, temporary RVAD, ECMO)
- Hemodynamically stable on oral medications or no more than 1 low-dose inotrope/vasopressor weaning
- Oral immunosuppression initiated and therapeutic drug monitoring established
- Renal function acceptable (not requiring inpatient RRT)
- No active infection requiring IV antibiotics that cannot be managed on step-down unit
- Team confidence in patient's clinical trajectory

## Mechanical Circulatory Support (MCS)

### Escalation Ladder in PGD / RV Failure

| Severity                               | Device                             | Indication                                                                                             |
| -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Moderate PGD                           | **IABP**                           | LV support, augments coronary perfusion; simple to place; limited RV benefit                           |
| Severe PGD — LV                        | **Impella CP/5.5**                 | Direct LV unloading; bridges to recovery or more durable support                                       |
| Severe RV failure                      | **Impella RP / Protek Duo**        | Percutaneous RVAD; high flow PA-to-RA bypass                                                           |
| Biventricular PGD                      | **VA-ECMO**                        | Complete cardiopulmonary support; highest-acuity device; standard of care for severe biventricular PGD |
| Bridge to transplant retransplantation | **BiVAD / Total Artificial Heart** | Rare; for irreversible PGD with no recovery                                                            |

### VA-ECMO in PGD

**Indications for VA-ECMO:**
- CI < 1.8 L/min/m² on ≥ 2 inotropes/vasopressors
- Severe biventricular PGD (severe PGD LV or PGD RV criteria met)
- Refractory hemodynamic instability with rising lactate despite pharmacologic optimization
- Cardiac arrest in the setting of PGD

**Targets on VA-ECMO:**
- ECMO flow: 2.0–2.5 L/min/m² (titrate to maintain CI)
- LV vent strategy: monitor PCWP; if > 18–20 mmHg or LV distension on echo → add Impella for LV unloading ("ECMELLA")
- Monitor end-organ recovery: lactate clearance, urine output, liver function
- Serial echo every 12–24 hours to assess for native cardiac recovery

**Weaning from VA-ECMO:**
- Trial flow reduction (typically by 0.5 L/min steps)
- Assess hemodynamics and echo at reduced flows before decannulation
- Most post-OHT PGD VA-ECMO support runs 3–7 days

> [!tip] ECMELLA in PGD
> In severe PGD with LV distension on VA-ECMO (PCWP > 18 or LA/LV dilation on echo), adding an Impella (ECMELLA configuration) offloads the LV, reduces wall stress, and may accelerate myocardial recovery. Consider early if PCWP is elevated on ECMO.

## Clinical Pearls

> [!tip] Pearl 1 — The Denervated Heart Cannot Tell You It's Ischemic
> The transplanted heart has no anginal symptoms. Graft ischemia and acute rejection are clinically silent until hemodynamic compromise develops. This is why serial echo, daily ECG (ST changes), and regular PA catheter assessment are non-negotiable. In any unexplained hemodynamic deterioration, put rejection on the differential and proceed to endomyocardial biopsy.

> [!tip] Pearl 2 — Isoproterenol is Your Heart Rate Agent
> Atropine and digoxin do not work in the denervated heart. If the patient is bradycardic and hemodynamically compromised, reach for isoproterenol (0.5–2 mcg/min IV) or use the epicardial pacing wires. Every beat per minute matters when CO is borderline.

> [!tip] Pearl 3 — CVP Rising + PCWP Normal = Isolated RV Failure
> This PA catheter pattern is pathognomonic for isolated RV failure. Do not volume-load further. Start iNO + milrinone immediately, maintain norepinephrine for coronary perfusion pressure, and call for echo.

> [!tip] Pearl 4 — Ischemic Time Is the Enemy
> Every additional hour of ischemic time increases PGD risk substantially. Ischemic time > 6 hours should raise your clinical vigilance for PGD from the moment the patient arrives. Have ECMO at bedside ready to deploy if hemodynamics are tenuous from the start.

> [!tip] Pearl 5 — Methylene Blue Works — Use It Early
> Vasoplegic syndrome post-CPB responds to methylene blue via NO-cGMP pathway inhibition. Studies show better efficacy when given early (within first 6 hours) rather than as a late rescue. If you need ≥ 2 vasopressors within 2–4 hours of arrival, consider methylene blue.

> [!tip] Pearl 6 — Watch Your Diuretics in RV Failure
> Aggressive diuresis in isolated RV failure is often counterproductive early. The RV is preload-dependent during recovery; too little preload starves an already-struggling RV. Gentle diuresis (targeting CVP 8–12) is appropriate; flood or dry will both hurt you.

> [!tip] Pearl 7 — Routine Biopsy Timing
> Endomyocardial biopsy for rejection surveillance typically begins at 1 week post-transplant. Any unexplained hemodynamic deterioration before scheduled biopsy should prompt early biopsy. Acute cellular rejection (ACR) and antibody-mediated rejection (AMR) both require specific treatment and can be clinically silent until late.

## Evidence & Landmark Trials

### Landmark Trials & Pivotal Studies

**1. ISHLT Consensus Statement on PGD (Kobashigawa et al., 2014)**
- Established the current ISHLT consensus definition and grading of PGD (LV and RV), which unified prior disparate definitions and enabled international comparative research. Remains the operational definition in use today.
- *J Heart Lung Transplant.* 2014;33(4):327–340.

**2. ISHLT Registy Annual Data Reports (1990–present)**
- Annual reporting of OHT outcomes from 500+ centers worldwide. The 2023 report documents > 4,000 annual transplants with 1-year survival ~86%, median survival 13+ years, and PGD as the leading cause of 30-day mortality.
- *J Heart Lung Transplant.* Annual publication.

**3. Marasco et al. — VA-ECMO for Severe PGD (2010)**
- Retrospective multicenter review demonstrating VA-ECMO as a viable and potentially life-saving bridge in severe PGD, with survival to hospital discharge in ~50–60% of patients requiring ECMO support post-OHT.
- *J Heart Lung Transplant.* 2010;29(1):63–69.

**4. Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) Registry — ECMO Post-Transplant**
- Large registry analysis confirming VA-ECMO as the most common form of MCS for PGD, with 30-day survival ~50% and best outcomes in centers with high-volume ECMO experience.

**5. Lund et al. — ISHLT Registry: Ischemic Time and PGD Risk**
- Registry data demonstrating stepwise increase in PGD and 30-day mortality with ischemic times > 4 hours, and significant mortality jump at > 6 hours — supporting clinical urgency in minimizing cold ischemic time.

### Recent PubMed Evidence (2020–2025)

Searches were performed using PubMed with queries targeting heart transplant ICU management, primary graft dysfunction, right ventricular failure, and mechanical circulatory support. Key PMIDs identified include: 38762216, 32205957, 30850154, 32150037, 28543813.

**Redefining PGD: Toward a Consensus in the New Era of Heart Transplantation (2024–2025)**
- Emerging literature has proposed updated PGD classification accounting for DCD donors and newer mechanical support devices, with recognition that the 2014 definition needs modernization for contemporary practice. Consensus publication expected.
- *PMC12011430*

**Right Ventricular Failure Post-Heart Transplant: Management with Inhaled Nitric Oxide**
- Multiple observational studies confirm that inhaled NO (20–40 ppm) reduces PVR and RV afterload, improves CI, and reduces need for RVAD in patients with pre-transplant pulmonary hypertension. iNO is now considered standard of care for RV failure post-OHT.
- *PMID: 28543813*

**Optimizing Outcomes: Multidisciplinary Heart Teams and MCS for PGD**
- Recent European Heart Journal Supplement review demonstrates that multidisciplinary transplant-ECMO team approach with early ECMO deployment improves outcomes in severe PGD compared to delayed escalation.
- *Eur Heart J Suppl.* 2025;27(Supplement 4):iv55.

## Key Guidelines

**1. ISHLT Guidelines for the Care of Heart Transplant Recipients (2023)**
*J Heart Lung Transplant.* 2023;42(5):e1–e141. PMID: 37080658

The comprehensive 2023 update (replacing the 2010 edition) provides Class I/IIa/IIb recommendations across all aspects of post-transplant care. Key ICU-relevant recommendations include:
- **Pulmonary artery catheter** for all OHT recipients in the immediate post-operative period (Class I)
- **Serial echocardiography** for biventricular assessment and PGD grading (Class I)
- **Inhaled NO** as first-line therapy for RV failure and elevated PVR post-OHT (Class I)
- **VA-ECMO** for severe PGD unresponsive to pharmacologic optimization (Class IIa)
- **Temporary pacing** to maintain HR 90–110 bpm in the denervated heart (Class I)
- Avoidance of atropine for bradycardia in the denervated heart (Class III — harm)

**2. ISHLT Guidelines for Evaluation and Care of Cardiac Transplant Candidates (2024)**
*J Heart Lung Transplant.* 2024. PMID: 39115488

Provides updated recipient selection criteria, pre-transplant workup, and fixed vs. reversible PVR assessment — directly informing post-operative risk stratification.

**3. ISHLT 2023 Guidelines for Mechanical Circulatory Support**
*J Heart Lung Transplant.* 2023. PMID: 37245143

Provides evidence-based recommendations for ECMO and temporary VAD use in the context of post-transplant PGD and cardiogenic shock.

**4. ISHLT Consensus on Primary Graft Dysfunction (2014)**
*J Heart Lung Transplant.* 2014;33(4):327–340

Foundational PGD definition and grading criteria still in active clinical use.
