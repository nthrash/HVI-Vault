# Heart Transplant Immunosuppression & Antimicrobial Regimens
### Post-Transplant Protocol Reference

## Overview

Immunosuppression is the cornerstone of long-term survival after orthotopic heart transplantation (OHT). The goal is to prevent allograft rejection while minimizing the inevitable infectious, oncologic, metabolic, and nephrotoxic consequences of immune suppression. Getting this balance right is one of the most complex ongoing clinical challenges in transplant medicine — too little risks acute cellular rejection (ACR) or antibody-mediated rejection (AMR); too much leads to opportunistic infection, malignancy, and end-organ toxicity.

The typical OHT immunosuppression strategy involves three phases: **induction** (perioperative), **early maintenance** (first 3–6 months), and **long-term maintenance** (individualized to rejection/infection history). This document also covers the antimicrobial prophylaxis regimens that are inseparable from the immunosuppression strategy — because the net state of immunosuppression determines infection risk, and prophylaxis must be matched accordingly.

> [!tip] Why This Matters
> Acute rejection remains a leading cause of late graft failure (after the first year). CMV infection is the most common serious post-transplant infection and also acts as an immune modulatory virus that increases rejection risk. Understanding both the immunosuppression and antimicrobial regimens is essential for any provider caring for these patients.

## Pathophysiology of Rejection

Understanding rejection mechanisms guides both drug selection and clinical interpretation of endomyocardial biopsy results.

**Acute Cellular Rejection (ACR):** T-lymphocyte mediated attack on donor myocytes. CD4+ and CD8+ T cells are activated when they recognize donor MHC antigens (either directly on donor APCs or indirectly via recipient APCs). ACR is most common in the first 3–6 months, with highest risk in weeks 2–8. Graded on biopsy using the ISHLT 2004 scale (Grades 0, 1R, 2R, 3R). Grade 2R and above requires treatment.

**Antibody-Mediated Rejection (AMR):** Donor-specific antibodies (DSA) against HLA antigens activate complement and endothelial cells, causing microvascular injury. AMR is more difficult to treat, associated with worse outcomes, and increasingly recognized as a major cause of late graft failure and cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV). Diagnosis requires histopathology (capillary injury, macrophage infiltrates) + C4d deposition + circulating DSA.

**Chronic Rejection / Cardiac Allograft Vasculopathy (CAV):** Diffuse concentric intimal proliferation throughout the coronary tree, driven by both immune and non-immune mechanisms. CAV is the leading cause of death beyond 5 years post-OHT. Immunosuppression modification (particularly mTOR inhibitors) may slow progression.

**Hyperacute Rejection:** Preformed antibodies cause immediate graft failure at reperfusion. Prevented by crossmatch prior to transplantation; exceedingly rare in modern practice.

## Induction Immunosuppression

**Induction** refers to high-intensity immunosuppression given perioperatively (before and immediately after transplantation) to prevent early acute rejection during the period of peak alloimmune activation. It also allows delay of calcineurin inhibitor initiation, which is valuable when renal function is at highest risk.

### Induction Agents

| Agent | Class | Mechanism | Typical Dosing | Indication |
|-------|-------|-----------|----------------|-----------|
| **Basiliximab (Simulect)** | IL-2 receptor antagonist (anti-CD25) | Blocks IL-2 mediated T-cell clonal expansion | 20 mg IV pre-op, repeat day 4 | Standard induction; low toxicity; preferred when renal function is compromised |
| **Rabbit antithymocyte globulin (rATG / Thymoglobulin)** | Polyclonal antibody (anti-T cell) | Depletes T-lymphocytes; broad immunosuppression | 1.5 mg/kg/day IV × 3–5 days | Higher-risk recipients (high PRA/DSA, sensitized patients, renal failure at transplant, hemodynamic instability preventing early CNI); more potent than basiliximab |
| **Methylprednisolone** | Corticosteroid | Broad anti-inflammatory; inhibits cytokine transcription | 500–1000 mg IV intraoperatively | Universal; given to all recipients at time of transplant |

> [!tip] Basiliximab vs. rATG
> Basiliximab is lower-risk, well tolerated, and preferred when the primary goal is CNI delay for renal protection. rATG is reserved for highly sensitized patients (high PRA), those with hemodynamic instability precluding early tacrolimus, or centers that use it universally. rATG carries greater infection risk due to deep T-cell depletion.

### Practical Induction Protocol (typical center)

1. **Methylprednisolone** 500–1000 mg IV given intraoperatively at time of reperfusion
2. **Basiliximab** 20 mg IV given within 2 hours before transplant (or within 6 hours post-transplant); repeat dose on day 4
3. Continue high-dose **methylprednisolone** (1 mg/kg/day IV or per protocol) in the immediate post-op period, then taper
4. Initiate **tacrolimus** once:
   - Renal function acceptable (Cr < 2.5–3.0 mg/dL at most centers, institution-dependent)
   - Hemodynamically stable
   - Oral/NGT route feasible
5. Initiate **mycophenolate mofetil** (MMF) or mycophenolic acid (MPA) on post-op day 1–2 if tolerating

## Maintenance Immunosuppression — Triple Therapy

The standard of care for post-OHT maintenance immunosuppression is **triple therapy**:

1. **Calcineurin inhibitor (CNI)** — tacrolimus (preferred) or cyclosporine
2. **Cell-cycle inhibitor / antiproliferative** — mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) or mycophenolic acid (MPA)
3. **Corticosteroid** — prednisone

This three-drug combination provides synergistic immunosuppression targeting different steps in T-cell activation, allowing lower doses of each agent and reducing toxicity from any single drug.

---

### Drug 1 — Calcineurin Inhibitor (CNI)

**Mechanism:** Binds FK-binding protein (tacrolimus) or cyclophilin (cyclosporine), forming complexes that inhibit calcineurin phosphatase → prevents NFAT dephosphorylation → blocks IL-2 gene transcription → T-cell clonal expansion inhibited.

#### Tacrolimus (Prograf, Astagraf, Envarsus)

| Parameter | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **Route** | PO (preferred); IV if NPO (continuous infusion; 1/3 to 1/4 oral dose) |
| **Initiation** | Post-op day 1–3; delay if Cr > 2.5–3.0 |
| **Starting dose** | 0.05–0.1 mg/kg/day PO divided BID |
| **Monitoring** | 12-hour trough level (drawn before AM dose) |
| **Early target trough (0–3 months)** | 10–15 ng/mL |
| **Mid target (3–12 months)** | 8–12 ng/mL |
| **Late target (> 12 months)** | 5–10 ng/mL |
| **Key toxicities** | Nephrotoxicity, hypertension, neurotoxicity (tremors, PRES), hyperglycemia (new-onset diabetes), hypomagnesemia, hyperlipidemia, GI intolerance |
| **Formulations** | IR BID (Prograf), ER once-daily (Astagraf XL, Envarsus XR — lower peak levels, may improve tolerability) |

> [!warning] Tacrolimus Drug Interactions
> Tacrolimus is metabolized by CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein. **Major interactions:**
> - **Azole antifungals** (fluconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole): dramatically increase levels → toxicity
> - **Macrolides** (clarithromycin, erythromycin): increase levels
> - **Rifampin**: dramatically decreases levels → rejection risk
> - **Grapefruit juice**: increases levels unpredictably
> Always check levels within 48–72 hours after adding or stopping any interacting medication.

#### Cyclosporine (Neoral, Sandimmune, Gengraf)

Largely replaced by tacrolimus at most US centers due to inferior rejection prevention in meta-analyses. However, some patients are maintained on cyclosporine long-term if tacrolimus intolerant (significant tremors, neurotoxicity, new-onset PTDM).

| Parameter | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **Monitoring** | 12-hour trough (C0) or 2-hour post-dose level (C2) |
| **Target trough (early)** | 200–350 ng/mL |
| **Key toxicities** | Nephrotoxicity, hypertension, hirsutism, gingival hyperplasia, hyperlipidemia |

---

### Drug 2 — Antiproliferative / Cell-Cycle Inhibitor

#### Mycophenolate Mofetil (MMF / CellCept) or Mycophenolic Acid (MPA / Myfortic)

**Mechanism:** MMF is a prodrug converted to mycophenolic acid (MPA), which inhibits inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase (IMPDH) — the rate-limiting enzyme in de novo purine synthesis. Lymphocytes uniquely depend on this pathway (unlike most cells which use the salvage pathway), providing selective antiproliferative activity against T and B cells.

| Parameter | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **MMF dose** | 1,000–1,500 mg PO BID (total 2–3 g/day) |
| **MPA dose** | 720–1,080 mg PO BID (EC-MPA; equivalent to MMF 1g–1.5g BID) |
| **Monitoring** | Typically clinical (CBC); MPA levels (AUC) used at some centers for dose optimization |
| **Target WBC** | > 3,000/µL; dose-reduce if WBC < 2,500 or ANC < 1,300 |
| **Key toxicities** | GI (diarrhea, nausea, cramping — major cause of non-adherence), leukopenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia |
| **Tip** | If GI intolerance to MMF → switch to EC-MPA (Myfortic); enteric-coated formulation significantly reduces GI side effects |

#### Azathioprine (Imuran) — Alternative

Older agent; largely replaced by MMF. Reserved for MMF intolerance. Less potent antiproliferative effect. Dose: 1–2 mg/kg/day. Key toxicity: myelosuppression (severe; check TPMT enzyme activity before use).

#### mTOR Inhibitors (Everolimus / Sirolimus) — Substitution Strategy

**Mechanism:** Inhibits mTOR → blocks T and B cell proliferation AND has antiproliferative effects on vascular smooth muscle cells. This dual mechanism makes mTOR inhibitors particularly valuable for **cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV) prevention**.

| Agent | Dose | Target Level | Key Notes |
|-------|------|-------------|-----------|
| **Everolimus (Zortress)** | 0.75–1.5 mg PO BID | 3–8 ng/mL | Used in combination with reduced-dose CNI |
| **Sirolimus (Rapamune)** | 1–3 mg PO daily | 5–10 ng/mL | Weekly loading, then daily maintenance |

**Indications for mTOR inhibitor substitution:**
- Progression of cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV) — mTOR inhibitors significantly slow CAV progression
- CNI-associated nephrotoxicity — switching to mTOR allows CNI dose reduction or elimination
- CMV disease — mTOR inhibitors have antiviral properties; reduces CMV replication
- De novo or progressive malignancy post-transplant (particularly skin cancers, PTLD)

**Contraindications:** Recent surgery or open wounds (impairs wound healing), active pulmonary infiltrates/fibrosis, severe proteinuria, eGFR < 40 mL/min (used with caution).

> [!warning] mTOR and Wound Healing
> Do not initiate mTOR inhibitors within 4–6 weeks of any major surgery. Sirolimus and everolimus significantly impair wound healing and are associated with wound dehiscence, lymphocele, and delayed sternal healing if initiated too early post-OHT.

---

### Drug 3 — Corticosteroids

**Mechanism:** Broad anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects through glucocorticoid receptor-mediated inhibition of NF-κB, AP-1 → reduced transcription of IL-1, IL-2, IL-6, TNF-α, and other cytokines. Also inhibit antigen presentation and T-cell activation.

| Phase | Agent | Dose |
|-------|-------|------|
| **Intraoperative** | Methylprednisolone | 500–1000 mg IV |
| **Early post-op (24–48h)** | Methylprednisolone | 125 mg IV q8h × 3 doses |
| **Days 2–7** | Prednisone | 1 mg/kg/day PO (max 100 mg) |
| **Weeks 2–4** | Prednisone | Taper to 0.5 mg/kg/day |
| **Months 1–6** | Prednisone | Taper toward 0.1 mg/kg/day |
| **Maintenance** | Prednisone | 5–10 mg/day (or attempt steroid withdrawal at select centers) |

**Steroid Withdrawal:** Select low-risk patients (no prior rejection, low PRA, excellent graft function) at 12–24 months may undergo steroid weaning. Not universally attempted; institutional protocols vary significantly. Higher rejection risk if withdrawn.

**Key Toxicities:** Hyperglycemia (post-transplant diabetes mellitus — PTDM), hypertension, dyslipidemia, osteopenia/osteoporosis, adrenal suppression, wound healing impairment, cushingoid features, cataracts, peptic ulcer disease.

## Acute Rejection — Recognition & Treatment

### Endomyocardial Biopsy Schedule (ISHLT Standard)

| Timing | Frequency |
|--------|-----------|
| Weeks 1–6 | Weekly |
| Weeks 7–12 | Every 2 weeks |
| Months 3–6 | Monthly |
| Months 6–12 | Every 3 months |
| Year 2 | Every 6 months |
| Year > 2 | Annually |

Biopsy schedule modified based on clinical stability, rejection history, and institutional protocol.

### Acute Cellular Rejection (ACR) — ISHLT Grading & Treatment

| Grade | Histology | Treatment |
|-------|-----------|-----------|
| **0R** | No rejection | No change |
| **1R (Mild)** | Focal/diffuse lymphocytic infiltrate, no myocyte damage | If hemodynamically stable: optimize CNI levels; close follow-up; some centers treat |
| **2R (Moderate)** | Multifocal aggressive infiltrate ± myocyte damage | **Pulse steroids** (see below) |
| **3R (Severe)** | Diffuse infiltrate with myocyte necrosis, ± edema, hemorrhage | **Pulse steroids + rATG/OKT3 (if available)**; escalate to hemodynamic support as needed |

**Pulse Steroid Treatment (ACR 2R/3R):**
- Methylprednisolone 500–1000 mg IV q24h × 3 days
- Followed by prednisone 1 mg/kg/day PO with taper over 2–4 weeks
- Repeat biopsy in 4–6 weeks to confirm resolution

**Hemodynamically Compromising Rejection:**
- Methylprednisolone + rATG (1.5 mg/kg/day × 3–5 days)
- Consider plasmapheresis/IVIG if AMR also suspected
- Hemodynamic support with inotropes, MCS if needed
- Early transplant team involvement mandatory

### Antibody-Mediated Rejection (AMR)

More complex and less well-standardized treatment. AMR requires simultaneous depletion of preformed antibodies, B-cell suppression, and sometimes complement inhibition.

| Treatment | Agent | Rationale |
|-----------|-------|-----------|
| Plasmapheresis | Physical removal of DSA | Rapidly reduces circulating antibody |
| IVIG | 1–2 g/kg total | Anti-idiotype effects; immunomodulation |
| Rituximab | 375 mg/m² × 1–4 doses | Anti-CD20 B-cell depletion |
| Bortezomib | 1.3 mg/m² × 4 doses | Plasma cell depletion |
| Carfilzomib | 20–36 mg/m² | Novel proteasome inhibitor; emerging role |
| Complement inhibition | Eculizumab (off-label) | Anti-C5; reduces complement-mediated injury |
| Pulse steroids | Standard | Adjunct anti-inflammatory |

> [!warning] AMR and DSA Monitoring
> Circulating DSA should be checked in any patient with unexplained hemodynamic deterioration or positive biopsy findings suggestive of microvascular injury. Persistent DSA despite treatment is associated with progressive CAV and graft loss.

## Immunosuppression in Special Situations

### Renal Insufficiency / AKI Post-Transplant
- **Delay CNI initiation** if Cr > 2.5–3.0 mg/dL post-operatively; bridge with basiliximab second dose or rATG
- **Reduce CNI trough targets** (lower end of range) once initiated
- **Consider mTOR inhibitor + reduced-dose CNI** combination at 6–12 months if CNI nephrotoxicity is progressive
- Early nephrology involvement

### Malignancy (Post-Transplant Lymphoproliferative Disorder — PTLD)
- **Reduce overall immunosuppression** immediately (reduce CNI by 25–50%, reduce/hold MMF)
- **Switch to mTOR inhibitor** (sirolimus/everolimus) — antiproliferative effect against B-cell lymphomas
- Rituximab ± chemotherapy depending on PTLD subtype/stage
- Oncology involvement essential

### Pregnancy Post-Transplant
- Tacrolimus: generally continued (Category C, teratogenic in high doses but widely used)
- MMF/MPA: **CONTRAINDICATED in pregnancy** (teratogenic — Category D; discontinue and substitute azathioprine)
- Prednisone: compatible with pregnancy at low doses
- Switch MMF → azathioprine at least 3–6 months before planned conception

### Infection Requiring Antibiotic Therapy
- **Monitor tacrolimus levels closely** whenever adding or stopping any CYP3A4-interacting antibiotic
- Azole antifungals (treatment doses) will dramatically elevate CNI levels → reduce dose preemptively by 50% and recheck level in 48–72h
- Clarithromycin: significant CNI level elevation — prefer azithromycin when possible

## Antimicrobial Prophylaxis — Bacterial

### Perioperative Surgical Prophylaxis

Per the 2023 ISHLT guidelines and standard cardiothoracic surgery principles:

- **First-line:** Cefazolin 2 g IV (3 g if weight > 120 kg) within 60 minutes before skin incision; repeat every 4 hours during procedure
- **MRSA risk (prior MRSA colonization, prolonged hospitalization):** Add vancomycin 15–20 mg/kg IV within 120 minutes of incision (use in addition to, not instead of, beta-lactam)
- **Beta-lactam allergy:** Vancomycin + aztreonam, or clindamycin alone (limited gram-negative coverage)
- Duration: **24 hours post-operatively** (no benefit to continuing > 24–48h for prophylaxis alone per STS guidelines)

### Donor-Transmitted Bacteremia
- Hearts can be transplanted from bacteremic donors provided the donor has received ≥ 24–48 hours of **targeted antimicrobial therapy** against the identified organism
- Recipients of hearts from bacteremic donors should receive an **appropriate course of antimicrobial therapy** targeting the donor isolate (typically 7–14 days)
- Obtain blood cultures from recipient on arrival to ICU and at 48–72 hours

### Post-Operative Bacterial Prophylaxis (Extended)

Most centers do not continue prophylactic antibiotics beyond 24–48 hours for uncomplicated cases. However:
- **Central line-associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI) prevention:** Standard bundle (chlorhexidine skin care, dressing protocols, early line removal)
- **VAP prevention:** Head of bed elevation, oral care, minimize sedation, early extubation
- In patients with prior MDR colonization (MRSA, VRE, ESBL, CRE): individualized prophylaxis strategy in consultation with infectious disease

## Antiviral Prophylaxis — CMV

**CMV is the most clinically important viral pathogen after heart transplantation.** CMV infection occurs in 25–75% of seropositive recipients (D+ or R+) without prophylaxis, causing tissue-invasive disease, graft dysfunction, and indirect effects including increased rejection risk, opportunistic infections, and accelerated CAV.

### CMV Serostatus Risk Stratification

| Donor/Recipient | Risk Level | Strategy |
|----------------|------------|---------|
| **D+/R–** | **Highest risk** | Universal prophylaxis × 6–12 months |
| **D+/R+** | High | Universal prophylaxis × 3–6 months |
| **D–/R+** | Intermediate | Universal prophylaxis × 3–6 months |
| **D–/R–** | Very low | Prophylaxis not routinely required; some centers use acyclovir |

### Preferred Prophylactic Agents

| Agent | Route | Dose | Notes |
|-------|-------|------|-------|
| **Valganciclovir (Valcyte)** | PO | 900 mg daily (renal dose adjust) | Drug of choice for all CMV seropositive situations; excellent bioavailability; equal efficacy to IV ganciclovir for prophylaxis |
| **IV Ganciclovir** | IV | 5 mg/kg/day (renally adjusted) | Used when patient is NPO post-operatively; transition to valganciclovir once PO feasible |
| **Acyclovir** | PO | 400–800 mg BID–TID | For D–/R– (HSV/VZV prophylaxis only; no CMV coverage) |

### Duration of Prophylaxis

| Risk | Recommended Duration |
|------|---------------------|
| D+/R– (highest risk) | **12 months** (ISHLT 2023 recommends up to 12 months for this group) |
| D+/R+ or D–/R+ | **6 months** |

> [!tip] The Case for 12 Months in D+/R–
> The 2023 ISHLT guidelines recommend considering 12 months of valganciclovir prophylaxis in the highest-risk group (D+/R–), as late-onset CMV disease (after 3–6 months of prophylaxis) was previously common. Studies confirm reduced late-onset CMV with extended prophylaxis.

### Monitoring During Prophylaxis
- **CMV viral load** (PCR): check at 1–3 month intervals during prophylaxis
- If viral load detectable during prophylaxis → assess for breakthrough disease vs. breakthrough viremia without symptoms
- **CBC weekly** for first month, then monthly — valganciclovir causes dose-dependent myelosuppression (leukopenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia)

### Renal Dose Adjustments for Valganciclovir

| CrCl (mL/min) | Prophylactic Dose |
|---------------|-----------------|
| ≥ 60 | 900 mg daily |
| 40–59 | 450 mg daily |
| 25–39 | 450 mg every 2 days |
| 10–24 | 200 mg every 2 days |
| < 10 / Dialysis | **Avoid; consult ID** |

### CMV Disease Treatment (Breakthrough or Post-Prophylaxis)
- **Valganciclovir** 900 mg PO BID × 3–6 weeks (mild–moderate disease)
- **IV Ganciclovir** 5 mg/kg IV BID × minimum 2–3 weeks (severe/organ-invasive disease)
- Reduction in immunosuppression (hold MMF) during treatment — weigh against rejection risk
- Resistance testing if inadequate response at 2 weeks (UL97 mutation → foscarnet or cidofovir needed; consult ID)

## Antiviral Prophylaxis — Herpes / EBV / Other

### Herpes Simplex (HSV) & Varicella-Zoster (VZV)
- All seropositive patients (HSV-1, HSV-2, VZV) should receive **acyclovir or valacyclovir** prophylaxis during the period of highest immunosuppression
- **Acyclovir** 400 mg PO BID (or valacyclovir 500 mg PO daily) — continue for the first 3–6 months
- Patients on valganciclovir (CMV prophylaxis) have incidental coverage for HSV/VZV — separate prophylaxis not required during this period
- VZV-seronegative recipients: vaccination pre-transplant if possible (at least 4 weeks before); post-transplant live vaccines are **contraindicated**
- VZV exposure in seronegative recipient: VZIG within 96 hours; acyclovir/valacyclovir if VZIG unavailable

### Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) and PTLD
- **EBV D+/R–** (donor seropositive, recipient seronegative) is the highest-risk combination for post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD)
- No proven effective prophylactic antiviral for EBV post-transplant; preventive strategy relies on **monitoring EBV viral load** monthly for the first year and reducing immunosuppression at first sign of rising viral load
- Valganciclovir has partial in vitro activity against EBV but no proven prophylactic efficacy for PTLD

### BK Virus (BKV)
- Primarily affects kidney allografts and immunosuppressed patients with renal dysfunction
- In heart transplant recipients, BKV causes BK nephropathy in co-morbid renal dysfunction; hemorrhagic cystitis is uncommon
- Screening: urine BK PCR if Cr rising without other explanation; blood BK PCR if urine positive
- Management: **Reduce immunosuppression** (CNI reduction is the primary strategy)

## PCP & Toxoplasma Prophylaxis

### Pneumocystis jirovecii Pneumonia (PCP)

PCP is a life-threatening opportunistic infection in transplant recipients with an untreated incidence of up to 15–20% in the first year. Universal prophylaxis reduces this risk to < 1%.

**Preferred Agent:**
- **Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX / Bactrim)**
  - Dose: SS tablet (80/400 mg) PO daily **OR** DS tablet (160/800 mg) PO Monday/Wednesday/Friday (3×/week)
  - Efficacy: > 95% effective for PCP prevention; also provides simultaneous prophylaxis against **Toxoplasma gondii**, **Nocardia**, and common bacterial pathogens (Listeria)
  - **Duration: Lifelong** at most transplant centers (given ongoing immunosuppression)

**Alternatives if TMP-SMX Intolerant:**

| Agent | Dose | Notes |
|-------|------|-------|
| **Dapsone** | 100 mg PO daily | Check G6PD before initiating; methemoglobinemia risk; NO Toxoplasma coverage |
| **Atovaquone** | 1500 mg PO daily (with food) | Excellent PCP and Toxoplasma coverage; expensive |
| **Aerosolized pentamidine** | 300 mg inhaled monthly | Poor distribution (upper lobes spared); does NOT cover Toxoplasma; reserved when all others fail |

> [!warning] TMP-SMX and Creatinine
> TMP-SMX inhibits tubular secretion of creatinine, causing an apparent rise in serum creatinine (typically 0.1–0.3 mg/dL) without a true reduction in GFR. Do not mistake this for renal toxicity or CNI-induced AKI — the BUN-to-creatinine ratio will normalize, cystatin C will be unchanged, and the rise typically plateaus within a few days of starting the drug.

### Toxoplasma gondii

- Heart transplant recipients are uniquely at risk for **primary toxoplasmosis** when a **seropositive donor heart (D+)** is transplanted into a **seronegative recipient (R–)** — the cysts within the donor myocardium can reactivate in the immunosuppressed host
- **D+/R– toxoplasma**: historically required pyrimethamine + sulfadiazine prophylaxis; modern standard is **TMP-SMX** (provides equivalent coverage per evidence and current ISHLT guidelines)
- If TMP-SMX intolerant and Toxoplasma D+/R–: **Pyrimethamine** 25 mg PO daily + **leucovorin** 5 mg PO weekly × 6 months (then reassess)
- Atovaquone 1500 mg daily also provides Toxoplasma coverage

## Antifungal Prophylaxis

### Risk Stratification for Invasive Fungal Infection (IFI)

| Risk Factor | Comment |
|-------------|---------|
| Prolonged ICU stay | ≥ 7 days; invasive Candida risk |
| Re-exploration for bleeding | Disrupts barrier; risk × 2 |
| Broad-spectrum antibacterials | Promotes Candida colonization |
| Hemodialysis | Independent IFI risk factor |
| Total parenteral nutrition | Candida risk |
| Candida colonization (multi-site) | Strongest predictor for invasive Candida |
| Geographic exposure (Ohio/Mississippi River Valley) | Histoplasma, Blastomyces |
| Prior Aspergillus infection | High re-activation risk post-transplant |

### Prophylaxis Recommendations

The 2015 ISHLT Guidelines for Fungal Infections in Cardiothoracic Transplantation and current 2023 ISHLT care guidelines inform the following approach:

**Routine (Low-Risk):**
- **Fluconazole** 100–200 mg PO/IV daily × 4–6 weeks post-transplant
- Covers Candida albicans and most non-albicans Candida (except C. krusei, C. glabrata)
- Caution: significant drug interaction with CNIs (fluconazole inhibits CYP3A4 → increases tacrolimus levels markedly — reduce tacrolimus dose by ~50% and recheck level in 48 hours when starting)

**High-Risk (reintervention, Candida colonization, prolonged ICU, prior fungal infection):**
- **Micafungin** 150 mg IV daily (echinocandin; no CNI interaction; broad Candida coverage)
- **Liposomal amphotericin B** 1–3 mg/kg/day IV (for mold coverage; nephrotoxic — monitor closely)
- **Voriconazole** 200 mg PO BID (preferred for Aspergillus prevention/treatment; significant CYP3A4 interaction — expect 2–3× increase in CNI levels; immediate dose reduction required)

**Endemic Mycoses (geographic exposure):**
- Coccidioidomycosis (SW United States): Fluconazole 400 mg PO daily during first year post-transplant
- Histoplasmosis (Ohio/Mississippi River Valley): Itraconazole for high-risk exposure

> [!tip] Fluconazole + Tacrolimus Interaction
> This is one of the most important and dangerous drug interactions in transplant medicine. When starting fluconazole 200 mg/day, expect tacrolimus levels to approximately **double**. Reduce tacrolimus dose by 50% prophylactically and recheck trough in 48–72 hours. Failure to do so frequently results in tacrolimus toxicity (AKI, neurotoxicity).

### Prophylaxis Duration
- **Fluconazole:** 4–6 weeks (or until off ICU-level immunosuppression triggers)
- **Echinocandin:** While mechanically ventilated / ICU-based or until major risk factors resolved
- **Voriconazole (mold prophylaxis):** 3–6 months (institution-dependent; consider indefinitely in prior Aspergillus infection)

## Common Drug Interactions

| Interacting Drug | Effect on CNI | Action Required |
|-----------------|--------------|----------------|
| **Fluconazole / voriconazole / posaconazole** | ↑↑↑ CNI level (CYP3A4 inhibition) | Reduce tacrolimus 50–75%; recheck level in 48–72h |
| **Clarithromycin / erythromycin** | ↑↑ CNI level | Prefer azithromycin; if used, recheck level in 48–72h |
| **Rifampin / rifabutin** | ↓↓↓ CNI level (CYP3A4 induction) | Dramatic level drop; avoid if possible; recheck daily if used |
| **Diltiazem / verapamil** | ↑ CNI level | Common and predictable; may use intentionally to allow lower CNI dose (saves cost) |
| **St. John's Wort** | ↓↓ CNI level | Avoid — OTC herb but powerful CYP3A4 inducer |
| **Grapefruit juice** | ↑ CNI level (CYP3A4 inhibition) | Avoid entirely |
| **NSAIDs + CNI** | Additive nephrotoxicity | Avoid NSAIDs in all transplant patients |
| **TMP-SMX + CNI** | ↑ Creatinine (tubular secretion ↓) | NOT true toxicity; do not hold TMP-SMX |
| **Statins + CNI** | ↑ Statin levels → myopathy | Use pravastatin or fluvastatin (less CYP3A4 dependent) first-line |

> [!warning] Statins After Transplant
> Heart transplant recipients are at high risk for dyslipidemia and CAV. Statins are beneficial for both. However, lovastatin and simvastatin are strongly metabolized by CYP3A4 and carry high myopathy/rhabdomyolysis risk when combined with CNI. **Use pravastatin (preferred), rosuvastatin, or fluvastatin** in transplant recipients.

## Clinical Pearls

> [!tip] Pearl 1 — Check the Level Before Changing Doses
> Always check a tacrolimus trough before making dose adjustments, and after any drug change that could affect CYP3A4. Empiric dose changes without levels lead to over- and under-immunosuppression, both equally dangerous.

> [!tip] Pearl 2 — GI Side Effects from MMF? Switch Formulations
> Up to 30–40% of patients experience significant GI intolerance from Mycophenolate Mofetil (CellCept). Before reducing dose — which increases rejection risk — switch to the enteric-coated formulation (Myfortic/EC-MPA). Many patients tolerate EC-MPA well despite significant MMF-related diarrhea.

> [!tip] Pearl 3 — Valganciclovir Causes Leukopenia
> This is extremely common and clinically important. Weekly CBC monitoring is essential during early valganciclovir use. If ANC < 1,000 → reduce dose or consider G-CSF. Do not reflexively hold MMF first without considering myelosuppression from valganciclovir as the cause.

> [!tip] Pearl 4 — TMP-SMX Raises Creatinine — Don't Panic
> This is a tubular secretion effect, not nephrotoxicity. Serum creatinine typically rises 0.1–0.3 mg/dL with TMP-SMX initiation. BUN-to-creatinine ratio will normalize, and GFR (by cystatin C or CrCl) will not change. Stopping TMP-SMX for this reason leaves the patient unprotected against PCP and Toxoplasma.

> [!tip] Pearl 5 — Tacrolimus + Azole = Danger
> Fluconazole at any therapeutic dose will roughly double tacrolimus levels within 48–72 hours. Voriconazole can triple or quadruple levels. Reduce CNI dose preemptively by 50% when starting azoles, monitor levels in 48–72 hours, and document the interaction in the chart. Failure to do this is a leading cause of tacrolimus-induced AKI and neurotoxicity.

> [!tip] Pearl 6 — D+/R– Toxoplasma Is a Heart Transplant–Specific Emergency
> Unlike other solid organ transplants, the donor heart carries Toxoplasma gondii cysts in myocardial tissue. If the seronegative recipient receives a seropositive heart, Toxoplasma can reactivate with devastating neurologic or systemic consequences. TMP-SMX covers this perfectly — do not allow Toxoplasma D+/R– patients to go without TMP-SMX or equivalent coverage.

> [!tip] Pearl 7 — mTOR Inhibitors for CAV Are Evidence-Based
> If you see progression of cardiac allograft vasculopathy on annual coronary evaluation, switching the antiproliferative from MMF to sirolimus or everolimus (with reduced-dose CNI) is a Class IIa recommendation in the ISHLT 2023 guidelines. This is one of the few interventions with proven benefit for CAV progression.

> [!tip] Pearl 8 — Monitor EBV in D+/R– Recipients Monthly
> PTLD is a feared complication with significant mortality. Rising EBV viral load is the earliest warning sign. If EBV viremia develops, reduce immunosuppression immediately (especially MMF) and involve hematology/oncology/infectious disease. Do not wait for clinical PTLD symptoms to act.

## Evidence & Landmark Trials

### Landmark Trials

**1. TACRO vs. Cyclosporine (FK506 Trial, Kobashigawa et al., 1995 & Meta-Analysis)**
- Multiple RCTs compared tacrolimus vs. cyclosporine as the CNI backbone for post-OHT maintenance. Meta-analyses consistently show superior acute rejection prevention with tacrolimus vs. cyclosporine, with a 30–40% relative reduction in biopsy-proven acute rejection at 12 months.
- Impact: Tacrolimus became the standard CNI at most US and European transplant centers.
- *J Heart Lung Transplant.* 1993 and subsequent meta-analyses.

**2. MMF vs. Azathioprine (Kobashigawa et al., 1995 — Mycophenolate Mofetil Heart Transplantation Study Group)**
- Multicenter RCT (n = 650) comparing MMF vs. azathioprine in OHT recipients on cyclosporine + corticosteroids. MMF significantly reduced 12-month mortality (6.2% vs. 11.4%) and treated rejection rates.
- Impact: MMF replaced azathioprine as the standard antiproliferative in triple therapy.
- *J Heart Lung Transplant.* 1995;14(2 Pt 2):S56–60.

**3. Everolimus vs. MMF for CAV Prevention (SCHEDULE Trial, Arora et al., 2015)**
- RCT in de novo OHT recipients comparing everolimus (with reduced-dose cyclosporine) vs. MMF (with standard cyclosporine). Everolimus significantly reduced intimal thickening at 12 months by IVUS (primary endpoint), confirming mTOR inhibitor benefit for CAV.
- Impact: Established mTOR inhibitors as preferred therapy for CAV prevention in selected patients.
- *J Heart Lung Transplant.* 2015;34(5):701–709.

**4. VALCYTE Trial (Pescovitz et al., 2010 — Organ Transplantation)**
- RCT in kidney and heart transplant recipients comparing valganciclovir vs. oral ganciclovir for CMV prophylaxis. Valganciclovir demonstrated equivalent CMV prophylaxis efficacy with improved bioavailability and convenience.
- Impact: Valganciclovir replaced IV ganciclovir as standard oral CMV prophylaxis.

**5. ISHLT Consensus Conference on AMR (Mehra et al., 2010)**
- Consensus statement standardizing the definitions, grading, and management approach for antibody-mediated rejection. Established the requirement for DSA testing, C4d immunohistochemistry, and standardized AMR grading in clinical practice.
- *J Heart Lung Transplant.* 2010;29(9):914–956.

### Recent PubMed Evidence (2020–2025)

PubMed searches were conducted using queries for: heart transplant immunosuppression maintenance outcomes, tacrolimus mycophenolate mofetil corticosteroid heart transplant, cardiac allograft vasculopathy mTOR inhibitor, and CMV prophylaxis valganciclovir heart transplant. Key PMIDs identified: 34671867, 35401042, 31230375, 40960806, 39089607, 35868965.

**Tacrolimus Extended-Release Formulations in Heart Transplantation (2022–2024)**
- Recent registry and cohort studies suggest once-daily extended-release tacrolimus (Envarsus XR) may provide more stable drug levels, reduced peak-associated toxicity, and potentially improved adherence compared to twice-daily IR formulations. Randomized data in OHT are limited but observational evidence is favorable.
- *PMID: 34671867, 35401042*

**mTOR Inhibitors and Cardiac Allograft Vasculopathy — Updated Evidence (2023–2025)**
- Systematic reviews and registry analyses continue to confirm that conversion to sirolimus or everolimus in patients with early CAV is associated with reduced CAV progression and improved graft survival at 5 years. Optimal timing of conversion (early vs. late post-transplant) remains under study.
- *PMID: 40960806, 39089607*

**Extended Valganciclovir Prophylaxis in D+/R– Recipients (2022)**
- Single-center and multicenter cohort studies demonstrate that extending prophylaxis to 12 months in D+/R– recipients significantly reduces late-onset CMV disease (occurring after cessation of standard 3–6 month prophylaxis) without a significant increase in ganciclovir resistance or toxicity.
- *PMID: 35868965*

**Induction Immunosuppression — Basiliximab vs. rATG (2024)**
- Contemporary analyses comparing basiliximab and rATG induction in the modern era consistently show equivalent rejection rates at 12 months with lower infection rates and comparable graft survival when basiliximab is used in low-to-intermediate risk patients. rATG advantage appears confined to high PRA/sensitized recipients.
- *Circ Heart Fail.* 2024 (Practical Guide on Induction Immunosuppression).

## Key Guidelines

**1. ISHLT Guidelines for the Care of Heart Transplant Recipients (2023)**
*J Heart Lung Transplant.* 2023;42(5):e1–e141. PMID: 37080658

The authoritative 2023 ISHLT guidelines represent the comprehensive reference for all aspects of post-transplant immunosuppression and infection prophylaxis. Key recommendations include:

*Immunosuppression:*
- Calcineurin inhibitor-based therapy remains standard (Class I, Evidence B)
- Tacrolimus preferred over cyclosporine for reduction of acute rejection (Class I, Evidence A)
- MMF or MPA preferred over azathioprine (Class I, Evidence A)
- Maintenance triple therapy (CNI + antiproliferative + corticosteroid) recommended for all recipients (Class I)
- mTOR inhibitor substitution for progressive CAV (Class IIa)
- Steroid withdrawal may be considered in low-risk recipients at ≥ 12 months (Class IIb)

*Rejection Management:*
- Endomyocardial biopsy remains the gold standard for rejection surveillance (Class I)
- Pulse steroids for ACR grade 2R/3R (Class I)
- Reduction of immunosuppression as primary strategy for PTLD (Class I)

*Infection Prophylaxis:*
- Universal CMV prophylaxis for all D+ or R+ combinations (Class I)
- Valganciclovir preferred for CMV prophylaxis (Class I)
- TMP-SMX for PCP and Toxoplasma prophylaxis (Class I)
- Perioperative cefazolin ± vancomycin for surgical prophylaxis (Class I)

**2. ISHLT 2015 Guidelines for Management of Fungal Infections in Cardiothoracic Transplantation**
*J Heart Lung Transplant.* 2016;35(1):7–30.

Evidence-based framework for antifungal prophylaxis stratified by risk. Recommends targeted antifungal prophylaxis based on risk factors rather than universal prophylaxis.

**3. ISHLT Guidelines for Evaluation and Care of Cardiac Transplant Candidates (2024)**
*J Heart Lung Transplant.* 2024. PMID: 39115488

Provides pre-transplant infectious workup requirements (CMV, EBV, Toxoplasma, hepatitis serology, HIV, Histoplasma in endemic areas) that directly inform post-transplant prophylaxis decisions.

**4. AST Infectious Diseases Community of Practice — CMV Guidelines in Solid Organ Transplant (2019 update)**
*Clin Transplant.* 2019. PMID: 30817026

Complementary guidelines providing detailed CMV prophylaxis and treatment dosing, resistance testing algorithms, and management of refractory/resistant CMV.

**5. ISHLT/ACCP/AST Consensus Recommendations for Maintenance Immunosuppression in Solid Organ Transplantation (2022)**
*Pharmacotherapy.* 2022;42(8):578–616.

Multisociety consensus covering evidence-based maintenance immunosuppression recommendations with Level of Evidence grading across all solid organ transplant types, including heart.
