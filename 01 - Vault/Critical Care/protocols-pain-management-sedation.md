# Pain Management & Sedation in the Critically Ill

---

## 1. General Schema for the Intubated Patient

The approach to analgesia and sedation in the ICU should follow a **multimodal strategy** — using moderate doses of several agents that work synergistically, rather than relying on high doses of a single drug.

### Core Sedation: Propofol and/or Dexmedetomidine

| Agent | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|-------|-----------|---------------|
| **Propofol** | Easier titration (shorter half-life); may achieve deeper sedation; antiepileptic activity; may reduce ICP; may treat alcohol withdrawal | Hypertriglyceridemia; excessive brain suppression at high doses may cause/prolong delirium; hypotension |
| **Dexmedetomidine** | Promotes physiologic sleep and reduces delirium; facilitates extubation (can continue through extubation); provides adjunctive analgesia; synergizes with ketamine | Higher rate of bradycardia; cannot achieve deep sedation; sympatholytic effects may worsen systolic HF; tachyphylaxis with prolonged use |

**Key Point:** This is not a binary choice. Some patients benefit from a combination of **low-dose propofol + low-dose dexmedetomidine**, avoiding the toxicity of high doses of either agent alone.

- **Low-dose propofol** functions as a sedative (patients arousable, relatively intact)
- **High-dose propofol** functions as a general anesthetic (EEG silent, deeply comatose) — this probably promotes delirium

### Remainder of the Regimen

**Analgesia (More Useful):**
- Acetaminophen 1,000 mg q6hr scheduled (650 mg q8hr if cirrhosis, EtOHism, or <50 kg)
- IV fentanyl PRN pain (avoid infusion if feasible)
- Pain-dose ketamine infusion ~0.1–0.3 mg/kg/hr for basal analgesia (risk of psychomimetic effects minimized by co-administration with dexmedetomidine or propofol)

**Analgesia (Also Consider):**
- Methocarbamol for musculoskeletal pain or muscle spasm
- Pregabalin for neuropathic pain (including major trauma/surgery with nerve damage)

**Adjunctive Sedation (More Useful):**
- Quetiapine 50–100 mg PO q12hr.
- Hydroxyzine 50–100 mg PO q6hr
- Melatonin 3 mg PO QHS. Up to 10mg

**Adjunctive Sedation (Also Consider):**
- Haloperidol IV PRN (use if tempted to give IV lorazepam)
- Valproic acid for refractory agitation
- Dissociative ketamine infusion 1–5 mg/kg/hr (last resort; for patients with profound hypotension limiting use of other sedatives)

## 2. Pure Analgesics

### Acetaminophen

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Mechanism** | Centrally acting, non-competitive, reversible COX inhibitor with analgesic and antipyretic effects |
| **Standard dosing** | 1,000 mg q6hr (max 4 g/day) |
| **At-risk dosing** | 650 mg q8hr (max 2 g/day) — severe alcoholism, stable cirrhosis, malnutrition, weight <50 kg |
| **Avoid entirely** | Acute liver injury, decompensated cirrhosis, neutropenia (may blunt detection of neutropenic fever) |
| **Route** | PO preferred over IV (no efficacy difference; lower cost). PR bioavailability may be as low as 24% |
| **Renal failure** | No dose adjustment |
| **Key principle** | Should be **scheduled** (not PRN) for patients with ongoing pain |
| **Half-life** | ~2–3 hours |

**Drug Interactions:** Warfarin (potentiates effect); CYP450 inducers increase NAPQI formation (chronic alcohol, isoniazid, phenytoin, carbamazepine, rifampin); cholestyramine decreases oral bioavailability (separate by >2 hours).

### Ketamine (Pain-Dose)

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Dose** | 0.1–0.3 mg/kg/hr infusion |
| **Indication** | Basal analgesia; patients with difficult-to-treat pain |
| **Key advantage** | Minimizes psychomimetic side effects when co-administered with dexmedetomidine or propofol |
| **Synergy** | Combines well with alpha-2 agonists — synergistic analgesia, hemodynamic stability, and avoidance of ketamine psychotomimetic effects |
| **Additional benefits** | May reduce opioid tolerance and opioid-induced hyperalgesia; may prevent alpha-2 agonist tachyphylaxis |

### Methocarbamol

| Parameter                  | Detail                                                                                                       |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **PO dosing**              | 500–1,500 mg q6hr                                                                                            |
| **IV dosing**              | 500 - 1,000 mg q8hr                                                                                          |
| **Indications**            | Musculoskeletal pain, muscle spasm (herniated discs, rib fractures, painful chest tubes), perioperative pain |
| **Contraindications (IV)** | Hypotension/tenuous hemodynamics; renal failure (polyethylene glycol in vehicle)                             |
| **Not effective for**      | Spasms due to upper motor neuron injury                                                                      |
| **Half-life**              | 1–2 hours                                                                                                    |

### NSAIDs — General Principles

**NSAIDs are generally not preferred in the ICU.** May consider selectively if ALL of the following are met:

- Low risk for AKI (anuric on chronic dialysis OR robust kidney function without nephrotoxins)
- Reasonably low risk for GI hemorrhage (on PPI, no cirrhosis or IBD, no GI ulcer history)
- No active hemorrhage or severe coagulopathy
- No history of aspirin/NSAID sensitivity (Samter's triad)
- No cirrhosis or inflammatory bowel disease

**Dose Ceiling Concept:** Above a certain dose, further increases only increase toxicity without increasing efficacy. Recent evidence shows the ceiling is often lower than previously thought.

| NSAID | Dose Ceiling / Typical Dose | Relative GI Bleed Risk | Key Notes |
|-------|----------------------------|----------------------|-----------|
| **Celecoxib** | 200 mg BID (max 400 mg/day); load 400 mg | ~1.5 | COX-2 selective; lower AKI and GI risk; sulfonamide allergy contraindication |
| **Ibuprofen** | 400 mg q4–8hr PRN (ceiling ~400 mg/dose) | ~1.8 | Best safety profile among nonselective COX inhibitors |
| **Ketorolac** | 10–15 mg IV q6hr (ceiling ~10–15 mg); limit ≤5 days | ~11.5 | Most toxic NSAID for GI/MI; reduce dose if >65 yr, renal impairment, or <50 kg |
| **Diclofenac** | 50 mg PO BID–TID (max ~150 mg/day) | ~3.3 | Celecoxib or ibuprofen are safer |
| **Naproxen** | 500 mg q12hr or 250 mg q6–8hr PRN | ~5.6 | Avoid GFR <30 |
| **Meloxicam** | 5–10 mg/day (max 10 mg/day) | ~3.5 | COX-2 >> COX-1; HD max 5 mg/day |
| **Indomethacin** | 20–25 mg TID or 40–50 mg BID–TID | ~4 | Max 150–200 mg/day |

### Lidocaine (IV)

Used as part of multimodal analgesia; inhibits sodium channels to reduce pain signaling. Particularly useful for visceral and neuropathic pain. Requires cardiac monitoring.

## 3. Opioids

### Selection of IV Opioid — Intubated Patients

| Priority     | Agent                    | Rationale                                                                                                                                                                        |
| ------------ | ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1st line** | **PRN IV Fentanyl**      | Rapid onset allows dose-titration without stacking; no histamine release (hemodynamic stability); lower nausea/vomiting risk; no active metabolite accumulation in renal failure |
| **2nd line** | **PRN IV Hydromorphone** | No histamine release; longer-lasting effects if patient requires very frequent fentanyl                                                                                          |
| **3rd line** | **PRN IV Morphine**      | Longer half-life makes titration harder; **contraindicated GFR <30** (M3G/M6G accumulation)                                                                                      |

### Selection of IV Opioid — Non-Intubated Patients

| Priority        | Agent                                    | Rationale                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| --------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1st line**    | **IV Fentanyl (low-dose, e.g., 25 mcg)** | Rapid onset; easily titrated; no histamine release; minimal hemodynamic effect at low doses; preferred for non-intubated patients when brief analgesia is needed                                                                |
| **2nd line**    | **IV Hydromorphone**                     | Longer duration than fentanyl; no histamine release; useful when more sustained analgesia needed; start 0.2–0.5 mg                                                                                                              |
| **3rd line**    | **IV Morphine**                          | Effective; less habit-forming; contraindicated GFR <30; more histamine release (hypotension, pruritus)                                                                                                                          |
| **Alternative** | **IV Nalbuphine**                        | Less respiratory depression (ceiling effect), less N/V, less pruritus, lowest abuse potential. Contraindicated in opioid-tolerant patients. *Not routinely used at all facilities — consider availability and team familiarity* |

### Selection of Oral Opioid

| Priority     | Agent                   | Key Notes                                                                                      |
| ------------ | ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1st line** | **PO Morphine**         | Least euphorogenic; minimal DDI; CI GFR <30                                                    |
| **2nd line** | **PO Hydromorphone**    | ~4× more potent than PO morphine; minimal DDI; caution severe renal failure                    |
| **3rd line** | **PO Oxycodone**        | Highest abuse potential; may use cautiously in renal failure (GFR >10); CI hepatic dysfunction |
| **4th line** | **PO Hydrocodone-APAP** | Fixed combo limits titration; lower abuse potential than oxycodone; CI hepatic dysfunction     |

|-------|-------------------|

| PO Hydrocodone | 15 mg (1.5× weaker than PO oxycodone) |

### Avoiding Opioid Infusions — Key Principles

Continuous opioid infusions **lack strong evidentiary basis** and should be avoided when possible:

- Continuous exposure rapidly causes tolerance → dependence → withdrawal
- Infusions will be up-titrated during pain but less aggressively down-titrated → progressive dose creep
- Fentanyl infusions accumulate in fat tissue → impossible to rapidly withdraw for extubation
- High cumulative exposure may cause opioid-induced hyperalgesia

**If an infusion is necessary:**

1. Use a rational dose (e.g., 25–50 mcg/hr fentanyl; note: 100 mcg/hr ≈ 400 mg PO oxycodone daily)
2. Wean aggressively at least once daily
3. Use PRN boluses for acute pain — do not merely up-titrate the infusion
4. Co-infuse pain-dose ketamine to limit tolerance and hyperalgesia
5. Monitor the ratio of PRN to infusion dosing; if no PRN needed, infusion rate is likely too high

**Acceptable uses of opioid infusions:**
- Profound respiratory failure requiring suppression of respiratory drive (severe status asthmaticus) — lesser of two evils vs. paralysis
- Chronic opioid patients requiring basal dosing to prevent withdrawal

### Opioid Side Effects — ICU Concerns

| Side Effect | Notes |
|-------------|-------|
| **Respiratory suppression** | Beneficial in intubated patients (ventilator synchrony) but delays extubation |
| **GI failure** | Nausea, gastroparesis, ileus, colonic pseudo-obstruction — use aggressive bowel regimen |
| **Urinary retention** | Increased detrusor and sphincter tone |
| **Dependence/withdrawal** | Brain adapts rapidly to continuous exposure; may contribute to post-ICU depression |
| **Delirium** | Appropriate use for pain probably doesn't cause delirium; misuse as sedative does |
| **Histamine release** | Primarily morphine — hypotension, urticaria, pruritus, bronchospasm. Fentanyl/tramadol do not release histamine |
| **Bradycardia** | Central mu-receptor stimulation → increased vagal tone; more notable with high-dose rapid IV fentanyl |
| **Opioid-induced hyperalgesia** | Paradoxical pain worsening at high doses; most problematic with remifentanil and fentanyl |

### Nalbuphine — Special Considerations

Nalbuphine deserves emphasis as it is under-utilized. It is a **partial mu-receptor agonist / kappa agonist** (similar mechanism to buprenorphine):

- **Ceiling on respiratory depression** — additional doses beyond moderate amounts don't worsen respiratory suppression (OR 0.27 vs. morphine)
- **Less nausea/vomiting** (OR 0.65 vs. morphine)
- **Less pruritus** (OR 0.17 vs. morphine) — can treat uremic pruritus
- **No active metabolite accumulation in renal failure**
- **Only non-controlled opioid** in the USA
- **Hemodynamically stable** even at very high doses
- **Anti-shivering** properties comparable to meperidine

**Contraindications:** Patients on chronic opioids (may precipitate withdrawal); patients with established opioid tolerance; patients expected to have very high opioid requirements.

### Patient-Controlled Analgesia (PCA) — Key Points

- PCA maintains analgesia — it does not rescue uncontrolled pain (load patient first with clinician-titrated boluses)
- **Basal rate should always be zero** unless the patient was on chronic opioids
- Adding a basal rate does not improve analgesia but increases toxicity
- Only the patient should activate the PCA (safety mechanism: sedated patients stop pressing)
- Discontinue all other opioid orders when starting PCA

## 4. Gabapentinoids

### General Indications

- **Neuropathic pain**: Guillain-Barré, diabetic neuropathy, spinal cord injury, post-herpetic neuralgia, SAH pain, post-stroke central pain
- **Post-cardiothoracic surgery analgesia**: Pregabalin 150 mg BID × 2 weeks + ketamine 0.1 mg/kg/hr × 48 hr reduces acute and chronic pain, opioid consumption, and improves long-term quality of life
- Partial seizures, anxiety, alcohol use disorder, restless leg syndrome

**Pregabalin is preferred** over gabapentin due to superior pharmacokinetics (>90% bioavailability independent of dose; gabapentin has dose-dependent, saturable absorption).

| Agent          | Dose              | Renal Adjustment                             |
| -------------- | ----------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **Pregabalin** | 75–150 mg q12hr   | GFR 30–60: 75 mg BID; GFR 15–30: 75 mg daily |
| **Gabapentin** | 300–1,200 mg q8hr | Dose reduction required                      |

**Side Effects:** Somnolence, respiratory depression, hypoactive delirium, myoclonus, dizziness. Rapid discontinuation can cause withdrawal (especially if used for seizures).

## 5. Alpha-2 Agonists

### Dexmedetomidine

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Infusion rate** | 0–1.4 mcg/kg/hr |
| **Loading** | Avoid boluses (risk of bradycardia/hemodynamic collapse); start at high rate (1–1.4 mcg/kg/hr) and down-titrate as drug takes effect (~1 hr) |
| **Weight-based dosing** | Use **lean body weight** in morbid obesity |
| **Key advantages** | No respiratory depression (can use on BiPAP and through extubation); promotes physiologic sleep; reduces delirium; arousable sedation; synergizes with ketamine |
| **Contraindications** | Bradycardia, heart block (grade 2–3 without pacemaker), severe hypotension, systolic HF |
| **Duration limit** | Consider limiting to <3–5 days to avoid tolerance/withdrawal |
| **Renal failure** | No adjustment (metabolites inactive) |
| **Cirrhosis** | Consider dose reduction |
| **Half-life** | ~1.5–2 hours (context-sensitive, may increase with prolonged infusion) |

**Optimizing Use:**
- Down-titrate (or stop) during the day; up-titrate at night to promote circadian rhythm
- If on dexmedetomidine >3–5 days, transition to oral clonidine or guanfacine to avoid withdrawal
- If bradycardia limits use but dexmedetomidine is mission-critical: combine with low-dose epinephrine or dobutamine infusion

### Oral Alpha-2 Agonists

| Agent | Starting Dose | Max Dose | Key Notes |
|-------|--------------|---------|-----------|
| **Clonidine** | 0.2 mg q6hr | 0.5 mg q6hr (2.4 mg/day for withdrawal) | Fastest onset (~1–2 hr); most hypotension; half-life extends to 41 hr in severe renal failure |
| **Guanfacine** | 0.5 mg q8–12hr | 1 mg q8hr (4 mg/day) | Less hypotension than clonidine; slower onset (2–4 hr); 2 mg QHS effective for delirium; less affected by renal failure |
| **Tizanidine** | 4 mg q6–8hr | 12 mg q8hr (36 mg/day) | Primarily analgesic with muscle relaxant properties; minimal hemodynamic effect; **CYP1A2 dependent** — CI with ciprofloxacin; avoid in hepatic dysfunction |

**Tolerance & Withdrawal:** Alpha-2 agonist withdrawal (tachycardia, hypertension, anxiety) can occur with abrupt cessation after several days. Limit duration, taper gradually, and avoid very high doses.

**Alpha-2 + Ketamine Synergy:**
- Synergistic analgesia
- Hemodynamic stability (ketamine raises BP, alpha-2 lowers BP)
- Alpha-2 suppresses ketamine's psychomimetic effects
- Ketamine may prevent alpha-2 tachyphylaxis

| **Droperidol** | Similar to haloperidol; may be used for agitation |

### Anti-5HT2A Predominant ("Atypical")

| Agent | Dose | Notes |
|-------|------|-------|
| **Olanzapine (Zyprexa)** | See detailed section below | Workhorse agent for ICU agitation |
| **Quetiapine** | 50–100 mg PO q12hr | Useful adjunctive sedation |
| **Lurasidone** | Variable | Fewer metabolic effects |

### Olanzapine (Zyprexa) — Detailed Reference

Olanzapine is one of the most versatile and effective agents for managing agitation, delirium, and nausea in the critically ill. It has a favorable side-effect profile compared to haloperidol and offers multiple routes of administration.

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **PO dose** | 5–10 mg PO q12–24hr; may start 2.5–5 mg in elderly or debilitated patients |
| **IM dose** | 5–10 mg IM; may repeat × 1 in 2 hours; max 30 mg/day IM |
| **ODT (orally disintegrating tablet)** | 5–10 mg — dissolves on tongue; useful for patients who refuse or cannot swallow tablets but can open mouth |
| **Onset** | PO: 15–45 min; IM: 15–30 min (peak ~30–45 min) |
| **Half-life** | 21–54 hours (mean ~30 hr) — allows q12–24hr dosing |
| **Duration** | Effective sedation lasts ~4–6+ hours after IM dosing |
| **Metabolism** | Hepatic (CYP1A2 primary, CYP2D6 minor); glucuronidation |

**Clinical Advantages:**

- **Rapid, reliable IM onset** — among the fastest-acting IM antipsychotics for acute agitation (comparable or superior to IM haloperidol)
- **Broad receptor profile** — antagonizes D2, 5-HT2A, 5-HT2C, H1, muscarinic, and alpha-1 receptors → sedation + antiemesis + anxiolysis + antipsychotic effect
- **Less EPS and dystonia** than haloperidol — particularly advantageous in elderly and Parkinson's-adjacent patients
- **Potent antiemetic** — useful for postoperative nausea, opioid-induced nausea, and chemotherapy-associated nausea (5-HT2/D2/H1 blockade)
- **Minimal QTc prolongation** — significantly safer than haloperidol and droperidol regarding torsades risk; can be used when QTc limits haloperidol
- **Long half-life** — once adequate sedation is achieved, dosing frequency is lower than shorter-acting agents
- **No IV formulation issues** — avoids propylene glycol toxicity concerns seen with IV lorazepam

**ICU Indications:**

- **Hyperactive delirium / agitation** — first-line atypical antipsychotic; especially when haloperidol is limited by QTc prolongation
- **ICU delirium prevention** — some evidence supports low-dose olanzapine (5 mg QHS) to reduce delirium incidence in high-risk patients
- **Nausea/vomiting** — 2.5–5 mg PO/ODT/IM; effective as rescue antiemetic and as part of multimodal PONV prophylaxis
- **Adjunctive sedation** — useful when trying to minimize propofol or benzodiazepine exposure
- **Agitation in alcohol/substance withdrawal** — adjunct to primary withdrawal therapy
- **Sleep promotion** — H1 antagonism provides sedation at lower doses (2.5–5 mg QHS)

**Key Precautions:**

| Concern | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **IM olanzapine + IM benzodiazepine** | **Avoid concurrent IM administration** — reports of cardiorespiratory depression, excessive sedation, and death. Separate by ≥1 hour. PO combinations are generally safer |
| **Hypotension** | Alpha-1 blockade can cause orthostatic hypotension; use cautiously in hypovolemic or hemodynamically unstable patients |
| **Anticholinergic effects** | Dry mouth, urinary retention, constipation, tachycardia — avoid in patients with bowel obstruction or severe ileus |
| **Metabolic effects** | Hyperglycemia, weight gain, hyperlipidemia — less relevant in acute ICU setting but consider in prolonged use |
| **Hepatic impairment** | Start at lower dose (2.5–5 mg); monitor LFTs |
| **Elderly with dementia** | Black box warning: increased risk of cerebrovascular events and mortality (class effect for all atypicals) — weigh risk/benefit in acute delirium |
| **Smoking status** | CYP1A2 inducer — smokers may require higher doses; dose reduction if smoking cessation occurs during admission |
| **Drug interactions** | CYP1A2 inhibitors (ciprofloxacin, fluvoxamine) increase levels — reduce dose by ~50% |

**Dosing Strategy for ICU Agitation:**

1. Mild agitation / delirium prophylaxis: **2.5–5 mg PO/ODT QHS**
2. Moderate agitation: **5–10 mg PO/ODT or IM**, reassess in 30–60 min
3. Severe agitation (acute): **10 mg IM**, may repeat 5–10 mg IM × 1 after 2 hr (max 30 mg/day)
4. Maintenance: **5–10 mg PO q12–24hr** once acute episode controlled
5. Antiemetic: **2.5–5 mg PO/ODT/IM q6–8hr PRN**

### Valproic Acid

May be helpful in refractory agitation. Consider when other agents have failed.

## 8. Benzodiazepines

**Benzodiazepines are generally a sedative of LAST RESORT.** They are the most deliriogenic sedative class and a risk factor for PTSD.

### Acceptable Uses

1. **Midazolam** for procedural sedation
2. Sedative of choice for **intoxication** (especially sympathomimetic — muscle relaxation + antiseizure)
3. Patients with **profound hypotension** who cannot tolerate propofol or dexmedetomidine (though ketamine may be better)
4. **Alcohol withdrawal** (although phenobarbital is generally preferred)

### Quick Reference

| Agent | Onset (IV) | Half-life | Key Notes |
|-------|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| **Midazolam** | 2–5 min | ~1.5–2.5 hr (context-sensitive) | Best IM benzo; accumulates in fat with repeated dosing; no propylene glycol |
| **Lorazepam** | 15–20 min | 10–20 hr | Front-line for status epilepticus; slow onset → dose stacking risk; propylene glycol toxicity at high doses (avoid >1 mg/kg/day) |
| **Diazepam** | Very rapid | 20–50 hr (active metabolites 50–100 hr) | Preferred for alcohol withdrawal (rapid onset + accumulation); propylene glycol toxicity with very high doses |
| **Oxazepam** | PO only (~3 hr) | 6–12 hr | Safe in cirrhosis/elderly (glucuronidation only, no CYP); lowest abuse potential among benzos |

## 10. Multimodal Therapy — Core Concepts

### Foundational Principles

1. **Low doses optimize risk/benefit** — substantial clinical benefit with minimal toxicity; creates a safety buffer
2. **Different agents function synergistically** — 1+1 = 3; moderate doses of several agents have a large combined impact

### Targeted Treatment

Evaluate the source of discomfort and treat appropriately:

| Source | Optimal Treatment |
|--------|------------------|
| **Pain** | Analgesic agent (acetaminophen, ketamine, opioid) |
| **Anxiety** | Sedative (propofol, dexmedetomidine) |
| **Agitated delirium** | Antipsychotic (quetiapine, haloperidol) |

Use behavioral pain scores (CPOT, BPS) for non-communicative patients to differentiate pain from agitation.

### Daily Sedation Re-Assessment

- Re-assess the sedation regimen **at least daily** (ideally more often)
- Always seek the lowest effective doses
- **Do not** robotically shut off everything — if patient is awake and comfortable (RASS 0), stopping all sedation may cause unnecessary agitation
- Target RASS 0 to -2 for most ICU patients

## 11. Reversal Agents

### Flumazenil (Benzodiazepine Reversal)

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Dose** | 0.2–0.5 mg IV; repeat PRN to max 1 mg (may give up to 3 mg in 1 hour) |
| **Onset** | 1–2 minutes; peak 6–10 minutes |
| **Duration** | Only 20–60 minutes — **beware resedation** |
| **Contraindications** | Mixed intoxication; known seizure disorder; chronic benzodiazepine use |
| **Risks** | Seizures, resedation, nausea/vomiting, arrhythmias |
| **Best indication** | After procedural sedation with benzo in a benzo-naive patient |

### Naloxone (Opioid Reversal)

Standard reversal agent for opioid intoxication. Titrate carefully to avoid precipitating withdrawal and loss of analgesia.

## 12. Special Populations

### Renal Failure

| Agent | Recommendation |
|-------|---------------|
| Acetaminophen | No adjustment |
| Fentanyl | Preferred opioid — inactive metabolites |
| Nalbuphine | Usable — inactive metabolite; exposure increases 50–85% |
| Oxycodone (PO) | May use cautiously if GFR >10 |
| Morphine | **Avoid if GFR <30** (neurotoxic M3G/M6G accumulation) |
| Hydromorphone | Caution in severe renal failure (less toxic than morphine) |
| Ketorolac | **Avoid** — nephrotoxic AND renally cleared |
| NSAIDs (general) | Avoid unless anuric on chronic dialysis |
| Dexmedetomidine | No adjustment (inactive metabolites) |
| Clonidine | Half-life extends to ~41 hr in severe renal failure — use guanfacine instead |
| Guanfacine | Less affected by renal failure; conservative dosing in severe CKD |
| Pregabalin | GFR 30–60: reduce by 50%; GFR 15–30: reduce by 75% |
| Gabapentin | Dose reduction required |
| Midazolam | Active metabolite may accumulate — caution |
| Lorazepam | No CYP interactions; generally acceptable |

### Hepatic Dysfunction

| Agent | Recommendation |
|-------|---------------|
| Acetaminophen | Avoid in acute liver injury/decompensated cirrhosis; max 2 g/day in stable cirrhosis |
| Morphine | Increased exposure with PO morphine; dose reduction |
| Hydromorphone | Reduced metabolism — dose reduction |
| Oxycodone | Decrease dose by 50%; avoid scheduled doses |
| Dexmedetomidine | Consider dose reduction; monitor for bradycardia |
| Tizanidine | **Avoid** — extensive first-pass hepatic metabolism |
| Melatonin | Avoid in severe hepatic dysfunction |
| Celecoxib | Moderate impairment: reduce dose by 50% |

### Chronic Buprenorphine Patients

- Nalbuphine may precipitate withdrawal (similar partial mu-agonist mechanism)
- Full mu-agonists may need higher doses to overcome partial agonist effect
- Consult pain management/addiction medicine
