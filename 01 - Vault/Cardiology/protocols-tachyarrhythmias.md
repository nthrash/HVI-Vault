## Clinical overview

**Tachyarrhythmias** may be the primary cause of shock, ischemia, acute heart failure, or altered mentation—or a compensatory response to another process. This reference covers atrial fibrillation (AF), atrial flutter, regular supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), and ventricular tachyarrhythmias. It intentionally does **not** cover bradyarrhythmias or pacing; those will be addressed separately.

Management follows two priorities:

1. Determine whether the rhythm is causing hemodynamic instability and needs immediate electrical therapy.
2. Identify the rhythm and correct the precipitant while selecting a therapy that is safe for the patient’s ventricular function, QT interval, coronary status, and conduction pathway.

> [!warning] **Do not treat compensatory sinus tachycardia as a primary arrhythmia**
> In shock, hypoxemia, hemorrhage, sepsis, pulmonary embolism, pain, withdrawal, or thyrotoxicosis, tachycardia may preserve cardiac output. Treat the underlying cause first unless an independent pathologic tachyarrhythmia is clearly present.

## First decision: is the patient unstable?

The 2025 AHA adult tachyarrhythmia-with-a-pulse algorithm defines concerning instability as persistent tachyarrhythmia causing **hypotension, acute altered mental status, signs of shock, ischemic chest discomfort, or acute heart failure**.

| Scenario | Immediate action |
| --- | --- |
| Tachyarrhythmia with a pulse and instability | **Synchronized cardioversion**; sedate whenever feasible without delaying therapy. |
| Sustained polymorphic VT | **Immediate unsynchronized shock (defibrillation)**. |
| Pulseless VT/VF | Cardiac-arrest algorithm: high-quality CPR, defibrillation, and ACLS medications. |
| Stable rhythm | Obtain/interpret a 12-lead ECG, identify rhythm class, correct drivers, and use rhythm-specific therapy. |

> [!tip] **Synchronize only when the rhythm allows it**
> Polymorphic VT and an irregular wide-complex tachycardia may not synchronize reliably. Do not delay defibrillation for a synchronization attempt when polymorphic VT is sustained or the patient deteriorates.

## Rapid rhythm classification

Use a 12-lead ECG whenever it does not delay resuscitation. Rhythm regularity and QRS width often guide the first safe move.

| ECG pattern | Leading possibilities | Immediate safety point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Regular, narrow QRS | Sinus tachycardia, AVNRT/AVRT, atrial tachycardia, atrial flutter with fixed conduction | If regular and re-entrant SVT is likely, vagal maneuvers then adenosine may terminate or clarify the rhythm. |
| Irregular, narrow QRS | AF, flutter with variable block, multifocal atrial tachycardia | Do not use adenosine as AF rate control; evaluate AF/flutter triggers and AV-nodal-control safety. |
| Regular, wide QRS | **VT until proven otherwise**, SVT with aberrancy, antidromic AVRT | Adenosine is considered only when the rhythm is regular and monomorphic; obtain expert input. |
| Irregular, wide QRS | Preexcited AF, polymorphic VT, AF with aberrancy | Treat as high risk; avoid AV-nodal blockers if preexcited AF is possible. |

### VT features on a 12-lead ECG

AV dissociation, capture/fusion beats, extreme axis, concordance across precordial leads, a very wide QRS, and a history of prior MI/structural heart disease increase the likelihood of VT. When uncertain, **manage wide-complex tachycardia as VT** until expert review is available.

## Diagnostic workup and reversible drivers

### Immediate assessment

- Continuous rhythm, blood-pressure, and oximetry monitoring; IV access; 12-lead ECG before and after therapy when feasible.
- Focused history: onset, palpitations/syncope/chest pain, prior rhythm/ablation/ICD, cardiomyopathy/CAD, medication adherence, QT-prolonging or stimulant exposure, and anticoagulation status.
- Targeted exam: perfusion, pulmonary edema, volume status, neurologic status, new murmur, device-pocket findings, and signs of infection, bleeding, or thromboembolism.

### Directed tests

| Test | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| CBC, BMP, magnesium, glucose | Detect anemia, potassium/magnesium disorders, renal dysfunction, and metabolic drivers. |
| Troponin and serial ECG | Evaluate suspected ACS or demand injury; interpret in clinical context because tachyarrhythmia itself can elevate troponin. |
| TSH and toxicology review | Consider when history or unexplained AF/atrial tachycardia suggests thyroid disease or drug effect. |
| Echocardiography/POCUS | Assess LV/RV function, pericardial effusion, acute valvular disease, and volume/hemodynamic phenotype. |
| Device interrogation | Essential after ICD shocks, recurrent VT, suspected device malfunction, or possible atrial tracking. |

Common reversible drivers are ischemia, hypoxemia/hypercapnia, shock, pain/agitation, fever/sepsis, hemorrhage, pulmonary embolism, acute heart failure, electrolyte disorders, alcohol/stimulants, medication withdrawal, and proarrhythmic/QT-prolonging drugs.

## Regular narrow-complex tachycardia

For a stable, regular narrow-complex tachycardia, use a rhythm strip/12-lead to distinguish sinus tachycardia from re-entrant SVT or flutter. A ventricular rate near 150/min should prompt a deliberate search for atrial flutter with 2:1 conduction.

| Situation | Management |
| --- | --- |
| Sinus tachycardia | Treat the driver; do not reflexively administer AV-nodal blockers. |
| Regular SVT, stable | Vagal maneuvers if appropriate; then adenosine **6 mg rapid IV push with saline flush**, followed by **12 mg** if needed. |
| Regular SVT, unstable | Synchronized cardioversion; adenosine may be considered if regular and narrow while preparations occur. |
| Persistent/recurrent SVT | Reassess diagnosis and triggers; beta blocker or nondihydropyridine calcium channel blocker may be appropriate when hemodynamics and ventricular function allow; seek cardiology/EP input. |

> [!warning] **Adenosine has a narrow lane**
> Use it for a **regular** narrow tachycardia or a **regular, monomorphic** wide tachycardia when the AHA algorithm criteria are met. Do not use it for an irregular wide-complex rhythm or suspected preexcited AF.

## AF and atrial flutter with rapid ventricular response

### Acute goals

AF/flutter management includes the “3 As”: **acute triggers, rate/rhythm management, and anticoagulation**. Hemodynamic instability caused by AF/flutter requires immediate synchronized cardioversion. In stable patients, choose rate versus rhythm control based on symptoms, duration, ventricular function, comorbidities, and thromboembolic risk.

| Clinical context | Preferred approach | Critical caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stable AF/flutter with RVR, EF >40% | IV beta blocker or diltiazem/verapamil for acute rate control. | Do not stack AV-nodal blockers without reassessment of BP, perfusion, and conduction. |
| AF/flutter with known moderate/severe LV systolic dysfunction or decompensated HFrEF | Avoid IV diltiazem/verapamil. Consider digoxin when beta blockade/CCB is ineffective or contraindicated; IV amiodarone may be considered in the critically ill/decompensated-HF setting. | Amiodarone may cardiovert; consider stroke risk and anticoagulation plan. |
| Hemodynamically unstable AF/flutter | Synchronized cardioversion. | Do not delay for anticoagulation or TEE in a life-threatening situation. |
| AF with rapid preexcitation (e.g., WPW) | If stable, IV procainamide or ibutilide is recommended; if unstable, electrical cardioversion. | **Avoid AV-nodal blockers**: beta blockers, diltiazem, verapamil, digoxin, adenosine, and IV amiodarone. |

### Cardioversion and stroke prevention

For elective cardioversion of AF lasting **48 hours or longer**, guideline-directed options are at least 3 weeks of uninterrupted therapeutic anticoagulation or imaging to exclude intracardiac thrombus. Therapeutic anticoagulation should be established before cardioversion and continued for at least 4 weeks afterward, except when the emergent need for cardioversion outweighs delay. Reported duration below 48 hours does not eliminate thromboembolic risk; assess patient risk, not duration alone.

### Acute rate-control reference

| Agent | Guideline-listed IV regimen | Use/cautions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Metoprolol tartrate | **2.5–5 mg IV over 2 min; up to 3 doses** | Consider in stable AF/flutter if BP/perfusion and acute HF phenotype permit. |
| Diltiazem | **0.25 mg/kg IV over 2 min** | Use only when EF is >40%; avoid in moderate/severe LV systolic dysfunction, decompensated HFrEF, or suspected preexcitation. |
| Esmolol | **500 mcg/kg IV over 1 min, then 50–300 mcg/kg/min** | Short half-life supports rapid titration; use cautiously in shock/decompensated HF. |
| Digoxin | Dose and interval are patient-specific. | Consider when beta blocker/CCB is ineffective or contraindicated; account for renal function and slower onset. |
| Amiodarone | Follow local infusion protocol. | Consider for acute rate control only in critical illness/decompensated HF when beta blocker/CCB is ineffective or contraindicated; can facilitate cardioversion. |

## Wide-complex tachycardia and ventricular arrhythmias

### Stable regular monomorphic wide-complex tachycardia

Treat as VT unless a qualified reviewer establishes another diagnosis. The AHA algorithm permits adenosine only if the rhythm is **regular and monomorphic**. Otherwise use antiarrhythmic infusion and expert consultation while correcting ischemia/electrolyte or drug-related triggers.

| Medication | AHA adult algorithm dose | Key limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Procainamide | **20–50 mg/min IV** until suppression, hypotension, QRS increases >50%, or maximum **17 mg/kg**; then **1–4 mg/min** maintenance. | Avoid with prolonged QT or congestive HF. |
| Amiodarone | **150 mg IV over 10 min**; repeat if VT recurs, then **1 mg/min for the first 6 h**. | Hypotension, bradycardia, QT prolongation, and drug interactions; follow local protocol and cardiology guidance. |

### Polymorphic VT and torsades de pointes

- Sustained polymorphic VT is treated with **immediate unsynchronized shock**.
- For recurrences with a prolonged QT interval (torsades), correct precipitating causes, stop QT-prolonging drugs when possible, correct electrolytes, and consider IV magnesium per the resuscitation protocol.
- When polymorphic VT occurs without prolonged QT, evaluate for acute ischemia; IV lidocaine or amiodarone may be considered for recurrences along with ischemia treatment.

### Sustained VT after MI or in structural heart disease

Treat ischemia, decompensated heart failure, electrolyte disturbance, and proarrhythmic drug exposure in parallel with acute rhythm management. New sustained VT, recurrent ICD therapy, syncope, or ventricular dysfunction warrants early cardiology/EP involvement and consideration of ischemic evaluation and ablation strategy.

## Electrical storm

**Electrical storm** is recurrent sustained ventricular arrhythmia, recurrent appropriate ICD therapy, or repeated VT/VF episodes requiring urgent intervention. It is a critical-care emergency.

1. Call cardiology/EP and obtain immediate device interrogation; correct ischemia, electrolytes, HF/shock, infection, drug toxicity, and catecholamine excess.
2. For structural heart disease with electrical storm, use beta blockade (preferably nonselective when appropriate) plus IV amiodarone unless contraindicated; provide analgesia/sedation to blunt adrenergic drive.
3. Escalate refractory cases to catheter ablation, autonomic modulation, and mechanical circulatory support in coordination with EP, interventional cardiology, and the shock team.

> [!warning] **ICD shocks are not definitive treatment**
> Repeated shocks worsen adrenergic activation and may perpetuate VT. Interrogate/reprogram the device early while treating the arrhythmia and its precipitant.

## Medication quick reference

| Do | Avoid |
| --- | --- |
| Confirm rhythm, stability, QT, EF, kidney function, medication interactions, and anticoagulation status before selecting a drug. | Giving diltiazem/verapamil in known moderate/severe LV systolic dysfunction or decompensated HFrEF. |
| Treat a regular wide-complex rhythm as VT until proven otherwise. | Giving adenosine to irregular wide-complex tachycardia. |
| Use procainamide or ibutilide for stable preexcited AF; cardiovert if unstable. | Giving AV-nodal blockers—including amiodarone—to preexcited AF. |
| Replete electrolytes and discontinue reversible QT-prolonging agents when possible. | Treating a QT-prolonged polymorphic rhythm as routine monomorphic VT. |

## CCAPP clinical pearls

1. **Rate is not the diagnosis.** A narrow, regular rhythm at ~150/min may be flutter with 2:1 conduction; slow the AV node only after assessing the clinical context.
2. **The unstable patient needs electricity, not a prolonged medication sequence.** Synchronize when appropriate; defibrillate sustained polymorphic VT.
3. **Wide and regular is VT until proven otherwise.** A mistaken AV-nodal or calcium-channel blocker can convert diagnostic uncertainty into collapse.
4. **AF in the ICU is often a symptom.** Correct hypoxemia, volume/hemodynamic abnormalities, sepsis, pain, catecholamine exposure, and electrolyte derangements—not just the heart rate.
5. **Preexcited AF is a different emergency.** An irregular, very rapid wide-complex rhythm should trigger immediate avoidance of AV-nodal blockers and early expert involvement.
6. **Amiodarone is not a harmless “rate-control drip.”** It can hypotend, interact with warfarin/digoxin, prolong QT, and facilitate cardioversion; define the anticoagulation and rhythm strategy.
7. **Document the before-and-after rhythm.** Save a 12-lead ECG/telemetry strip before treatment whenever safe; it improves EP diagnosis and long-term planning.

## Evidence and guidelines

- The **2025 AHA Advanced Life Support guideline** is the primary source for adult tachyarrhythmia stabilization, cardioversion/defibrillation pathways, adenosine use, and stable wide-complex antiarrhythmic infusions.
- The **2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS AF guideline** guides acute AF rate control, cardioversion anticoagulation, and the specific danger of AV-nodal blockade in preexcited AF.
- The **2022 ESC ventricular-arrhythmia guideline** supports a multimodal electrical-storm approach, including antiarrhythmic therapy, beta blockade, sedation, device management, ablation, and circulatory support when needed.
