## Overview

**Mitral valve disease** encompasses both **mitral stenosis (MS)** and **mitral regurgitation (MR)**, which present fundamentally different pathophysiology and management challenges. MS is characterized by obstruction to left atrial emptying; MR (primary/organic or secondary/functional) involves inadequate valve closure allowing blood to regurgitate into the left atrium during systole. Acute severe MR—particularly from papillary muscle rupture post-myocardial infarction—represents a true cardiac emergency with mortality rates exceeding 50% without intervention. Chronic MR and MS present with more indolent but significant morbidity. In the ICU, precipitating factors such as acute ischemia, hemodynamic stress, arrhythmias, and infection often unmask or acutely worsen preexisting mitral pathology.

> [!tip] **Why This Matters**
> Acute mitral regurgitation can present insidiously—a quiet or absent murmur may mask severe disease. PA catheter V-waves, acute pulmonary edema, and rapid hemodynamic collapse are hallmarks. Emergency surgical consultation is the standard of care; medical stabilization buys time but is not definitive. Recognizing and acting on acute MR can mean the difference between salvage and mortality.

## Etiology & Risk Factors

### Mitral Stenosis

**Rheumatic Heart Disease** (most common worldwide; ~2% prevalence in US)
- Post-streptococcal sequela; progressive fusion and calcification of valve leaflets
- Presents 10–30 years after rheumatic fever
- Risk increased with female sex, recurrent streptococcal infections

**Non-Rheumatic MS**
- Degenerative calcification (age-related, similar to aortic stenosis)
- Radiation-induced fibrosis (mitral and tricuspid)
- Congenital abnormalities (parachute mitral valve, supravalvular ring)
- Antiphospholipid syndrome, systemic lupus erythematosus
- Infective endocarditis with vegetation obstruction (rare)

### Mitral Regurgitation: Primary (Organic)

**Degenerative MR**
- Myxomatous prolapse (Barlow disease, billowing leaflets)
- Fibroelastic deficiency (thin, atrophic leaflet tissue)
- Most common cause of organic MR in developed nations
- Risk factors: Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos, aneurysmal root disease

**Rheumatic Heart Disease**
- Leaflet retraction and calcification
- Often mixed stenosis-regurgitation pattern

**Endocarditis**
- Vegetation, perforation, or rupture of chordae tendineae
- Acute onset, high mortality

**Chordal/Papillary Muscle Pathology**
- Chordae tendineae rupture (spontaneous in myxomatous disease; post-traumatic or ischemic)
- Papillary muscle rupture post-MI (posteromedial PM more common; typically 2–7 days post-infarction)
- Papillary muscle dysfunction from prior infarction or cardiomyopathy

**Other Causes**
- Trauma (blunt chest injury, iatrogenic from biopsy or device placement)
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (SAM mechanism)

### Mitral Regurgitation: Secondary (Functional)

**Left Ventricular Dilation**
- Ischemic or nonischemic cardiomyopathy
- Tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy
- Dilated RV with annular dilation and leaflet tethering

**Left Atrial Enlargement**
- Chronic MS, chronic MR, atrial fibrillation
- LA dilation exceeds annular capacity

**Papillary Muscle Displacement**
- LV remodeling pulls papillary muscles apart; leaflet tethering occurs
- "Proportionate" (global LV dysfunction) vs. "disproportionate" (localized LV remodeling) patterns affect intervention outcomes

**Annular Calcification**
- Impaired leaflet motion; often coexists with AS

**ICU Precipitants of MR Worsening**
- Acute ischemia (posteromedial papillary muscle territory most vulnerable)
- Tachycardia and increased contractility (increased regurgitant flow)
- Increased afterload (diuretics inadequate; hypertensive crisis)
- Acute atrial fibrillation with RVR
- Positive pressure ventilation (increases LV afterload initially)

## Pathophysiology

### Acute vs. Chronic Mitral Regurgitation: A Critical Distinction

**Acute Severe MR**
- **Non-compliant left atrium** has not had time to enlarge; sudden volume overload causes sharp rise in LA pressure
- V-wave on PA catheter is prominent and abrupt
- Pulmonary edema develops rapidly (often with relatively mild left atrial dilation on imaging)
- Regurgitant volume is accommodated poorly; cardiac output drops acutely
- The left ventricle is **hyperdynamic** from acute volume overload but cannot sustain output if MR is massive
- Compensatory mechanisms (increased contractility, sympathetic tone) are maxed out within hours to days
- **Hemodynamic consequence: cardiogenic pulmonary edema + shock** — this is the ICU emergency
- Without intervention, 50–80% mortality within days

**Chronic Severe MR**
- **Compliant left atrium** has enlarged over months to years; accommodates regurgitant volume without steep pressure rise
- Pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) may remain normal or only mildly elevated at rest
- V-waves are present but not dramatically pathologic relative to the enlarged cavity
- LV remodeling accommodates increased wall stress through eccentric hypertrophy
- Increased total stroke volume (forward + regurgitant) supports cardiac output despite MR
- Symptoms develop insidiously; patients tolerate severe MR for years
- **Hemodynamic consequence: slowly progressive LV dysfunction, atrial fibrillation, eventual decompensation**
- Asymptomatic patients with normal LV function can be managed medically; intervention is elective

### Mitral Stenosis Pathophysiology

**Fixed Obstruction**
- Narrowed mitral valve area (MVA) creates pressure gradient between LA and LV
- Normal MVA is 4–6 cm²; critical stenosis is <1 cm²
- Gradient is flow-dependent: higher HR or cardiac output widens the gradient

**Heart Rate Dependence**
- Tachycardia shortens diastole → less time for mitral inflow → LA pressure rises abruptly
- Atrial fibrillation with RVR is particularly dangerous (lose atrial kick + tachycardia effect)
- Conversely, rate control (or sinus rhythm restoration) can dramatically improve LA hemodynamics

**Pulmonary Hypertension**
- Chronic elevation of LA pressure → pulmonary venous congestion → reactive pulmonary arteriolar vasoconstriction
- Precapillary PH can develop and partially persist even if MS is later corrected
- RV strain and right heart failure ensue; tricuspid regurgitation worsens

**Reduced Cardiac Output**
- Fixed MVA limits LV filling → reduced LV preload → reduced stroke volume
- Unlike MR, LV function is typically normal or supernormal (small, underfilled cavity)
- Cardiac output becomes "fixed" at a lower level; exercise intolerance is cardinal

**ICU Consequences of MS**
- Atrial fibrillation is common; loss of sinus rhythm precipitates acute decompensation
- Tachycardia from infection, pain, or agitation → rapid LA pressure rise → pulmonary edema
- Diuretics provide symptomatic relief but can worsen hemodynamics if overdone (reduced preload → further ↓CO)
- Beta-blockers and non-DHP CCBs are essential; vasodilators alone are insufficient

## Clinical Presentation

### Acute Mitral Regurgitation (Papillary Muscle Rupture Post-MI)

| Finding | Clinical Significance |
|---------|----------------------|
| **Sudden dyspnea, orthopnea, PND** | Acute pulmonary edema from non-compliant LA |
| **Hypotension, cool extremities** | Cardiogenic shock; CO drops acutely with severe MR |
| **Soft or absent systolic murmur** | Classic pitfall! Massive regurgitant flow → equalization of LV-LA pressure → no gradient → quiet murmur |
| **Displaced apical impulse, hyperdynamic precordium** | LV hyperkinesis from acute volume overload |
| **New S3, bibasilar crackles** | Heart failure; pulmonary edema |
| **Elevated JVP with prominent S/V waves** | RA hypertension from RV strain |
| **Recent MI history (2–7 days), ongoing chest pain/recurrent ischemia** | High suspicion for papillary muscle rupture |

**Red Flags**
- Acute pulmonary edema unresponsive to diuretics alone → think acute MR
- Cardiogenic shock out of proportion to LV infarct size → consider mechanical complication
- Soft or absent murmur does NOT rule out severe MR
- Hemodynamic instability despite aggressive vasopressor use → MR worsens with increased afterload

### Chronic Mitral Regurgitation

| Presentation | Severity Correlation |
|--------------|----------------------|
| **Asymptomatic, normal LV size** | Mild-moderate MR; can observe |
| **Dyspnea on exertion, fatigue** | Moderate-severe MR; LV dilation developing |
| **Orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea** | Severe MR with pulmonary congestion |
| **Palpitations, syncope** | Atrial fibrillation (most common); or decreased CO with exercise |
| **Holosystolic murmur, displaced apical impulse** | Classic MR findings; correlate with severity on echo |

**History Pearls**
- Ask about family history of early valve disease, sudden cardiac death (suggests myxomatous disease, Marfan syndrome)
- Progressive exertional dyspnea over months to years (vs. acute dyspnea = acute MR)
- Palpitations (often AF-related)
- Prior rheumatic fever, rheumatic carditis

### Mitral Stenosis

| Finding | Correlation |
|---------|-------------|
| **Dyspnea on exertion, orthopnea** | Symptom threshold depends on HR and CO; can be precipitous if AF develops |
| **Fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance** | Limited CO; worse with tachycardia |
| **Palpitations, hemoptysis** | AF (common) or acute pulmonary edema from LA pressure surge |
| **Malar flush, low-volume pulses** | Signs of chronic MS (low CO, pulmonary hypertension) |
| **Loud S1, opening snap, diastolic rumble** | Classic MS findings; rumble longer and louder with more severe stenosis |
| **RV heave, loud P2 (pulmonary HTN)** | Indicates severe MS with secondary PH |
| **Atrial fibrillation** | Present in ~50% chronic MS; dramatically worsens symptoms |

**Red Flags**
- Sudden onset dyspnea + hemoptysis → acute pulmonary edema from LA pressure surge (often from new AF with RVR)
- Syncope with exertion → critical stenosis limiting CO; at risk for sudden decompensation

## Diagnostic Workup

### Echocardiography (TTE/TEE)

**Transthoracic Echocardiography (TTE)**
- First-line; assess valve anatomy, function, gradients, chamber dimensions, LV function, pulmonary pressures
- Often diagnostic for MR; MVA measurement less accurate if suboptimal acoustic windows

**Transesophageal Echocardiography (TEE)**
- **Indications for acute MR:**
  - Hemodynamically unstable patient being considered for emergent surgery (superior views of papillary muscle, chordae, LV outflow tract)
  - Preoperative planning if surgical anatomy unclear
  - Evaluation for endocarditis (vegetations, dehiscence, abscess)
  - Assessment of secondary MR (papillary muscle tethering pattern, annular dimensions)
- **Indications for chronic MR:**
  - Indeterminate TTE; guiding intervention (MitraClip candidacy, anatomy assessment)
- Provides superior assessment of leaflet pathology, chordal rupture, and LA appendage

#### Mitral Regurgitation Severity Grading

| Grade | Jet Area / LA Area | Regurgitant Volume | Regurgitant Fraction |
|-------|--------------------|--------------------|----------------------|
| **Mild** | <20% | <30 mL | <30% |
| **Moderate** | 20–40% | 30–60 mL | 30–50% |
| **Moderately Severe** | 40–60% | 60–120 mL | 50–60% |
| **Severe** | >60% | >120 mL | >60% |

**Multiparametric Assessment:**
- Color Doppler jet area (user-dependent; affected by loading conditions, Nyquist limit)
- Regurgitant volume and fraction (more accurate, less preload-dependent)
- Vena contracta (narrowest point of jet at valve leaflets; vena contracta >6 mm = severe)
- Left atrial and LV dimensions
- LV systolic function (LVEF); LVFS (LV fractional shortening)
- Pulmonary vein flow pattern (reversal or blunting in systole = severe MR)

#### Mitral Stenosis Severity Grading

| Grade | Valve Area | Mean Gradient | PASP |
|-------|-----------|----------------|----|
| **Mild MS** | >1.5 cm² | <5 mmHg | Normal |
| **Moderate MS** | 1.0–1.5 cm² | 5–10 mmHg | Mild-mod. ↑ |
| **Severe MS** | <1.0 cm² | >10 mmHg | Moderate-severe ↑ |

**Key Measurements:**
- **Mitral valve area (MVA)**: Planimetry (direct measurement) or Doppler continuous-wave gradient → pressure half-time (PHT); PHT less accurate if MR, AR, or AF present
- **Mean diastolic gradient**: Integrated CW Doppler envelope; >10 mmHg suggests severe stenosis
- **PASP (Pulmonary Artery Systolic Pressure)**: Tricuspid regurgitation jet velocity + RA pressure; secondary to LA hypertension in MS
- **Left atrial size**: Often enlarged; AF common if LA >40 mm

### PA Catheter Findings

**Acute Mitral Regurgitation**
- **Prominent V-waves** (steep, tall wave in PCWP or LA tracing)
- V-waves may exceed 40–50 mmHg (or be higher than the peak of the A-wave)
- Reduced cardiac output, elevated PCWP (often 20–30 mmHg)
- **Caveat:** V-waves are not pathognomonic for MR; seen in left ventricular rupture, ventricular septal rupture, severe TR, and with certain catheter positions. Distinguish by other clinical clues and imaging.

**Chronic Mitral Regurgitation**
- V-waves present but proportionate to LA dilation
- Variable PCWP depending on LA compliance and LV function

**Mitral Stenosis**
- Elevated PCWP with prominent A-waves (LA contraction against stenotic valve)
- Reduced cardiac output
- Discordance between PCWP and LV end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) is typical: PCWP ↑, LVEDP normal or low
- Pulmonary artery pressure elevated (secondary to LA hypertension)

### Laboratory Studies

| Test | Role in MV Disease |
|------|-------------------|
| **Troponin, CK-MB** | Detect MI; elevated in acute MR from papillary muscle rupture |
| **BNP/NT-proBNP** | Elevated in acute/decompensated MR or MS; prognostic marker; guides diuretic titration |
| **CBC** | Baseline; assess for anemia (worsens heart failure symptoms) |
| **BMP (K, Cr, eGFR)** | Diuretic and vasodilator dosing; renal function affects prognosis |
| **LFTs, albumin** | Hepatic congestion from elevated JVP (RV failure) |
| **Lactate** | Cardiogenic shock assessment; elevated in acute MR with hypoperfusion |
| **PT/INR, PTT** | If considering TEER or surgery (anticoagulation planning) |
| **Blood cultures** | If endocarditis suspected (fever, embolic phenomena, new regurgitation) |

---

### Chest X-Ray

- **Acute MR:** Bilateral infiltrates (pulmonary edema), possible Kerley B lines, enlarged LA (straightened left heart border)
- **Chronic MR:** LA and LV enlargement, signs of heart failure if decompensated
- **MS:** LA enlargement, pulmonary vascular redistribution (upper lobe prominence), possible pulmonary edema

---

### Other Imaging

**Cardiac CT/CMR**
- CMR for myocardial viability assessment post-MI (helps predict outcome of revascularization vs. mechanical complication)
- CT for rapid diagnosis in unstable patient (valvular anatomy, chamber size, exclude other pathology)

## Management

### Acute Mitral Regurgitation: Medical Stabilization

> [!warning] **Time-Critical Diagnosis**
> Acute severe MR from papillary muscle rupture is a surgical emergency. Medical therapy buys time—hours to days—but is not definitive. Delay in surgical consultation increases mortality. If papillary muscle rupture is suspected, activate cardiac surgery immediately while initiating medical therapy.

#### Goals of Acute MR Medical Management
1. **Reduce regurgitant volume** → reduce afterload, improve forward CO, reduce pulmonary congestion
2. **Reduce LV preload** → decrease LA pressure, mitigate pulmonary edema
3. **Optimize contractility** if cardiogenic shock present, avoiding increased afterload
4. **Mechanical support** if medical therapy inadequate and surgery is delayed or contraindicated

#### Interventions: Acute Mitral Regurgitation

| Intervention | Mechanism | Dosing | Evidence & Notes |
|--------------|-----------|--------|------------------|
| **Noninvasive Positive Pressure Ventilation (CPAP/BiPAP)** | ↓ WOB, ↓ LV afterload via PEEP → ↓ regurgitant fraction, improves forward CO | 5–10 cm H₂O PEEP; titrate FiO₂ for SpO₂ ≥90% | **Therapeutic, not just supportive.** PEEP particularly beneficial (reduces end-systolic volume → less regurgitation). If fails, intubate and place on IMV with elevated PEEP (10–15 cm H₂O). **References:** Observational ICU series show rapid improvement in oxygenation and hemodynamics. |
| **Invasive Mechanical Ventilation** | Sedation → sync with ventilator; PEEP ↑ intrathoracic pressure → ↓ LV afterload; reduce work of breathing | TV 6–8 mL/kg IBW; PEEP 10–15 cm H₂O; FiO₂ to SpO₂ ≥90% | Reduces LV afterload more predictably than NIV if patient is hypoxic or unable to cooperate. Avoid excessive tidal volumes (increase intrathoracic pressure excessively). |
| **Vasodilators (IV Nicardipine)** | ↓ systemic vascular resistance → ↓ LV afterload → ↓ regurgitant volume, improves forward CO | 0.5–2.0 mg/hr IV, titrate by 0.5 mg/hr q5–15min to SBP goal (95–120 mmHg); max 15 mg/hr | **First-line if hypertensive.** Rapid, titratable onset/offset. Avoid excessive drop in SBP (worsens forward CO). Goal is to reduce afterload while maintaining adequate perfusion pressure. |
| **Vasodilators (IV Nitroprusside)** | ↓ preload + ↓ afterload (balanced effect) | 0.5–10 µg/kg/min IV infusion; titrate by 0.5 µg/kg/min q5–10min | **Second-line** (risk of cyanide toxicity with prolonged use >4 h or high-dose). Potent but less selective than nicardipine. Use if hypertensive crisis. |
| **Diuretics (IV Furosemide)** | ↓ intravascular volume → ↓ LA pressure, mitigates pulmonary edema | 10–40 mg IV bolus; repeat q2–4h or continuous infusion 5–20 mg/hr; titrate to UOP goal | **Essential.** Reduces PCWP and alleviates congestion. Avoid over-diuresis (reduces preload excessively → ↓ forward CO in some patients). Monitor K⁺, Cr. |
| **Intra-Aortic Balloon Pump (IABP)** | ↓ systemic vascular resistance, improves diastolic coronary perfusion → ↓ regurgitant fraction, ↑ forward CO | Timing 1:1 with cardiac cycle; Helium or CO₂ inflated balloons | **Physiologically sound; evidence is observational.** Multiple case series report dramatic hemodynamic improvement; MR patients identified as "super-responders." No RCT in acute MR population. **Limitations:** Contraindicated in aortic regurgitation; requires stable rhythm; vascular access complications. Consider if medical therapy plateauing and awaiting surgery. |
| **Percutaneous Mechanical Support (Impella, VA-ECMO)** | Impella: ↓ LV afterload via active unloading; VA-ECMO: systemic support + oxygenation | Impella CP: 2.5–3.5 L/min; VA-ECMO: 3.5–4.5 L/min, FiO₂/sweep gas titrated | **Bridge to surgery or decision-making tool.** VA-ECMO allows time for full stabilization, cardiopulmonary recovery, ICU optimization before definitive intervention. **References:** Recent case series (VV-ECMO + M-TEER in acute post-MI papillary muscle rupture) show feasibility. |

> [!tip] **Positive Pressure Ventilation: Dual Benefit in Acute MR**
> PPV reduces work of breathing AND reduces LV afterload via PEEP, thereby reducing the regurgitant fraction and improving forward cardiac output. This is not merely "supportive"—it is a therapeutic intervention. Apply PEEP deliberately (10–15 cm H₂O on invasive ventilation) and watch hemodynamics improve in many patients with acute MR.

> [!warning] **Vasopressor Warning**
> Avoid pure alpha-agonists (phenylephrine, norepinephrine at high doses). Increasing afterload worsens MR by increasing LV end-systolic volume and regurgitant fraction. If inotropic support is needed, use **low-dose dobutamine** (2–5 µg/kg/min, not exceeding 5) to reduce afterload while maintaining contractility—but only as a bridge. Definitive therapy is always surgery for acute papillary muscle rupture MR.

> [!warning] **IABP Evidence Caveat**
> IABP improves hemodynamics in acute MR, but evidence is based on observational data, translational models, and case series. MR patients are identified as "super-responders." However, no RCT of IABP in acute post-MI MR exists. Use IABP judiciously as a temporizing measure while preparing for emergency surgery.

### Acute Mitral Regurgitation: Definitive Intervention

#### Emergency Mitral Valve Surgery: Standard of Care

**Indications**
- Acute severe MR with cardiogenic shock unresponsive to medical therapy and mechanical support
- Acute severe MR with refractory pulmonary edema
- Papillary muscle rupture (nearly 100% of cases require surgery)
- Large chordal rupture with hemodynamic compromise

**Operative Mortality**
- **25–30% operative mortality** in acute post-MI papillary muscle rupture series (reflects high acuity, comorbidities)
- Mortality higher if shock present, ECMO-supported, older age, extensive infarct
- **However, medical management alone is associated with >50% mortality within days**
- Surgery remains the standard of care despite high operative risk

**Surgical Options**
1. **Mitral valve repair** (preferred if anatomy suitable; ~50–70% of acute MR cases)
   - Papillary muscle reimplantation, chordal transfer, or leaflet edge repair
   - Preserves native valve; better long-term outcomes
2. **Mitral valve replacement** (if repair not feasible)
   - Mechanical prosthesis (durability, requires anticoagulation)
   - Bioprosthesis (lower anticoagulation burden; risk of degeneration in younger patients)
   - Higher operative mortality than repair in acute setting

**Key Points**
- **Do not delay surgical consultation** while optimizing medically
- Goal: transfer to OR within 24–48 hours if patient stable enough for transport
- IABP and VA-ECMO can bridge to surgery if transport or ICU optimization needed
- Redo surgery rates are low after successful acute repair (~2–5% at 5 years)

---

#### Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair (TEER/MitraClip): Role in Acute MR

**Current Evidence & Limitations**
- Evidence for acute MR is **limited to case series and registries**—no RCTs
- **Largest published series** (post-MI papillary muscle rupture, all deemed at "prohibitive surgical risk"):
  - 87% procedural success rate
  - 30% in-hospital mortality (vs. ~25–30% surgical mortality; rates are comparable but post-MI surgical candidates are selected lower-risk)
  - 22% subsequently underwent elective surgical mitral valve replacement (TEER as bridge or temporizing measure)
- **Other recent series** (Putti et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026) report successful M-TEER as emergency bail-out after surgical repair failure and in conjunction with mechanical support (VA-ECMO)

**Candidacy for Acute MR TEER**
- **Guideline-directed practice:** TEER is indicated for **prohibitive surgical risk only**
- Patients who refuse surgery, are too frail, have contraindications (severe liver disease, respiratory failure dependent on vasopressors, etc.)
- Bridge to surgical decision-making if patient stabilizes on ECMO
- **NOT a substitute for surgery** in operable acute papillary muscle rupture; defer to cardiothoracic surgery first

**Advantages of Acute TEER**
- Percutaneous approach; can be performed at bedside with TEE guidance
- No need for general anesthesia, CPB; may tolerate better in shocked patients
- Rapid reduction of MR may improve hemodynamics within hours

**Disadvantages of Acute TEER**
- **Technical challenges:** Anatomically distorted valve (flail leaflet, ruptured papillary muscle), small/remodeled LA, hemodynamic instability make procedure technically harder
- **Procedural success lower in acute MR** than chronic MR (~87% vs. >95%)
- **Post-procedure MR grades:** Residual moderate MR not uncommon; may require multiple clips or escalation to surgery
- **Learning curve:** Requires high-volume center; not available at all institutions
- **Time cost:** Preparation, transport to cath lab, procedure duration may exceed time to surgical OR

**Decision-Making Framework: Acute MR**
1. **Presumed operable patient** → emergency surgical consultation; prepare for OR. TEER is not first-line.
2. **Prohibitive surgical risk** (patient refuses, extreme age/frailty, uncorrectable coagulopathy, etc.) → consider TEER if high-volume center available; can be done while on IABP or VA-ECMO.
3. **Hemodynamically deteriorating despite ECMO support** → TEER may buy time; dual decision with surgery/ECMO team.

> [!warning] **Acute MR TEER: Evidence Caveat**
> Do not use TEER as first-line therapy for acute post-MI papillary muscle rupture. Operative mortality for acute MR surgery (~25–30%) is not prohibitive, and surgery is the standard of care. Reserve TEER for patients with truly prohibitive surgical risk. The largest acute MR TEER series (87% procedural success, 30% mortality) involved patients explicitly deemed unsuitable for surgery—not representative of all-comers with acute MR.

### Chronic Mitral Regurgitation Management

#### Indications for Intervention (ACC/AHA 2020)

**Surgical Repair/Replacement Indicated If:**
- **Symptomatic** patients with severe MR (LVEF >30%, LVESD <55 mm) — Class IIa
- **Asymptomatic** with:
  - LV dysfunction (LVEF 31–60% or LVESD ≥40 mm) — Class I
  - Atrial fibrillation — Class IIa
  - Pulmonary hypertension (sPAP >50 mmHg) — Class IIa
- Repair superior to replacement; MVP candidates are ~75% repairable

**Watchful Waiting (Medical Management) If:**
- Asymptomatic, normal LV function (LVEF >60%, LVESD <40 mm), normal LV filling pressures
- **Serial echo every 6–12 months** to detect early LV dysfunction

#### Medical Management

| Drug Class | Agent | Dosing | Goal | Notes |
|-----------|-------|--------|------|-------|
| **ACE-I/ARB** | Lisinopril, enalapril, losartan | Lisinopril: 5–40 mg daily; titrate to LVEF, BP, Cr | Target LVEF improvement, afterload reduction | First-line for asymptomatic MR with normal LVEF or mild LV dysfunction. Slows progression. |
| **Beta-blocker** | Carvedilol, metoprolol | Carvedilol: 3.125–25 mg BID; titrate slowly | HR 50–60 bpm, SBP 100–120 mmHg | If LVEF <50% (HFrEF); slow HR reduces regurgitant volume. |
| **MRA** | Spironolactone, eplerenone | Spironolactone: 12.5–50 mg daily; monitor K⁺ | K⁺ <5.0 mEq/L, Cr stable | Add if LVEF <40% and symptomatic (HF). |
| **ARNI** | Sacubitril/valsartan (Entresto) | 24–97 mg BID (sacubitril/valsartan) | Reduce afterload, slow LV remodeling | Consider if HFrEF (LVEF <40%) and symptomatic; emerging evidence for slowing progression. |
| **Diuretics** | Furosemide, torsemide | Titrate to euvolemia (no orthopnea, minimal DOE) | Euvolemia | Use only if volume overloaded; avoid excessive diuresis (reduces CO). |

**Anticoagulation**
- **Warfarin (INR 2–3)** or DOAC if atrial fibrillation present
- No role for anticoagulation in chronic MR without AF or other indication

**Monitoring**
- Clinical exam for signs of decompensation
- **Echocardiography:** Repeat annually if moderate-severe MR; q2y if mild
- When to reassess for surgery:
  - New dyspnea on exertion
  - LVEF decline to <50%
  - LV dilation (LVESD >40 mm)
  - AF development
  - Pulmonary HTN on Echo (sPAP >50 mmHg)

### Secondary (Functional) Mitral Regurgitation

**Goals**
- Treat underlying LV dysfunction (ischemic or nonischemic cardiomyopathy)
- Reverse remodeling and reduce MR burden via neurohormonal blockade

**Medical Optimization**
- Guideline-directed medical therapy for HFrEF (ACE-I/ARB/ARNI, beta-blocker, MRA, SGLT2i, finerenone)
- Treat ischemic cardiomyopathy: revascularization if feasible
- Rate control in AF
- Biventricular pacing (CRT) if QRS ≥120 ms and LVEF ≤35% (reduces LV dilation → improves MR)

**Intervention Indications (COAPT vs. MITRA-FR Distinction)**
- **Guideline-directed approach (ACC/AHA 2020):**
  - **"Disproportionate" secondary MR** (COAPT phenotype: moderate-severe MR in context of LV dysfunction but less global dilation) → **MitraClip may benefit** (Class IIb, LOE B-R from COAPT trial)
  - **"Proportionate" secondary MR** (MITRA-FR phenotype: severe LV dilation with MR proportionate to degree of remodeling) → **MitraClip not recommended** (MITRA-FR trial showed no benefit)
  - Key distinction: degree of MR relative to LV dysfunction and LA size

**COAPT vs. MITRA-FR Trials** (2019–2020)
- **COAPT:** 614 patients with moderate-severe/severe MR, LVEF 26–55%, PCWP >18 mmHg despite optimal HFrEF therapy. MitraClip reduced hospitalizations and improved symptoms vs. medical therapy alone. Predominantly disproportionate MR phenotype.
- **MITRA-FR:** 372 patients with severe MR, LVEF ≤40%, mostly proportionate MR (large LA and dilated LV). No benefit of MitraClip on primary endpoint.
- **Clinical pearls:** Predict response by assessing LA size, regurgitant volume, and degree of LV remodeling; multidisciplinary team discussion (cardiology, cardiothoracic surgery, structural heart) recommended.

**MitraClip Technical Considerations**
- Procedural success >95% in chronic degenerative MR; lower (~87%) in acute MR
- Residual MR grade ≥2 is not uncommon (20–30% of cases); may require additional clips
- AF-related outcomes: no increase in de novo AF; symptom control often improves even if AF persists
- Antithrombotic strategy: **SAPT (aspirin monotherapy) superior to DAPT at 12 months** post-TEER (Muscoli et al., 2026 MitraSafe study); lower mortality (94% vs. 78.7% freedom from all-cause death) and reduced bleeding in SAPT cohort

### Mitral Stenosis: ICU Management

#### Acute Decompensation: Heart Failure from MS

**Goals**
1. **Rate control** → restore diastolic filling time → reduce LA pressure
2. **Reduce preload** → diuresis
3. **Maintain forward CO** (cautious diuresis; avoid hypovolemia)

| Intervention | Dosing | Mechanism | Notes |
|--------------|--------|-----------|-------|
| **Beta-blocker: Metoprolol** | 25–100 mg daily (div. BID); IV 5 mg bolus x 3 (q5min) | ↓ HR → ↑ diastolic filling time, ↓ LA pressure gradient | First-line for rate control. Target HR 60–70 bpm. Achieves effect within minutes (IV) to hours (PO). |
| **Non-DHP CCB: Diltiazem** | 30–120 mg daily (div. BID–TID); IV 0.25 mg/kg bolus over 2 min, then 5–15 mg/hr infusion | ↓ HR + negative inotropy; ↓ contractility may worsen CO initially but ↓ LA pressure | Alternative if beta-blocker contraindicated. IV onset ~2–5 min. Watch for hypotension, bradycardia. |
| **Digoxin** | Loading: 0.5–1.0 mg PO div. (0.25 mg q6h x 4–5 doses); maintenance 0.125–0.375 mg daily | Positive inotrope + AV nodal slowing; ↓ HR in AF | Slower onset (hours). Use if AF + reduced LV function (HF). Narrow therapeutic window; monitor levels, renal function. |
| **IV Furosemide** | 10–40 mg bolus; repeat q2–4h or continuous 5–20 mg/hr | ↓ intravascular volume → ↓ LA pressure | Essential for pulmonary edema. **Caution:** over-diuresis → ↓ preload → ↓ CO, worsens symptoms. Target euvolemia. |
| **Vasodilators** | Nicardipine, nitroprusside (see acute MR dosing) | ↓ afterload, pulmonary vascular resistance | **Limited role in MS.** Do not use as monotherapy (vasodilators do not address the fixed obstructive lesion). Use only if hypertensive and as adjunct to diuretics + rate control. |

> [!warning] **Vasodilators Alone Insufficient in MS**
> MS is a fixed anatomic obstruction. Vasodilation without rate control will worsen outcomes by increasing CO → increasing gradient across stenotic valve → worsening pulmonary edema. **Always pair vasodilators with rate-control agents and diuretics.**

#### Definitive Intervention in MS

**Percutaneous Mitral Balloon Commissurotomy (PMBC)**
- **Indication:** Symptomatic MS (NYHA ≥II) with favorable anatomy (leaflet thickening score <8, no LAA thrombus, no MR >2+)
- **Procedure:** Balloon catheter across mitral valve; inflate to split fused commissures
- **Success:** ~90% achieve MVA increase to >1.5 cm²; symptom improvement dramatic
- **Durability:** ~50–60% remain free of restenosis at 10 years
- **Complications:** Cardiac perforation, severe MR from commissural tear (2–5%), systemic embolism (anticoagulate pre-procedure)

**Surgical Mitral Valve Replacement/Commissurotomy**
- Reserved for:
  - Failed PMBC or unfavorable anatomy for PMBC
  - Severe MR coexisting with MS (can't separate treatment)
  - Recurrent restenosis after PMBC
  - Acute MS with mechanical complication (ruptured papillary muscle, etc.)

#### ICU Scenarios & Decision-Making in MS

| Scenario | Approach |
|----------|----------|
| **Hemodynamically stable MS, new AF with RVR** | IV beta-blocker (metoprolol 5 mg x 3) or IV diltiazem; IV furosemide PRN for congestion. Consider heparin (systemic anticoagulation risk post-MI if applicable). Once rate-controlled, can proceed with PMBC if symptomatic. |
| **Acute pulmonary edema from MS despite medical therapy** | Aggressive diuresis + IV beta-blocker/CCB. If fails, prepare for emergent PMBC (highest acuity patients) or surgical commissurotomy. Respiratory support (CPAP or intubation) as needed. |
| **MS + severe LV dysfunction** | Complex case. LV dysfunction may be from diastolic dysfunction (elevated LVEDP from impaired relaxation) or from prior ischemia. Optimize rate control + careful diuresis. Consult cardiothoracic surgery before PMBC (may need valve replacement instead). |
| **Hemoptysis in MS + AF** | Suggests acute LA pressure surge from AF with rapid ventricular response. Emergent rate control (IV beta-blocker or CCB) + diuretics. If recurrent, expedite PMBC. |

## Clinical Pearls

> [!tip]
> 1. **The quiet murmur of acute severe MR:** A patient with acute post-MI cardiogenic shock may have a soft, difficult-to-hear systolic murmur or no murmur at all. Equalization of LV-LA pressures in acute MR eliminates the gradient needed for a loud murmur. Listen carefully but do not rely on murmur absence to exclude acute MR. Suspect papillary muscle rupture if acute pulmonary edema + shock post-MI.

> [!tip]
> 2. **V-waves are not pathognomonic for MR:** Prominent V-waves on PA catheter can also indicate left ventricular rupture, ventricular septal rupture, or severe tricuspid regurgitation. Always integrate PA data with imaging (echocardiography, TEE). A "positive deflection" on PA tracing should prompt echo confirmation.

> [!tip]
> 3. **Positive pressure ventilation is therapeutic in acute MR, not just supportive:** PEEP reduces LV end-systolic volume and thereby decreases regurgitant fraction. Apply PEEP deliberately (10–15 cm H₂O on invasive ventilation); watch hemodynamics improve in many patients before escalating to mechanical support or surgery.

> [!tip]
> 4. **Do not delay cardiac surgery consultation in acute MR:** Medical optimization (vasodilators, diuretics, PPV, IABP) buys time—often 24–48 hours. But surgical consultation should happen within hours, not days. Redo surgeries (elective repair after palliative TEER, for example) are more morbid. Get definitive repair/replacement in the acute phase if feasible.

> [!tip]
> 5. **IABP is helpful in acute MR but not a substitute for surgery:** IABP improves hemodynamics acutely (reduces afterload, improves coronary flow). However, evidence is observational. IABP is contraindicated in aortic regurgitation; don't place blindly. Use as a bridge to OR, not as definitive therapy.

> [!tip]
> 6. **Chronic MR with normal LV function can be observed:** Do not operate on asymptomatic patients with severe MR if LVEF >60%, LVESD <40 mm, and normal LA pressure. Serial echocardiography (q1–2 years) is appropriate. Intervene when LV dysfunction develops (LVEF 30–60%, LVESD ≥40 mm, or AF/PH).

> [!tip]
> 7. **Rate control is essential in mitral stenosis; vasodilators alone are insufficient:** MS is a fixed obstruction. Shortening diastolic filling time (tachycardia) increases the LA-LV gradient. Beta-blockers or non-DHP CCBs are first-line. Vasodilators should be reserved for hypertensive patients and paired with diuretics + rate control.

> [!tip]
> 8. **Secondary MR phenotyping matters:** "Proportionate" secondary MR (severe LV dilation) does not benefit from MitraClip (MITRA-FR), while "disproportionate" secondary MR (moderate MR relative to degree of LV dysfunction) may benefit (COAPT). Involve cardiology and cardiothoracic surgery in decision-making to avoid futile intervention.

## Evidence & Landmark Trials

### Landmark Clinical Trials

#### EVEREST II (2011)

- **Population:** 279 patients with moderate-to-severe or severe MR (structural/degenerative cause; excluded functional MR)
- **Intervention:** MitraClip (edge-to-edge repair) vs. standard surgical repair
- **Primary outcome:** Composite of death, MR ≥2+, and need for ≥2 clips
- **Result:** MitraClip was non-inferior to surgery for MR reduction; fewer adverse events in MitraClip arm; 95% of MitraClip patients achieved MR <2+ at 12 months
- **Clinical impact:** Established MitraClip as safe and effective for degenerative MR in operable patients; validated percutaneous edge-to-edge repair concept

#### COAPT (2018)

- **Population:** 614 patients with moderate-to-severe or severe secondary MR, LVEF 26–55%, PCWP >18 mmHg despite optimal HFrEF therapy
- **Intervention:** MitraClip + medical therapy vs. medical therapy alone
- **Primary outcome:** Hospitalization for heart failure at 24 months
- **Result:** MitraClip reduced HF hospitalizations by 46%; mortality trended lower (not significantly different). Secondary MR patients with disproportionate pattern (moderate-severe MR relative to LV size) saw greatest benefit.
- **Clinical impact:** MitraClip approved for secondary MR in appropriately selected patients; demonstrated benefit in HF population with elevated filling pressures

#### MITRA-FR (2018)

- **Population:** 372 patients with severe secondary MR, LVEF ≤40%, mostly proportionate MR (large LA, dilated LV)
- **Intervention:** MitraClip + medical therapy vs. medical therapy alone
- **Primary outcome:** Composite of death or unplanned hospitalization for HF at 12 months
- **Result:** No significant difference between groups; MitraClip did not reduce primary endpoint
- **Clinical impact:** Demonstrated that not all secondary MR patients benefit from MitraClip; phenotyping (proportionate vs. disproportionate) is critical for selecting candidates

**Distinction: COAPT vs. MITRA-FR**
- **COAPT patients** had more moderate MR with less dilated LA; MitraClip benefit seen
- **MITRA-FR patients** had more severe dilated cardiomyopathy with proportionate MR; no benefit
- **Clinical lesson:** Screen for COAPT phenotype (moderate regurgitant volume, less severe LV/LA dilation) before offering MitraClip for secondary MR

## Key Guidelines

### ACC/AHA 2020 Guidelines for the Management of Valvular Heart Disease
- **Mitral Regurgitation:**
  - Asymptomatic severe MR with normal LV function → watchful waiting (Class I, LOE C)
  - Symptomatic severe MR → repair preferred; surgery Class I (LOE C)
  - Asymptomatic severe MR + LV dysfunction (LVEF 30–60% or LVESD ≥40 mm) → surgery Class I (LOE C)
  - TEER (MitraClip) for secondary MR: Class IIb, LOE B-R (modest evidence, disproportionate MR phenotype preferred based on COAPT)

- **Mitral Stenosis:**
  - PMBC for symptomatic moderate-to-severe MS with favorable anatomy → Class I (LOE B)
  - Surgical mitral valve replacement for MS with unfavorable anatomy or failed PMBC → Class I (LOE C)
  - Rate control (beta-blocker, non-DHP CCB) in MS with AF → Class I (LOE C)

### AHA Scientific Statement on Mechanical Complications of Acute Myocardial Infarction (2021)
- Papillary muscle rupture is a rare (0.1–0.2% of MI) but catastrophic complication
- Mortality with medical therapy alone >50% within 24–48 hours
- Emergency cardiac surgery is standard of care; operative mortality 25–30%
- IABP and percutaneous mechanical support (Impella, ECMO) can be used as bridges to definitive surgery
- Transcatheter interventions (TEER) are emerging but should be reserved for prohibitive surgical risk cases
