# Valvular Heart Disease in the ICU

## Overview

Valvular heart disease (VHD) in the critical care setting presents in two broad scenarios: patients with known VHD who decompensate due to a superimposed illness, and patients presenting acutely with new or catastrophic valve dysfunction (e.g., acute mitral regurgitation from papillary muscle rupture, acute aortic regurgitation from endocarditis or aortic dissection). Both demand rapid hemodynamic assessment, tailored medical stabilization, and early cardiology and cardiothoracic surgery involvement.

The cardiac ICU provider must understand the underlying physiology of each valve lesion, how critical illness alters hemodynamics, and when medical management is failing and intervention is urgently needed. Unlike elective VHD management, the ICU setting offers limited time for optimization — decisions about escalation to surgery or transcatheter intervention must be made under hemodynamic duress.

> [!warning] Why This Matters
> Acute decompensated valvular disease accounts for a significant proportion of cardiac ICU admissions. Mismanagement — particularly inappropriate vasopressors, aggressive diuresis, or delay in surgical referral — can be fatal. Know the physiology, then treat accordingly.

---

## Etiology & Risk Factors

### Aortic Stenosis (AS)

- **Calcific/degenerative AS** — most common in adults >65 years; calcification of a normal tricuspid valve
- **Bicuspid aortic valve** — congenital; presents 10–20 years earlier than calcific AS; present in ~1–2% of the population
- **Rheumatic heart disease** — predominantly developing world; often concurrent mitral disease
- Risk factors: age, male sex, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney disease, smoking

### Mitral Regurgitation (MR)

- **Primary (organic) MR** — intrinsic leaflet pathology
  - Mitral valve prolapse (MVP/Barlow's) — most common cause in developed countries
  - Rheumatic disease, infective endocarditis, connective tissue disorders
  - Flail leaflet (chordal or papillary muscle rupture) — **acute MR, high-acuity**
- **Secondary (functional) MR** — structurally normal leaflets with tethering due to LV remodeling
  - Ischemic (post-MI LV dilation) or non-ischemic dilated cardiomyopathy

### Aortic Regurgitation (AR)

- **Chronic AR** — bicuspid aortic valve, rheumatic disease, annuloaortic ectasia, Marfan syndrome
- **Acute AR** — infective endocarditis, type A aortic dissection, blunt chest trauma; **surgical emergency**

### Mitral Stenosis (MS)

- **Rheumatic** — overwhelmingly predominant cause; commissural fusion, leaflet thickening, subvalvular disease
- **Non-rheumatic** — mitral annular calcification (MAC), congenital, radiation, post-repair/replacement

### ICU Precipitants of Decompensation

| Precipitant | Lesion Most Affected |
|---|---|
| Atrial fibrillation (rapid ventricular response) | MS, AR, MR |
| Systemic infection / sepsis | Any (↑ CO demands) |
| Anemia | AS (masks symptoms; unmasked by stress) |
| Acute myocardial infarction | Acute MR (papillary muscle rupture), functional MR |
| Aortic dissection | Acute AR |
| Infective endocarditis | Acute MR, Acute AR |
| Volume overload (e.g., massive transfusion) | MS, MR |

## Pathophysiology

### Aortic Stenosis

Calcific AS causes progressive obstruction to LV outflow. The LV compensates with concentric hypertrophy to normalize wall stress. Over time, this leads to diastolic dysfunction, reduced LV compliance, and dependence on atrial contraction (atrial kick) for filling. The hypertrophied LV is exquisitely sensitive to preload reduction and afterload increase.

**In the ICU:** AS patients decompensate when:
- Heart rate increases (less diastolic filling time)
- Preload falls (diuresis, hemorrhage, sepsis-related vasodilation)
- AF develops (loss of atrial kick = precipitous drop in CO)
- Demand increases (sepsis, anemia, fever)

The classic triad of **syncope, angina, and dyspnea on exertion** marks symptomatic AS. Once symptoms develop, median survival without intervention is ~2–3 years.

### Acute Mitral Regurgitation

In acute MR (e.g., papillary muscle rupture), the LV suddenly faces a large volume load into a non-compliant left atrium. Because the LA hasn't had time to dilate, left atrial pressure rises sharply → pulmonary edema. Simultaneously, effective forward stroke volume drops → cardiogenic shock. The regurgitant fraction can be >50% of stroke volume.

**Contrast with chronic MR:** In chronic MR, gradual LA and LV dilation accommodates the volume load, maintaining relatively normal filling pressures — until the LV fails.

### Aortic Regurgitation (Acute vs. Chronic)

- **Chronic AR:** LV undergoes eccentric hypertrophy and dilation to accommodate the diastolic volume load; LVEDP remains relatively normal until late
- **Acute AR:** The normal-sized LV cannot accommodate sudden diastolic volume load; LVEDP rises dramatically, causing functional mitral stenosis, pulmonary edema, and reduced forward CO; the aortic valve may close prematurely

> [!warning] Acute AR — Hemodynamic Collapse
> Acute severe AR (e.g., from endocarditis or dissection) can cause rapid hemodynamic collapse. The "wide pulse pressure" typical of chronic AR may be absent. Tachycardia is a critical compensatory mechanism — do not administer rate-slowing agents (beta-blockers, non-dihydropyridine CCBs).

### Mitral Stenosis

Obstruction at the mitral valve limits LV filling, maintaining elevated LA pressure. LA hypertension propagates backward to the pulmonary vasculature → pulmonary hypertension, right heart strain. In severe MS, CO becomes fixed and cannot augment with exercise. Tachycardia worsens MS hemodynamics by shortening diastolic filling time.

## Clinical Presentation

### Signs and Symptoms

| Lesion | Classic Symptoms | Classic Exam Findings |
|---|---|---|
| **Severe AS** | Exertional dyspnea, angina, syncope | Harsh crescendo-decrescendo systolic murmur (RUSB), delayed/diminished carotid upstroke (pulsus parvus et tardus), S4 |
| **Acute MR** | Sudden pulmonary edema, shock | Soft/absent holosystolic murmur (low-gradient!), S3, pulmonary rales, hypotension |
| **Chronic MR** | Progressive dyspnea, fatigue, AF | Holosystolic murmur (apex → axilla), displaced PMI, S3 |
| **Acute AR** | Sudden dyspnea, chest pain, shock | Soft diastolic murmur, tachycardia, narrow pulse pressure, pulmonary rales |
| **Chronic AR** | Dyspnea on exertion, palpitations | Decrescendo diastolic murmur (LUSB), wide pulse pressure, bounding pulses, Austin Flint murmur |
| **MS** | Dyspnea, orthopnea, AF, hemoptysis | Opening snap, low-pitched mid-diastolic rumble (apex), loud S1, signs of right heart failure |

### History Pearls

- Always ask about **prior rheumatic fever**, history of endocarditis, connective tissue disorders, and prior cardiac procedures
- Ask about **preceding symptoms** in a previously stable patient — sudden worsening often indicates a precipitant (endocarditis, MI, arrhythmia)
- In AS patients, identify who has known valve disease: a "routine" sepsis work-up may reveal the patient can't augment CO due to fixed outflow obstruction

### Red Flags

> [!warning] Acute MR Red Flags
> - Sudden onset pulmonary edema in the setting of recent MI (within 1–7 days) → suspect papillary muscle rupture
> - Hypotension + pulmonary edema without clear HF history
> - Murmur may be SOFT or absent in low-output acute MR — absence of murmur does NOT exclude severe valvular pathology

> [!warning] Acute AR Red Flags
> - Aortic regurgitation + chest/back pain → aortic dissection until proven otherwise (do NOT initiate anticoagulation empirically)
> - Fever + new AR murmur → infective endocarditis with leaflet destruction
> - Acute AR with premature mitral valve closure on echo = severe hemodynamic compromise

> [!warning] AS in the Critically Ill
> - Aggressive diuresis in the AS patient with hypovolemic shock can cause catastrophic preload collapse
> - Starting vasodilators (e.g., for hypertension) in the unrecognized AS patient can cause refractory hypotension

## Diagnostic Workup

### Echocardiography — The Cornerstone

Urgent **transthoracic echocardiography (TTE)** is the first-line imaging study for all suspected VHD in the ICU. It establishes:
- Valve anatomy and morphology
- Severity of stenosis (valve area, mean gradient) or regurgitation (vena contracta, regurgitant volume)
- LV and RV size and function
- LA size, pulmonary artery pressures
- Pericardial effusion

**Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE)** is preferred when:
- TTE is technically inadequate (ARDS, post-sternotomy, obesity, MV)
- Suspected endocarditis or abscess
- Pre-procedural planning (MitraClip, TAVR, surgical repair)
- Evaluation of prosthetic valve dysfunction

### Grading Severity of Key Lesions

**Aortic Stenosis (ACC/AHA 2020)**

| Stage | AVA | Mean Gradient | Vmax |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moderate | 1.0–1.5 cm² | 20–40 mmHg | 3.0–4.0 m/s |
| Severe | < 1.0 cm² | > 40 mmHg | > 4.0 m/s |
| Very Severe | < 0.6 cm² | > 60 mmHg | > 5.0 m/s |

> [!tip] Low-Flow, Low-Gradient AS
> In low-output states, gradients may be low despite severe AS. **Dobutamine stress echocardiography** is most useful in patients with *classical* low-flow, low-gradient AS (LVEF <50%) to differentiate true severe AS from pseudo-severe AS. True severe AS: ↑ gradient with little change in AVA. Pseudo-severe AS: ↑ AVA with ↑ flow, gradients normalize.[^15][^16][^17]
>
> In patients with *paradoxical* low-flow, low-gradient AS (LVEF ≥50%, stroke volume index <35 mL/m²), the issue is often restrictive LV filling due to concentric remodeling, diastolic dysfunction, or atrial fibrillation — not pseudo-severe AS. **Aortic valve calcium scoring by CT** is the preferred modality to confirm severity in this population.[^16][^18][^17]
>
> Both SAVR and TAVR reduce mortality compared to conservative management in symptomatic patients with confirmed severe low-flow, low-gradient AS, with the benefit most pronounced in classical low-flow, low-gradient AS.[^15][^16]

**Mitral Stenosis Severity**

| Parameter | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|---|---|---|---|
| MVA (cm²) | > 1.5 | 1.0–1.5 | < 1.0 |
| Mean gradient (mmHg) | < 5 | 5–10 | > 10 |
| PASP (mmHg) | < 30 | 30–50 | > 50 |

### Laboratory Studies

- **BNP/NT-proBNP** — elevated in most decompensated VHD; useful for trending
- **Troponin** — elevated in demand ischemia from AS or ACS triggering MR
- **CBC** — anemia worsens hemodynamics in AS and MS
- **BMP/CMP** — renal function, electrolytes; guides diuresis
- **Blood cultures x2** — if endocarditis suspected (prior to antibiotics)
- **Coagulation** — if emergent surgery anticipated
- **Type and screen** — all decompensated VHD with potential surgical intervention

### Hemodynamic Monitoring

- **Right heart catheterization (PA catheter)** — valuable in complex VHD with shock; distinguishes cardiogenic from distributive shock, guides volume and vasoactive management
  - Acute MR: large V-waves on PCWP tracing (may be mistaken for PA pressure)
  - MS: elevated PCWP with gradient between PCWP and PA diastolic pressure
- **Arterial line** — standard for hemodynamic instability, titration of vasoactives

### Additional Imaging

- **CT aortogram** — if acute AR with chest pain to rule out dissection
- **Coronary CT angiography or invasive catheterization** — pre-operatively in severe AS (>40 years with risk factors) and acute MR to rule out concurrent CAD
- **Cardiac MRI** — gold standard for regurgitant volume quantification; rarely practical in ICU setting

## Management

### General ICU Principles

1. **Early echocardiography** — do not manage hemodynamics without knowing the anatomy
2. **Avoid empiric vasodilators or diuretics** until diagnosis is established
3. **Cardiology and cardiac surgery consultation early** — the decision to intervene is time-sensitive
4. **Optimize comorbidities** — treat anemia, infection, arrhythmia as precipitants
5. **Anticoagulation for AF** — apply CHA₂DS₂-VASc with caution in the setting of impending surgery

---

### Severe Aortic Stenosis in the ICU

**Goals:** Maintain preload, heart rate, and sinus rhythm; avoid systemic vasodilation.

| Parameter | Target |
|---|---|
| Heart rate | 60–80 bpm (bradycardia reduces CO; tachycardia reduces diastolic filling) |
| Preload | Maintain adequate (avoid aggressive diuresis) |
| Sinus rhythm | Restore ASAP if AF; cardioversion if hemodynamically unstable |
| Vasopressors | **Phenylephrine or norepinephrine** preferred (maintain SVR without tachycardia) |
| Vasodilators | **AVOID** (precipitate shock) |

> [!tip] The AS Sepsis Patient
> Septic vasodilation with fixed outflow obstruction = high-risk combination. These patients need vasopressors early and targeted resuscitation to avoid over-diuresis. TTE is mandatory before aggressive fluid or vasoactive management.

**Definitive intervention:**

- **Surgical AVR (SAVR)** — preferred in low/intermediate-risk patients with anatomy unsuitable for TAVR, or concomitant coronary/aortic disease requiring surgery
- **Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR)** — approved for all surgical risk categories; increasingly used for urgent/emergent indications in decompensated AS during the index hospitalization
  - Urgent TAVR pathways are emerging for patients with acute decompensated AS; longer hospitalizations prior to TAVR are independently associated with worse outcomes, supporting early structural heart team consultation[^1][^2]
  - 30-day mortality for TAVR in cardiogenic shock: approximately 13–28% depending on severity; in non-shock decompensated AS: approximately 4–16%[^1][^2][^3][^4]
  - Survivors of the first 30 days after urgent TAVR have similar long-term mortality to those without cardiogenic shock — emphasizing the importance of surviving the acute period[^1]
- **Balloon aortic valvuloplasty (BAV)** — temporizing bridge measure; improves hemodynamics transiently; NOT a definitive therapy; mortality approaches 50% if not followed by definitive AVR within 90 days[^2]

### Acute Mitral Regurgitation

Acute severe MR (e.g., papillary muscle rupture) is a **surgical emergency** with mortality >75% without intervention.

**Medical stabilization (bridge to intervention):**

| Intervention | Rationale | References |
|---|---|---|
| **Intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP)** | Reduces afterload → decreases regurgitant fraction, increases forward CO; MR patients identified as "super-responders" to IABP in observational data | [^8], [^9] |
| **Sodium nitroprusside** | Reduces SVR → improves forward flow; requires arterial line; use with caution in hypotension | [^9], [^10] |
| **Diuretics (IV furosemide)** | Treat pulmonary edema; avoid over-diuresis | [^10] |
| **Positive pressure ventilation (noninvasive or invasive)** | Reduces work of breathing; reduces LV afterload and MR severity, improves forward flow; PEEP particularly beneficial | [^8], [^9] |
| **Impella / VA-ECMO** | In refractory cardiogenic shock as bridge to surgery | [^8] |

> [!warning] Vasopressors in Acute MR
> Vasoconstrictors (phenylephrine, norepinephrine) INCREASE afterload → worsen regurgitant fraction → increase pulmonary edema. If vasopressors are required for hypotension, use at lowest effective dose while pursuing definitive intervention.

> [!tip] Positive Pressure Ventilation in Acute MR
> Noninvasive and invasive positive pressure ventilation (PPV) provides dual benefit in acute MR: it reduces work of breathing while simultaneously reducing LV afterload, decreasing mitral regurgitant volume, and improving forward cardiac output. PEEP is particularly effective at reducing regurgitant fraction. PPV should be considered early in patients with acute MR and respiratory distress.[^8][^9]

> [!warning] IABP Evidence Caveat
> While IABP is commonly used in the United States for MR-related cardiogenic shock and is physiologically sound (reduces afterload, improves forward flow), high-quality clinical trial data are lacking for this specific population. Evidence is based on translational models and observational studies identifying MR patients as "super-responders" to IABP.[^8][^9]

**Definitive intervention:**

- **Emergency mitral valve surgery** — standard of care for acute severe MR; repair preferred over replacement when feasible; high operative mortality (~25–30%) but superior to medical management alone[^8][^9]
- **Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER/MitraClip)** — may be considered in highly selected patients with prohibitive surgical risk as part of a multidisciplinary Heart Team approach[^8]
  - Evidence for TEER in acute MR (e.g., papillary muscle rupture) is limited to case series and registry data; not an established alternative to surgery[^11][^9]
  - In the largest series of post-MI papillary muscle rupture treated with TEER (all patients turned down for surgery): procedural success 87%, in-hospital mortality 30%; 22% subsequently underwent elective surgical MVR — highlighting TEER's potential role as a bridge to surgery in stabilized patients[^9]
  - Current guidelines recommend TEER only for patients who are not surgical candidates with prohibitive risk; emergency surgery remains the treatment of choice[^8][^9]

**Drug dosing for afterload reduction:**

| Drug | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium nitroprusside | 0.3–10 mcg/kg/min IV | Titrate to MAP; requires arterial line; cyanide toxicity >48–72h or renal failure |
| Nicardipine | 5–15 mg/h IV infusion | Alternative to nitroprusside; lower risk of cyanide toxicity; preferred in some ICUs |
| Furosemide | 40–200 mg IV bolus or infusion | Adjust based on diuresis and hemodynamics |
| Dobutamine | 2–20 mcg/kg/min IV | For low-output states; tachycardia limits use |

### Acute Aortic Regurgitation

Acute severe AR is a **cardiac surgical emergency**.

**Medical bridge:**

- **Sodium nitroprusside** — reduces SVR, improves forward CO
- **Dobutamine** — increases HR and CO (tachycardia is actually beneficial — reduces diastolic filling time for regurgitation)
- **AVOID beta-blockers, non-DHP CCBs** — these reduce heart rate, prolonging diastole and worsening regurgitation
- **AVOID IABP** — worsens AR (balloon inflation in diastole increases aortic diastolic pressure → more regurgitation)

> [!warning] IABP Contraindicated in Severe AR
> IABP inflates in diastole to increase coronary perfusion pressure — but in severe AR, this increases aortic diastolic pressure and worsens the regurgitant volume. Contraindicated.

**Definitive:** Emergency surgical aortic valve replacement (± aortic root replacement if dissection or annuloaortic ectasia).

---

### Chronic Severe AR (Decompensated in ICU)

- **Vasodilators** (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, amlodipine) — reduce regurgitant volume, offload LV
- **Diuretics** — manage pulmonary congestion
- Plan for surgical AVR when EF <55% or LVESD >50mm, even if asymptomatic
- Avoid bradycardia — heart rate 60–80 is acceptable; faster rates reduce diastolic regurgitation time

---

### Mitral Stenosis

**Goals:** Slow heart rate (maximize diastolic filling time), control ventricular rate in AF, relieve pulmonary congestion.

| Intervention | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Rate control (AF)** | IV metoprolol, diltiazem, or digoxin; target HR 60–80 |
| **Diuretics** | Relieve pulmonary congestion; avoid preload collapse |
| **Anticoagulation** | Heparin infusion for AF; high thromboembolic risk in MS |
| **Oxygen / NIV** | For pulmonary edema; intubation carries risk of decompensation |

**Definitive:**

- **Percutaneous mitral balloon commissurotomy (PMBC)** — preferred for pliable, non-calcified, non-regurgitant valves with favorable anatomy (Wilkins score ≤8); can be performed urgently in decompensated MS
- **Surgical mitral valve repair or replacement** — for unfavorable anatomy, significant MR, or if concurrent procedures needed

**Drug dosing for rate control in MS:**

| Drug | IV Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Metoprolol | 5 mg IV q5min × 3 | First-line if no decompensation |
| Diltiazem | 0.25 mg/kg IV then infusion 5–15 mg/h | Alternative; avoid in HFrEF |
| Digoxin | 0.25–0.5 mg IV loading | Adjunct; especially useful in RV failure |

### Prosthetic Valve Dysfunction in the ICU

**Thrombotic obstruction:**
- Presents as sudden hemodynamic deterioration in anticoagulated (or sub-therapeutic) patient
- Echo: elevated gradients, restricted leaflet motion
- Treatment approach should be individualized based on multiple clinical factors (see table below)[^12][^13]
- **Surgery** — preferred for left-sided prosthetic valve thrombosis when feasible, especially in critically ill patients without serious comorbidities; lower mortality in NYHA class I–II patients (<5%)[^12][^13]
- **Fibrinolysis (ultraslow, low-dose regimen: 25 mg tPA over 6–24 hours without bolus)** — Class I recommendation (ACC/AHA) for symptomatic left-sided mechanical valve thrombosis; first-line for right-sided prosthetic thrombosis; acceptable for left-sided thrombosis in high/prohibitive surgical risk patients, those with small thrombus burden (≤0.8 cm²), mild–moderate HF symptoms (NYHA class I–III), and low bleeding risk[^12][^14][^13]
  - Ultraslow, low-dose fibrinolysis achieves hemodynamic success rates >90% with embolic event rates <2% and major bleeding rates <2%[^12][^14]
  - The SAFE-PVT trial found no significant difference in efficacy between surgery and fibrinolysis (71.8% vs. 67.5% achieving complete valve function restoration), but fibrinolysis had fewer major complications (7.5% vs. 28.2%)[^14]

| Factor | Favors Surgery | Favors Fibrinolysis |
|---|---|---|
| Surgical expertise | Readily available | Not available or high risk |
| Surgical risk | Low | High or prohibitive |
| Bleeding risk | N/A | Low (no contraindication to fibrinolysis) |
| Episode | Recurrent valve thrombosis | First-time episode |
| NYHA class | IV | I, II, or III |
| Thrombus size | Large (>0.8 cm²) | Small (≤0.8 cm²) |
| Valve position | Left-sided (when feasible) | Right-sided (first-line) |
| Other pathology | LA thrombus, concomitant CAD, other valve disease | No other pathology |

**Prosthetic valve endocarditis:**
- High in-hospital mortality; early surgical consultation mandatory
- Surgical indications: heart failure, perivalvular abscess/fistula, persistent bacteremia, large vegetation with emboli

> [!tip] Prosthetic Valve Regurgitation
> Paravalvular leak (PVL) is a common complication of transcatheter valves. Mild PVL is acceptable; moderate-severe PVL is associated with hemolysis and heart failure. Transcatheter PVL closure is increasingly available as an alternative to reoperation.

## Clinical Pearls

> [!tip] Pearl 1: Know the Lesion Before Treating the Pressure
> Hypotension in AS is treated differently than hypotension in acute MR. Do not reflexively start vasodilators for "hypertensive pulmonary edema" without ruling out AS. A bedside TTE takes 5 minutes and changes everything.

> [!tip] Pearl 2: The Quiet Murmur of Acute MR
> Acute severe MR may have a soft or absent murmur due to rapid equalization of LA and LV pressures. A silent valve in a patient with flash pulmonary edema and new hypotension after MI should raise immediate concern for papillary muscle rupture.

> [!tip] Pearl 3: V-Waves on the PA Catheter
> Giant V-waves on the PCWP tracing are not pathognomonic for MR — they are seen whenever the LA is non-compliant and filling rapidly. However, in the right clinical context (post-MI shock + pulmonary edema), they strongly support acute MR.

> [!tip] Pearl 4: IABP Is Not Universal
> IABP is helpful in acute MR (reduces afterload, improves forward CO) but is contraindicated in severe AR (worsens regurgitation) and relatively contraindicated in severe AS (does not help fixed obstruction; bilateral femoral disease common). Know the physiology before placing.

> [!tip] Pearl 5: Rate Control in MS Is Therapeutic
> In decompensated MS with rapid AF, successful heart rate control (IV metoprolol or diltiazem to HR <80) can dramatically improve hemodynamics — more reliably than diuresis alone. Treat the rate first.

> [!tip] Pearl 6: Don't Delay Surgical Consultation
> Acute MR and acute AR are surgical emergencies. Medical stabilization buys time but does not reverse the underlying pathology. Call CT surgery early — the window for successful intervention narrows with every hour of shock.

> [!tip] Pearl 7: Urgent TAVR Is Now an Option
> For patients with acute decompensated severe AS who are TAVR candidates, urgent TAVR within the index hospitalization improves outcomes. Don't assume that ICU stabilization precludes transcatheter intervention — have the structural heart team evaluate early.

> [!tip] Pearl 8: Endocarditis Masquerades
> New or worsening valve regurgitation in any febrile ICU patient should prompt blood cultures and echocardiography. Infective endocarditis on native or prosthetic valves can present with acute hemodynamic collapse from leaflet destruction.

> [!tip] Pearl 9: Positive Pressure Ventilation Is Therapeutic in Acute MR
> In acute severe MR with respiratory distress, positive pressure ventilation (noninvasive or invasive) is not just supportive — it's hemodynamically therapeutic. By reducing LV afterload, PPV decreases the regurgitant fraction and improves forward cardiac output. Don't delay intubation or NIV in the decompensating patient with acute MR.[^8][^9]

## Evidence & Landmark Trials

### Landmark Trials

**PARTNER I (2010–2011)**
- Population: Inoperable (Cohort B) and high-risk (Cohort A) patients with severe AS
- Intervention: TAVR (Edwards SAPIEN) vs. standard therapy (Cohort B) or SAVR (Cohort A)
- Result: In inoperable patients, TAVR reduced all-cause mortality at 1 year (30.7% vs. 50.7%). In high-risk patients, TAVR was non-inferior to SAVR at 1 year (24.2% vs. 26.8%)
- Impact: Established TAVR as the standard of care for inoperable and high-risk severe AS

**PARTNER 3 (2019)**
- Population: Low-risk patients with severe symptomatic AS
- Intervention: TAVR (SAPIEN 3) vs. SAVR
- Result: TAVR was superior to SAVR for the composite of death, stroke, or rehospitalization at 1 year (8.5% vs. 15.1%)
- Impact: Expanded TAVR indication to all surgical risk groups; TAVR now a valid option for low-risk patients

**EVEREST II (2011)**
- Population: Symptomatic moderate-to-severe or severe primary MR
- Intervention: MitraClip vs. surgery
- Result: Surgery was superior for efficacy (MR reduction) but MitraClip had fewer adverse events; 5-year outcomes comparable in low/moderate surgical risk patients
- Impact: Established safety profile of transcatheter edge-to-edge repair for primary MR; led to FDA approval

**COAPT (2018)**
- Population: Symptomatic HFrEF with moderate-to-severe or severe secondary (functional) MR despite GDMT
- Intervention: MitraClip + GDMT vs. GDMT alone
- Result: MitraClip reduced heart failure hospitalizations at 24 months (35.8% vs. 67.9% annualized rate) and all-cause mortality (29.1% vs. 46.1%)
- Impact: Practice-changing; MitraClip now Class IIa recommendation for selected patients with secondary MR and HFrEF refractory to GDMT. Five-year data confirmed durable benefit.

**MITRA-FR (2018)**
- Population: Similar to COAPT — HFrEF with severe secondary MR
- Intervention: MitraClip + GDMT vs. GDMT
- Result: **No benefit** of MitraClip (death or HF hospitalization: 54.6% vs. 51.3% at 12 months)
- Impact: Highlighted importance of patient selection; in COAPT, MR was "disproportionate" to LV dilation; in MITRA-FR, MR was proportionate — key distinction for MitraClip benefit

### Current Evidence 

1. **Tarantini G et al.** "Acute Decompensated Valvular Disease in the Intensive Care Unit." *JACC: Advances.* 2024;3:101402. — Comprehensive review of ICU management of all valve lesions, emphasizing multidisciplinary team approach, hemodynamic goals, and bridging strategies to intervention. Highlights need for urgent structural heart team involvement.

2. **Sá MP et al.** "Emergency transcatheter aortic valve implantation for acute heart failure due to severe aortic stenosis in critically ill patients with or without cardiogenic shock." *EuroIntervention.* 2022. PMID 36210517. — Meta-analysis showing 30-day mortality ~28% in cardiogenic shock vs. ~16% in non-shock decompensated AS undergoing urgent TAVI; survivors have similar long-term outcomes as elective cases.

3. **Cheung A et al.** "Urgent vs Elective Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement in Patients With Severe Aortic Stenosis." *PMC.* 2025. — Demonstrates feasibility and acceptable outcomes of urgent TAVR; longer index hospitalization prior to TAVR associated with worse outcomes, supporting early structural heart consultation.

4. **Estévez-Loureiro R et al.** "Management of Severe Mitral Regurgitation in Patients With Acute Myocardial Infarction: JACC Focus Seminar 2/5." *JACC.* 2024;83(22). PMID 38692830. — Reviews post-MI MR management including mechanical support strategies, timing of surgery, and emerging role of transcatheter rescue in papillary muscle rupture.

5. **Stone GW et al.** "Five-Year Follow-up after Transcatheter Repair of Secondary Mitral Regurgitation (COAPT)." *NEJM.* 2023;388(23). — Confirms durable reduction in HF hospitalizations and improved survival with MitraClip at 5 years in appropriately selected HFrEF patients with disproportionate secondary MR; annualized HF hospitalization rate 17.4% vs. 30.3%.

## Key Guidelines

### ACC/AHA 2020 Guideline for the Management of Patients with Valvular Heart Disease
*(Otto CM et al., Circulation 2021)*

- Introduced staging classification (A–D) for all valve lesions based on anatomy, hemodynamics, symptoms, and LV response
- Class I recommendation: AVR for symptomatic severe AS regardless of surgical risk
- Class I recommendation: TAVR for inoperable patients with severe AS; TAVR or SAVR for all surgical risk groups based on shared decision-making
- Class IIa: MitraClip for symptomatic secondary MR refractory to GDMT (LVEF 20–50%, LVESD ≤70mm, PASP ≤70mmHg)
- Class IIa: MitraClip for primary MR at high/prohibitive surgical risk

### 2021 ESC/EACTS Guidelines for the Management of Valvular Heart Disease
*(Vahanian A et al., Eur Heart J 2022)*

- Emphasizes Heart Valve Team (cardiology + cardiac surgery + structural interventionalist) for all moderate-severe VHD decisions
- TAVI preferred for patients >75 years or high/prohibitive surgical risk; SAVR preferred for low-risk patients <75 years
- Recommends PMBC as first-line for pliable MS with favorable anatomy in centers with expertise

### 2021 AHA/ACC Focused Update: Mechanical Complications of Acute MI
*(Ibanez B et al., Circulation 2021)*

- Emergency surgical MV repair/replacement for post-MI papillary muscle rupture; fibrinolysis not an option
- MCS (IABP, Impella) recommended to stabilize prior to emergency surgery when feasible
- MitraClip as rescue in selected high-risk surgical candidates

### ESC 2025 Guidelines for the Management of Valvular Heart Disease
*(Vahanian A et al., Eur Heart J 2025)*

- Updated evidence for TAVI across risk groups with longer-term follow-up data
- Increasing recognition of transcatheter mitral and tricuspid interventions
- Reinforcement of multidisciplinary Heart Valve Team for all complex VHD
