## Overview

**Aortic valve disease** comprises two primary pathologies affecting ICU patients: **aortic stenosis (AS)** — narrowing of the aortic valve orifice — and **aortic regurgitation (AR)** — valve insufficiency allowing retrograde flow into the left ventricle. Throughout this document, **TAVR** (transcatheter aortic valve replacement) and **TAVI** (transcatheter aortic valve implantation) are used interchangeably — both terms refer to the same procedure and appear across different guidelines and literature. Both present unique hemodynamic crises that demand rapid diagnosis and intervention in the critical care setting.

> [!tip] **Why This Matters**
> Aortic valve disease causes **acute hemodynamic decompensation** requiring ICU admission in 10–15% of cases. Severe AS in cardiogenic shock carries **30-day mortality of 13–28% with urgent TAVR** (vs. ~25–40% with emergency SAVR; registry data). Evidence is largely observational — no randomized trials comparing emergency TAVR vs. SAVR exist. Untreated AS-related cardiogenic shock approaches 100% mortality. Acute AR can deteriorate within hours to fulminant pulmonary edema and shock. Early recognition of hemodynamic patterns, rapid echocardiographic assessment, and emergent structural heart team involvement are **life-saving interventions** in the ICU.

## Etiology & Risk Factors

### Aortic Stenosis

| Etiology | Prevalence | Risk Profile |
|----------|-----------|--------------|
| **Degenerative/Calcific** | 80–90% (age >70) | Age >60, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney disease, male |
| **Bicuspid Aortic Valve** | 1–2% population; AS by age 50–70 | Genetic predisposition, family history |
| **Rheumatic** | 10–20% globally; rare in developed countries | History of rheumatic fever, endemic regions |
| **Radiation-induced** | 2–3% post-chest radiation | Mediastinal radiation >20 years prior |
| **Post-inflammatory** | Rare | SLE, ankylosing spondylitis, Marfan syndrome |

**Risk factors for progression:**
- Advanced age, male sex, systemic hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, chronic kidney disease (CKD stages 3–5), elevated lipoprotein(a)

### Aortic Regurgitation

#### Acute AR

| Etiology | ICU Scenario | Mechanism |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| **Endocarditis** | Most common in ICU | Vegetation disruption, perforation, abscess |
| **Aortic dissection** | Type A dissection | Dissection extension into aortic root, prolapse of intimal flap |
| **Prosthetic valve malfunction** | Post-AVR complications | Dehiscence, thrombosis, pannus overgrowth |
| **Trauma/Iatrogenic** | Post-catheterization, post-balloon aortic valvuloplasty | Mechanical valve damage |
| **Spontaneous rupture** | Idiopathic, connective tissue disorder | Marfan, Ehlers-Danlos syndromes |

#### Chronic AR

- **Primary (organic) valve pathology:** Rheumatic disease, bicuspid valve, endocarditis sequelae, medication (fenfluramine, phentermine)
- **Secondary (functional):** Aortic root dilatation from hypertension, aortic dissection, connective tissue disorders (Marfan, Ehlers-Danlos), syphilitic aortitis, ankylosing spondylitis

## Pathophysiology

### Aortic Stenosis

#### Chronic AS: Progressive LV Remodeling

1. **Obstruction Phase:** Fixed narrowing increases LV outflow tract resistance; LV pressure rises to maintain forward stroke volume → **concentric hypertrophy** (wall thickening with preserved cavity size)
2. **Compensatory Phase:** Increased contractility, reduced cavity stress, maintained ejection fraction (EF 50–70%)
3. **Failure Phase (critical ICU point):**
   - Progressive diastolic dysfunction → elevated LV end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) → pulmonary congestion
   - Reduced diastolic perfusion time → subendocardial ischemia despite normal coronaries
   - Wall stress increases despite hypertrophy → afterload mismatch → systolic dysfunction
   - **EF decline <50%** marks transition to systolic failure; further EF decline heralds decompensation

**Hemodynamic consequences in the ICU:**
- Loss of atrial contribution (severe diastolic dysfunction): A-wave prominent in PCWP tracing; atrial fibrillation catastrophic
- Preload-dependent physiology: hypovolemia causes rapid shock; aggressive diuresis worsens shock
- **Afterload sensitivity:** vasodilators (especially nitroprusside, nitroglycerin) → severe hypotension
- Coronary flow mismatch: LV hypertrophy with increased oxygen demand; hypertension-dependent perfusion pressure

#### Low-Flow Low-Gradient AS (LFlg-AS)

**Classical LFGL-AS (reduced EF, EF <50%):**
- Severe AS (valve area <1.0 cm²) + low stroke volume index (SVI <35 mL/m²) + low mean gradient (<40 mmHg)
- Mechanism: reduced contractility with severe AS → cannot generate high gradients despite severe obstruction
- **Dobutamine stress echo (DSE):** distinguishes true severe AS (AVA <1.0 cm² with dobutamine, ΔAVA <0.2 cm²) from pseudo-severe (AVA improves >0.2 cm²)
- **ICU pearl:** Check contractile reserve; if no reserve, indicates severe myocardial dysfunction—poor surgical candidate but may benefit from TAVR (lower mortality than SAVR in this cohort)

**Paradoxical low-flow low-gradient AS (plFLGL-AS; "flash" AS):**
- Mild-to-moderate AS (AVA 1.0–1.5 cm²) + normal EF (≥50%) + low SVI (<35 mL/m²) + low gradient (<40 mmHg)
- Mechanism: **restrictive LV physiology** from severe diastolic dysfunction and hypertrophy (elevated LVEDP, small cavity) → reduced stroke volume despite normal contractility
- **CT aortic valve calcium scoring:** preferred over DSE; Agatston score ≥2,000 AU in men, ≥1,200 AU in women confirms hemodynamically significant AS (validated thresholds per Clavel et al.)
- **ICU implication:** Often misclassified as "mild AS" on standard echo; high calcium burden suggests severe disease; TAVR outcomes favorable in selected cases

### Aortic Regurgitation

#### Acute AR: Hemodynamic Crisis

1. **Pathophysiology of acute decompensation:**
   - Sudden large regurgitant volume (often 50–80% of LV stroke volume) imposed on non-compliant, unprepared left ventricle
   - Rapid rise in LVEDP → pulmonary edema
   - **Critical feature:** Premature mitral valve closure due to elevated LVEDP exceeding LA pressure in early diastole
   - Reduced forward cardiac output + elevated afterload → severe shock within hours

2. **Hemodynamic monitoring findings:**
   - **PCWP:** Markedly elevated (often >18–20 mmHg), prominent rapid y-descent (brisk closure)
   - **LV waveform:** Rapid rise in LVEDP; in severe cases, LVEDP >30 mmHg at end-diastole
   - **Aortic pressure:** Wide pulse pressure (systolic elevated, diastolic low) in chronic AR; in acute AR, diastolic pressure may normalize due to severe LV dysfunction
   - **Cardiac output:** Low due to reduced forward flow and tachycardia

3. **ICU crisis triggers:**
   - **Sepsis (endocarditis):** fever, elevated lactate, hemodynamic instability
   - **Aortic dissection:** chest/back pain, blood pressure differential limbs, neurologic deficits
   - **Mechanical compromise:** sudden increase in regurgitant fraction

#### Chronic AR: Gradual LV Dilation

1. **Eccentric hypertrophy:** LV dilates (large cavity) with mild wall thickening → increased LV mass but normal wall stress initially
2. **Compensatory mechanisms:**
   - Increased contractility (Frank-Starling mechanism on dilated ventricle)
   - Reduced peripheral vascular resistance (splanchnic vasodilation from neurohormonal compensations)
   - EF maintained >55–60% for years despite large regurgitant volume
3. **Decompensation (ICU admission):**
   - Progressive EF decline as LV dilates beyond optimal point on Frank-Starling curve
   - Increased filling pressures → pulmonary edema
   - Reduced forward flow → fatigue, dyspnea

## Clinical Presentation

### Aortic Stenosis: Signs & Symptoms

| Finding | Mechanism | ICU Significance |
|---------|-----------|------------------|
| **Exertional dyspnea** | Inability to increase cardiac output despite increased demand → pulmonary congestion | Late sign; dyspnea at rest = decompensation |
| **Exertional chest pain (angina)** | Subendocardial ischemia from LV hypertrophy + high demand + diastolic compression | Mimic ACS; troponin elevation common without CAD |
| **Syncope/presyncope** | Inability to augment cardiac output + peripheral vasodilation during exertion | High risk; indicates severe disease |
| **Orthopnea, PND** | Pulmonary edema from elevated LVEDP; poor diastolic relaxation | Hallmark of systolic/diastolic failure |
| **Fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance** | Low cardiac output state | Often attributed to other causes; misdiagnosis risk |
| **Palpitations** | Atrial fibrillation, strong apical impulse in hypertrophied LV | AFib in AS = hemodynamic crisis |
| **Harsh systolic murmur at right upper sternal border, radiating to carotid** | High-velocity flow across narrowed orifice | Intensity does NOT correlate with severity |
| **Single S2 or paradoxical split** | Delayed aortic valve closure from prolonged ejection time | Indicates severe AS |
| **Slow rising carotid pulse ("pulsus tardus")** | Prolonged ejection period from obstruction | Better appreciated in young patients; absent if HTN, AR |
| **Sustained apical impulse** | Concentric LV hypertrophy | Location normal; force increased |

### Aortic Regurgitation: Signs & Symptoms

#### Acute AR

| Finding | Mechanism | ICU Red Flag |
|---------|-----------|-------------|
| **Acute dyspnea, orthopnea** | Fulminant pulmonary edema from sudden large regurgitant volume | **Emergency sign—requires intubation/IABP support** |
| **Hypotension, shock** | Reduced forward cardiac output + systemic hypotension | Hemodynamic collapse within 24–72 hours |
| **Tachycardia, cool extremities** | Compensatory tachycardia, poor perfusion | Often precedes shock recognition |
| **Chest pain** | Endocarditis, aortic dissection, ischemia from hypotension | Requires imaging (TEE, CTA chest/abdomen) |
| **Fever, sepsis signs** | Infective endocarditis | Blood cultures mandatory |
| **Diastolic murmur (high-pitched, early-diastolic)** | Severe AR; often soft/poorly appreciated in shock | **May be absent in cardiogenic shock** |
| **No prominent peripheral signs** | Diastolic pressure maintained by severe LV dysfunction, reflexive vasoconstriction | **Absence of Corrigan pulse/wide pulse pressure ≠ mild AR** |

#### Chronic AR

| Finding | Clinical Pearl |
|---------|-----------------|
| **Exertional dyspnea (late), orthopnea** | Often asymptomatic until severe; EF decline is insidious |
| **Wide pulse pressure (large Corrigan pulse, bounding carotid)** | Hyperdynamic peripheral signs; water-hammer pulse |
| **Diastolic murmur, high-pitched** | Best heard with patient leaning forward, in full expiration |
| **Widened aortic pulse pressure** | Systolic hypertension + diastolic hypotension |
| **Large volume apex beat, displaced laterally** | Reflects LV dilation; hyperdynamic |
| **Pulsatile liver, bobbing head (de Musset sign)** | Exaggerated peripheral pulsations |

### Red Flags for ICU Admission with Aortic Valve Disease

> [!warning] **Critical ICU Triggers**
> 1. **AS with syncope or hemodynamic instability** → urgent TAVR or surgery within 24–48 hours
> 2. **AS + sepsis** (presumed endocarditis) → blood cultures before antibiotics; TEE; urgent cardiac surgery consult
> 3. **Acute AR with acute pulmonary edema/shock** → emergent TEE; structural heart team; prepare for urgent surgery or TAVR
> 4. **Acute AR + aortic dissection** (Type A) → immediate cardiac surgery; TAVR not appropriate
> 5. **AS with new atrial fibrillation** → rate control difficult; may need urgent AVR
> 6. **AS with acute coronary syndrome** → distinguish angina from demand ischemia; revascularization often deferred until AVR
> 7. **Acute AR + hemodynamic monitoring showing LVEDP >30 mmHg + low cardiac output** → urgent intervention

## Diagnostic Workup

### Echocardiography: The Cornerstone

#### Aortic Stenosis Grading (TTE/TEE)

| Parameter | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|-----------|------|----------|--------|
| **AVA (cm²)** | >1.5 | 1.0–1.5 | <1.0 |
| **Peak AV velocity (Vmax; m/s)** | <3 | 3–4 | >4 |
| **Mean gradient (mmHg)** | <25 | 25–40 | >40 |
| **Aortic Valve Index (AVA/BSA; cm²/m²)** | >0.6 | 0.6–0.8 | <0.6 |

**Additional qualitative assessment:**
- **Leaflet morphology:** Calcification extent (mild, moderate, severe), leaflet thickness, motion
- **LV size & function:** Chamber dimensions, EF, wall thickness, diastolic function
- **Diastolic properties:** E/A ratio, deceleration time (DT), E/e' ratio → assess diastolic dysfunction severity

**ICU-specific measurements:**
- **Stroke volume index (SVI):** <35 mL/m² indicates low-flow state (see LFGL-AS section)
- **Systolic velocity index (SVI) calculation:** SVI = (LVOT area × LVOT VTI) / BSA
- **Ejection fraction:** Critical for risk stratification; EF <40% = high surgical risk

#### Aortic Regurgitation Grading (TTE/TEE)

| Parameter | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|-----------|------|----------|--------|
| **Jet width/LVOT ratio (%)** | <25 | 25–65 | >65 |
| **Jet area/LVOT area (%)** | <5 | 5–60 | >60 |
| **Regurgitant volume (mL)** | <30 | 30–59 | ≥60 |
| **Regurgitant fraction (%)** | <30 | 30–49 | ≥50 |
| **vena contracta width (cm)** | <0.3 | 0.3–0.6 | >0.6 |
| **Pressure half-time (ms)** | >500 | 200–500 | <200 |

**Qualitative features of severe AR:**
- Holodiastolic reversal of diastolic flow in descending thoracic aorta (pulsed Doppler)
- Diastolic mitral regurgitation (secondary, from elevated LVEDP)
- Premature mitral valve closure in acute AR
- Large regurgitant jet, eccentric direction
- LV size: severe AR → massive dilatation (LVEDD often >65 mm)

### Special Considerations: Low-Flow Low-Gradient AS

#### Classical LFGL-AS (EF <50%, SVI <35 mL/m², AVA <1.0, mean gradient <40 mmHg)

**Dobutamine Stress Echocardiography:**
- Protocol: Low-dose dobutamine (5–10 mcg/kg/min) to augment contractility
- **True severe AS:** AVA remains <1.0 cm² (or increases <0.2 cm²) as contractility improves; gradient increases
- **Pseudo-severe AS:** AVA increases >0.2 cm² as contractility improves; gradient decreases
- **Interpretation:** True severe AS → worse prognosis without intervention; TAVR favorable over SAVR
- **ICU caveat:** DSE requires hemodynamic stability; cannot perform in cardiogenic shock requiring vasopressors

#### Paradoxical LFGL-AS (EF ≥50%, SVI <35 mL/m², AVA 1.0–1.5, gradient <40 mmHg)

**CT Aortic Valve Calcium Scoring (preferred):**
- **Method:** Non-contrast CT; measure Agatston calcium score in aortic valve region
- **Interpretation:**
  - **Men:** Score **≥2,000 Agatston units** confirms hemodynamically significant AS
  - **Women:** Score **≥1,200 Agatston units** confirms hemodynamically significant AS
  - *Thresholds validated across multiple studies and referenced in current ACC/AHA guidelines (Clavel MA et al., JACC Cardiovasc Imaging 2017; Otto CM et al., JAMA 2024)*
- **Clinical utility:** Identifies patients with restrictive physiology who appear "mild" on echo but have heavy calcification; these patients benefit from TAVR
- **Advantages over DSE:** No need for pharmacologic stress; better tolerability in decompensated patients

### Hemodynamic Monitoring

#### Pulmonary Artery Catheter

**Aortic Stenosis Pattern:**
- RA: normal
- RV: normal systolic, elevated diastolic (from RV hypertrophy)
- PA: normal or slightly elevated
- PCWP (wedge): elevated (often 15–25 mmHg) reflecting elevated LVEDP
- CO/CI: low (often <2.5 L/min/m²) in decompensated AS
- **Gradient:** Elevated systolic gradient between LV and aorta (best with simultaneous aortic pressure)

**Acute Aortic Regurgitation Pattern:**
- RA: normal to elevated
- PCWP: markedly elevated (20–30 mmHg) with rapid y-descent
- CO/CI: low (<2.0 L/min/m²)
- Aortic pressure: wide pulse pressure in chronic AR; narrower in acute AR with LV failure
- **Gradient:** No LV-aortic gradient (open aortic valve)

#### Arterial Line Monitoring

**AS waveform:**
- Slowly rising systolic upstroke (delayed rise time)
- Narrow pulse pressure
- Sustained systolic plateau (anacrotic notch)

**AR waveform:**
- **Chronic:** Wide pulse pressure, rapid diastolic runoff (sloping diastolic line)
- **Acute:** May show relatively narrow pulse pressure if severe LV dysfunction present

### Laboratory Studies

| Test | Aortic Stenosis | Acute AR | Chronic AR |
|------|-----------------|----------|-----------|
| **Troponin (hs-cTn)** | Often elevated (demand ischemia) | Elevated if endocarditis | May be elevated during decompensation |
| **BNP/NT-proBNP** | Markedly elevated in decompensation | Very elevated in acute pulmonary edema | Elevated; prognostic |
| **Lactate** | May be elevated in shock | Elevated in cardiogenic shock | Elevated if decompensated |
| **Creatinine/eGFR** | Often elevated (prerenal from low CO) | Acute kidney injury common | May show baseline CKD |
| **Blood cultures** | Essential if endocarditis suspected | **Mandatory before antibiotics** | If subacute endocarditis |
| **CBC** | Assess for anemia (worsens physiology) | Elevated WBC if sepsis | Baseline |
| **Coagulation studies (PT/INR, PTT)** | Baseline if TAVR planned | Assess if DIC (severe sepsis) | If anticoagulation needed |

### Imaging Beyond Echocardiography

#### Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA)

**Indications:**
- **AS evaluation:** Aortic root diameter (assess for root dilatation), coronary ostia location, aortic valve anatomy, iliofemoral vessel assessment for TAVR access
- **Acute AR:** Assess for aortic dissection (Type A dissection is contraindication to TAVR, requires immediate surgery)
- **Calcium scoring:** Paradoxical LFGL-AS assessment

**ICU-specific:** CTA may require transfer from ICU; coordinate with structural heart team; IV contrast burden for renal function

#### Transesophageal Echocardiography (TEE)

**Indications:**
- **Acute AR + suspected endocarditis:** Assess for vegetations, perforations, abscess
- **Acute AR + aortic dissection:** Visualize intimal flap, pericardial effusion
- **Intraoperative guidance:** Real-time hemodynamic assessment during TAVR or surgery
- **Persistent shadowing on TTE:** Better visualization of valve anatomy

**ICU pearls:** TEE requires sedation; brief hemodynamic instability during probe passage; have resuscitation ready

## Management

### Severe Aortic Stenosis in the ICU: Hemodynamic Support

#### Fundamental Principles

> [!warning] **The AS Paradox**
> Patients with severe AS are **preload-dependent, afterload-sensitive, and contractility-driven**. Avoid diuretics and vasodilators unless pulmonary edema life-threatening. Maintain sinus rhythm. Target low-normal heart rate (55–70 bpm) for maximal diastolic filling time.

#### Hemodynamic Targets & Monitoring Strategy

| Parameter | Target | Rationale | ICU Action |
|-----------|--------|-----------|-----------|
| **SBP** | 100–120 mmHg | Maintain coronary perfusion without excessive afterload | Titrate vasopressor/vasodilator cautiously |
| **Heart rate** | 55–70 bpm | Maximize diastolic fill time; slow heart rates favor filling | Beta-blocker or AV nodal blocker if tachycardic |
| **LVEDP** | 15–18 mmHg | Optimize Frank-Starling; excessive LVEDP → pulmonary edema | Judicious diuretics; avoid hypovolemia |
| **Cardiac index** | >2.0 L/min/m² | Maintain forward flow | Inotrope if CI <2.0 despite preload optimization |
| **Urine output** | >0.5 mL/kg/hr | Indicator of perfusion | Monitor carefully; avoid aggressively diuresing |
| **Lactate** | <2 mmol/L | Marker of tissue hypoxia | Serial checks; if rising → escalate intervention |

#### Pharmacologic Support in AS

> [!warning] **Drug Strategy in Severe AS — Inotropes Are NOT First-Line**
> The hypertrophied AS ventricle faces a **fixed mechanical obstruction** — inotropes cannot meaningfully increase forward flow because the valve is the bottleneck, not contractility. Increased inotropy raises myocardial oxygen demand without improving output, and dobutamine's beta-2 vasodilation drops SVR against a fixed gradient, worsening hypotension. Registry evidence confirms this: dobutamine >5 mcg/kg/min in AS cardiogenic shock was associated with **100% incidence of death or recurrent shock**, vs. 57% in lower-dose users (Debry et al., EuroIntervention 2018; PMID 29741481). The same study showed BAV within 48 hours of starting inotropes was key to survival — emphasizing that **definitive intervention, not pharmacologic escalation, is the priority**.
>
> **First-line pharmacology:** Vasopressors — **norepinephrine** (most commonly used; mild beta-1 inotropy with alpha-dominant vasopressor effect) or **phenylephrine** (pure alpha-1; avoids tachycardia, may benefit AS physiology). **Vasopressin** is a non-adrenergic alternative specifically identified as advantageous in fixed-obstruction states including severe AS — it raises SVR without catecholamine-related tachycardia or increased myocardial oxygen demand (Filomia et al., ESC Heart Failure 2026; PMID 41711251).
>
> Inotropes have a narrow role as a **bridge only** in the subset with **concomitant significantly reduced EF** on top of AS, at low doses (dobutamine ≤5 mcg/kg/min), while expediting TAVR/SAVR. **NEVER use vasodilators as monotherapy** in decompensated AS.

| Drug | Mechanism | Dose | AS ICU Use | Caution |
|------|-----------|------|-----------|---------|
| **Dobutamine** | Beta-1 (inotrope), beta-2 (mild vasodilation), DA | 2–5 mcg/kg/min | **Use with caution and only as bridge** — does NOT overcome fixed obstruction; reserve for AS with **concomitant reduced EF** and low CI; tachycardia and vasodilation are harmful in AS | Risk of tachycardia, SVR drop, arrhythmias; pair with vasopressor |
| **Milrinone** | PDE-3 inhibitor (inotrope + vasodilator) | 0.25–0.75 mcg/kg/min | **Generally avoid in AS cardiogenic shock** — vasodilatory effect drops SVR and worsens hemodynamics against fixed obstruction; lusitropic effect is theoretical benefit; requires vasopressor to offset hypotension | Systemic vasodilation is particularly dangerous in AS; use only in exceptional circumstances |
| **Norepinephrine** | Alpha-1 > beta-1 | 0.01–0.5 mcg/kg/min | **First-line vasopressor in AS cardiogenic shock** — most commonly used in CS registries (64.7%); alpha-dominant with mild beta-1 inotropy; maintains perfusion pressure | More tachycardia than phenylephrine; generally well tolerated |
| **Phenylephrine** | Pure alpha-1 agonist | 0.5–1.4 mcg/kg/min | **Alternative vasopressor** — pure vasoconstriction without tachycardia; may be preferred when tachycardia is already problematic (e.g., AF with RVR in AS) | Reflex bradycardia may actually benefit AS filling; avoid in AR |
| **Vasopressin** | V1 receptor (non-adrenergic vasoconstriction) | 0.03–0.04 units/min IV (fixed dose) | **Emerging preference in fixed-obstruction CS** — raises SVR without catecholamine tachycardia or increased myocardial O₂ demand; specifically identified as advantageous in severe AS (Filomia et al., ESC HF 2026) | Splanchnic vasoconstriction; do not titrate above 0.04 units/min |
| **Nitroglycerin** | Nitric oxide donor; venodilator >> arteriodilator | 10–400 mcg/min IV | **Use cautiously only if**:pulmonary edema life-threatening; always pair with inotrope | **Risk:** Severe hypotension, syncope, ischemia; AVOID monotherapy |
| **Nitroprusside** | Direct vasodilation (arterial + venous) | 0.3–2 mcg/kg/min | **AVOID in AS unless** extreme circumstances (pulmonary edema + inotrope). **NOT recommended** | Causes reflex tachycardia, worsens ischemia, systemic hypotension |
| **Beta-blocker** (if tachycardic) | AV nodal blockade, reduced dP/dt | Esmolol: 50–200 mcg/kg/min loading, then infusion | **Appropriate if AF or tachycardia** present; slows rate, improves filling | May reduce contractility; avoid if HF signs prominent |

#### Specific ICU Scenarios in Severe AS

**Scenario 1: Decompensated AS with pulmonary edema but preserved BP**
- **Goal:** Diuresis + gentle inotropy
- **Approach:** IV furosemide 40–80 mg IV q4–6h; monitor UOP, orthostatic BP
- **Inotrope:** Dobutamine 2–5 mcg/kg/min (modest dose to maintain CO without tachycardia)
- **Monitor:** PCWP target 15–18 mmHg; CI >2.0 L/min/m²

**Scenario 2: Cardiogenic shock from severe AS (SBP <90 mmHg, CI <2.0)**
- **Emergent TAVR or surgical AVR** is definitive therapy (see TAVR vs SAVR section)
- **Bridge therapy** (if TAVR delayed):
  - Dobutamine 5–10 mcg/kg/min + Phenylephrine 0.5–1.4 mcg/kg/min (titrate to SBP 90–100 mmHg, CI >2.0)
  - Consider **balloon aortic valvuloplasty (BAV)** as mechanical bridge (see BAV section)
  - Goal: Stabilize patient, reduce lactate, improve end-organ perfusion for 24–48 hours until definitive intervention
- **Avoid:** Diuretics (worsen preload-dependent state); vasodilators monotherapy (collapse BP)

**Scenario 3: Severe AS + new atrial fibrillation with RVR**
- **Immediate action:** Rate control FIRST (goal HR 60–80 bpm)
- **Drugs:** IV esmolol loading 0.5 mg/kg over 1 min, then infusion 50–200 mcg/kg/min; or IV diltiazem (but watch for hypotension)
- **Cardioversion:** Electrical cardioversion if unstable/hemodynamic compromise
- **Anticoagulation:** Start UFH or LMWH if no contraindication (endocarditis workup pending)
- **Plan:** AFib in AS is high-risk; expedite TAVR/AVR discussion

**Scenario 4: Severe AS + acute coronary syndrome (ACS) features (chest pain, troponin rise)**
- **Challenge:** Troponin elevation common in severe AS from demand ischemia; distinguish from obstructive CAD
- **Approach:**
  - Obtain 12-lead ECG (assess ST changes, localization)
  - Coronary angiography if STEMI pattern or hemodynamic instability
  - If NSTEMI + severe AS: revascularization often deferred until after AVR (TAVR or surgery) to avoid procedural complications
  - Antiplatelet therapy (aspirin, clopidogrel) mandatory if stent placed; coordinate with surgery if urgent AVR planned
- **ICU support:** Dobutamine + phenylephrine as above; avoid further vasodilation

### TAVR vs SAVR: Decision-Making in the ICU

#### Summary of Evidence & Mortality Data

| Setting | TAVR 30-Day Mortality | SAVR 30-Day Mortality | Notes |
|---------|----------------------|----------------------|-------|
| **Cardiogenic shock** | 13–28% | 25–40% | Emergency TAVR appears superior to emergency SAVR (risk difference −0.15, 95% CI −0.25 to −0.04; Deng et al. 2025); most data from observational registries, not RCTs |
| **Decompensated (non-shock)** | 4–16% | 8–20% | TAVR favorable; lower morbidity |
| **Elective high-risk** (STS >4%) | 8–12% | 12–15% | TAVR non-inferior; frailty/comorbidities favor TAVR |
| **Elective intermediate-risk** | 3–5% | 3–5% | Similar outcomes; valve choice (bioprosthetic vs mechanical) influences long-term decisions |
| **Low-risk elective** | 2–4% | 2–3% | Outcomes similar; durability concerns favor SAVR in younger patients |

**Key trials cited (see Evidence & Landmark Trials section):**
- **PARTNER 3 (2019):** TAVR non-inferior to SAVR in intermediate-risk patients; fewer major adverse events
- **EXCEL trial:** TAVR in low-risk patients; durable at 3 years with favorable outcomes

#### Structural Heart Team Decision-Making

| Factor | TAVR Advantage | SAVR Advantage | ICU Implication |
|--------|---|---|---|
| **Age >75 years** | Yes (reduced mortality) | — | Prioritize TAVR if anatomically suitable |
| **Cardiogenic shock** | Yes (13–28% vs 25–40% SAVR) | — | **Urgent TAVR appears superior to emergency SAVR**; registry data only — no RCT comparison |
| **LVEF <30%** | Yes (TAVR outcomes better) | — | SAVR high-risk; TAVR preferred |
| **Frailty, high STS score (>8%)** | Yes (lower morbidity) | — | Surgical risk unacceptable; TAVR only option |
| **Aortic root too large (>50 mm)** | — | Yes (better long-term durability) | TAVR may require root enlargement; consider SAVR |
| **Severe AR (secondary)** | — | Yes (SAVR corrects; TAVR may worsen) | AR contraindication to TAVR; requires surgery |
| **Small aortic annulus (<19 mm)** | — | Yes (TAVR sizing challenge) | SAVR more predictable; TAVR requires special sizing/devices |
| **Bicuspid AV with fusion pattern** | — | Yes (better anatomy assessment) | TAVR may work but higher deployment failure risk |
| **Age <60 years (elective, low-risk)** | — | Yes (mechanical valve durability) | SAVR preferred; future reinterventions likely with bioprosthesis |
| **Hostile chest (post-sternotomy, radiation)** | Yes (avoid resternotomy) | — | TAVR only safe option |
| **Septic endocarditis** | — | Yes (requires vegetectomy, abscess management) | Surgery essential; TAVR inadequate for tissue debridement |

#### ICU-Specific Timing for TAVR

> [!warning] **Cardiogenic Shock: Timing is Destiny**
> Patients who undergo **urgent TAVR within 48 hours of CS onset** have significantly better outcomes than those delayed >72 hours. **Long ICU stay (>7 days) prior to TAVR independently predicts worse outcomes.** Early structural heart team involvement and expedited imaging (TEE, CTA) shortens door-to-balloon time.

**Target timeline:**
- **Diagnosis → Structural heart team consult:** <4 hours
- **Consult → TAVR decision:** <8 hours (TEE/CTA in parallel)
- **TAVR decision → Procedure:** <24 hours (goal: within 48 hours of shock onset)

### Balloon Aortic Valvuloplasty (BAV): Bridge Therapy

#### Indications & Mechanism

- **Bridge to definitive AVR:** Emergent hemodynamic stabilization in cardiogenic shock awaiting TAVR or SAVR
- **Diagnostic tool:** In rare cases, assesses contractile reserve (increased post-BAV EF suggests recoverable myocardium)
- **Palliative:** End-stage patients declining surgery/TAVR

#### Technique

- **Access:** Femoral arterial (retrograde across aortic valve)
- **Balloon:** 20–25 mm diameter noncompliant balloon; single or double balloon technique
- **Complications:** Aortic regurgitation (10–20% develop new/worsened AR), stroke (1–2%), ventricular perforation, MI

#### Hemodynamic Results & Outcome

- **Acute effect:** AVA increases from <0.6 cm² to 1.0–1.2 cm²; gradient decreases 50%; cardiac output increases transiently
- **Durability:** Restenosis occurs in 50–70% within 6 months; **NOT a long-term solution**
- **Mortality:** **Approximately 50% of BAV-only patients die within 90 days if definitive AVR not performed**

#### ICU Use Strategy

**Step 1:** ICU patient in cardiogenic shock; TAVR/SAVR scheduled but delayed (imaging pending, OR occupied, transfer logistics)

**Step 2:** Emergency BAV as bridge
- Stabilizes hemodynamics: ↑ CI, ↓ lactate, ↑ UOP
- Buys 24–48 hours for definitive intervention
- Allows further workup if needed (coronary angiography, advanced imaging)

**Step 3:** Following BAV
- Monitor closely for restenosis (repeat echo at 24–48 hours)
- Worsened AR post-BAV → urgent consideration for urgent surgery (may not be TAVR candidate if developed severe AR)
- Proceed with TAVR/SAVR within 48–72 hours

> [!tip] **BAV Mortality Pearl**
> If BAV performed, **definitive AVR (TAVR or SAVR) must occur within 90 days**; waiting longer dramatically increases mortality. BAV + delayed surgery = 50% 90-day mortality. Commit to timeline at time of BAV.

### Acute Aortic Regurgitation: Medical Bridge & Urgent Intervention

#### Immediate ICU Management

**Goal:** Reduce regurgitant volume, maintain forward flow, stabilize while awaiting surgical/interventional management

#### Hemodynamic Strategy for Acute AR

| Parameter | Target | Drug Choice | Rationale |
|-----------|--------|------------|-----------|
| **Heart rate** | 80–100 bpm (non-dissection) | Beta-blocker, calcium channel blocker | ↓ diastolic fill time for AR reduces regurgitant volume; **Exception: aortic dissection-related AR** → beta-blockade is critical to reduce dP/dt regardless of HR effect on AR |
| **SBP** | 90–100 mmHg | Vasodilator (nicardipine, hydralazine) | Reduces afterload → ↓ regurgitant volume |
| **Cardiac output** | CI >2.0 L/min/m² | Inotrope (dobutamine, milrinone) if CI falls | Maintain forward flow |
| **PCWP** | 15–20 mmHg | Diuretics if pulmonary edema | Gentle diuresis; avoid hypovolemia |

**Specific drugs for acute AR:**

| Drug | Mechanism | Dose | Use in Acute AR | Caution |
|------|-----------|------|---|---|
| **Nicardipine** | Calcium channel blocker, vasodilator | 0.5–2.25 mg/hr IV; titrate q5–15 min | **PREFERRED vasodilator** in acute AR; reduces afterload, mild negative inotrope offset by afterload reduction | Monitor BP; avoid if hypotensive |
| **Hydralazine** | Direct arteriolar vasodilation | 10–20 mg IV q4–6h or 0.5–1.4 mcg/kg/min infusion | Good for afterload reduction; less titratable than nicardipine | Reflex tachycardia (desired in AR context) |
| **Nitroprusside** | Arterial + venous vasodilation | 0.3–2 mcg/kg/min | Can use if nicardipine insufficient; rapid onset | Tachyphylaxis; cyanide toxicity with prolonged use; avoid >24 hours |
| **Nitroglycerin** | Venodilator > arteriodilator | 10–400 mcg/min | **AVOID monotherapy**; may reduce afterload insufficiently; pair with arteriolar agent | Excessive venodilation may worsen preload |
| **Esmolol** | Beta-1 blocker | 50–200 mcg/kg/min | **CRITICAL in acute AR**: slows HR (reduce AR regurgitation window); pair with vasodilator | Monitor EF; avoid if HF evident |
| **Diltiazem** | Calcium channel blocker | 0.25 mg/kg IV loading, then 5–15 mg/hr | Alternative AV nodal blocker; also vasodilator | Negative inotrope; risk if EF very low |
| **Dobutamine** | Inotrope + mild vasodilation | 2–10 mcg/kg/min | **Use cautiously**: inotropic support if CI low, BUT tachycardia may worsen AR | Risk of tachycardia; pair with rate-limiting agent |
| **Milrinone** | Inotrope + vasodilator | 0.25–0.75 mcg/kg/min | Favorable hemodynamics (inotropy + vasodilation), but systemic hypotension; requires vasopressor | Pair with norepinephrine (preferred) rather than phenylephrine in AR |
| **Norepinephrine** | Alpha-1 + beta-1 | 0.01–0.5 mcg/kg/min | **PREFERRED vasopressor in hypotensive acute AR** — provides inotropic support alongside vasoconstriction; better tolerated than pure alpha agents | Risk of tachycardia; acceptable in AR context |
| **Phenylephrine** | Pure alpha-1 agonist | 0.5–5 mcg/kg/min | **AVOID in acute AR** — pure vasoconstriction without inotropy worsens AR severity by increasing afterload without improving forward flow | Preferred in AS but contraindicated in AR |

#### IABP: Contraindication in Acute AR

> [!warning] **ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION**
> **Intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) is CONTRAINDICATED in acute AR.** IABP inflation in diastole (when aortic valve is open) increases AR volume directly into left ventricle → **hemodynamic deterioration, acute pulmonary edema, cardiogenic shock escalation.** IABP has been associated with **increased mortality in acute AR.** Use alternative mechanical support (Impella, ECMO) if needed.

#### Mechanical Circulatory Support (if medical management fails)

- **Impella 2.5 or 5.0:** Transaortic pump; provides forward flow, unloads LV — preferred MCS in acute AR
- **Peripheral VA-ECMO:** Use with caution — increases LV afterload, which can worsen AR severity. If VA-ECMO required, consider concurrent LV unloading (Impella + ECMO "ECPELLA" strategy) to mitigate this
- **LAVA-ECMO (Left Atrial to Aortic ECMO):** Emerging strategy at experienced centers; avoids aortic afterload increase inherent to peripheral VA-ECMO; may be preferable in severe AR with refractory shock
- **Goal:** Bridge to surgery (acute AR is **surgical emergency, not TAVR indication**)

> [!warning] VA-ECMO Caution in Acute AR
> Peripheral VA-ECMO increases aortic afterload and can worsen AR regurgitant volume, potentially accelerating hemodynamic deterioration. Prefer Impella; if VA-ECMO is necessary, LV unloading or LAVA-ECMO at experienced centers should be considered (Miller PE et al., JACC Advances 2024).

#### Etiologic Management

**If endocarditis (most common cause of acute AR in ICU):**
1. **Blood cultures** × 3 before antibiotics
2. **Empiric antibiotics** (vancomycin 15–20 mg/kg q12h IV + gentamicin 3–5 mg/kg q12h IV)
3. **TEE:** Assess vegetations, abscess, dehiscence, pseudoaneurysm
4. **Cardiac surgery consultation:** Most endocarditis cases with hemodynamic compromise require urgent surgery (vegetectomy, debridement, valve replacement)

**If aortic dissection (Type A):**
1. **CTA chest with IV contrast** to confirm diagnosis
2. **Immediate cardiac surgery consult** (Type A dissection = surgical emergency)
3. **Blood pressure control:** Target SBP 100–120 mmHg; reduce dP/dt (beta-blocker + vasodilator)
4. **TAVR is NOT indicated** in acute dissection with AR; requires surgical repair of aortic root

**If post-balloon aortic valvuloplasty (new severe AR):**
1. Likely requires urgent surgical valve replacement (TAVR may be inadequate if valve destruction)

#### Transition to Definitive Therapy

- **Urgent surgical AVR** is definitive treatment for acute AR (usually within 24–48 hours)
- **TAVR role:** Limited; indicated only if patient not surgical candidate AND AR is secondary (e.g., from aortic root dilatation, not organic valve destruction)
- **Outcomes:** Surgical AVR mortality 5–15% in acute AR with shock; delay >48 hours significantly worsens outcomes

### Chronic Aortic Regurgitation: Decompensation Management

#### Recognition of Decompensation

Patients with chronic AR may present with:
- Acute dyspnea, orthopnea (decompensated heart failure)
- New or worsened edema, ascites
- Reduced exercise tolerance
- Syncope (suggests severe aortic root dilatation or acute mechanical event)

#### Imaging & Grading

**Echocardiography:**
- Assess AR severity (vena contracta, regurgitant volume, fraction)
- Measure LV dimensions (LVEDD, LVESD) and EF
- Assess ascending aorta diameter (>50 mm suggests need for surgical intervention regardless of AR grade)

#### Management of Decompensated Chronic AR

**Medical management (initial stabilization):**
- **Diuretics:** IV furosemide for pulmonary edema; titrate to euvolemia
- **Vasodilators:** ACE inhibitor (lisinopril, enalapril) or direct vasodilator (hydralazine) to reduce afterload
- **Beta-blockers:** Esmolol or metoprolol to reduce dP/dt (slows aortic diastolic runoff)
- **Inotropic support if hypotensive:** Dobutamine, milrinone (similar to AS strategy)

**Definitive therapy:**
- **Surgical AVR:** Indicated if EF declining, LVEDD >75 mm, LVESD >50 mm, or acute decompensation unresponsive to medical therapy
- **TAVR role:** Increasingly used in inoperable patients with chronic AR; less ideal than SAVR but available option if anatomy suitable
- **Aortic root replacement:** Consider if aorta >50 mm or Marfan/connective tissue disorder

### Low-Flow Low-Gradient Aortic Stenosis: Special Considerations

#### Classical LFGL-AS (EF <50%)

**Diagnosis & Confirmation:**
1. **Echo findings:** AVA <1.0 cm², mean gradient <40 mmHg, EF <50%, SVI <35 mL/m²
2. **Dobutamine stress echo (DSE):**
   - If AVA increases >0.2 cm² or gradient decreases: Pseudo-severe AS (mild obstruction); conservative management
   - If AVA <1.0 cm² persists and gradient increases: True severe AS; worse prognosis; requires intervention

**ICU Management:**
- **Inotropic support:** Dobutamine 5–10 mcg/kg/min to assess contractile reserve
- **Hemodynamic targets:** Maintain SBP >90, CI >2.0 L/min/m²
- **Avoid aggressive diuresis:** Reduced preload worsens CO in decompensated AS + HF

**Intervention decision:**
- **True severe LFGL-AS:** High mortality without AVR (>50% at 1 year); TAVR superior to SAVR in this cohort (better outcomes, lower mortality)
- **Pseudo-severe AS:** Conservative management; focus on HF therapy, monitor closely

#### Paradoxical LFGL-AS (EF ≥50%)

**Diagnosis & Confirmation:**
1. **Echo findings:** AVA 1.0–1.5 cm² ("mild-moderate"), mean gradient <40 mmHg, EF ≥50%, SVI <35 mL/m²
2. **Mechanism:** Restrictive LV physiology from severe concentric LV hypertrophy + diastolic dysfunction → reduced stroke volume despite normal contractility
3. **CT aortic valve calcium scoring:**
   - **Men:** Agatston **≥2,000** = confirmed hemodynamically significant AS; TAVR likely beneficial
   - **Women:** Agatston **≥1,200** = confirmed hemodynamically significant AS; TAVR likely beneficial
   - *Note: DSE is inconclusive in ~55% of classical LFGL-AS cases, reinforcing CT calcium scoring as a complementary — not competing — modality (Clavel MA & Pibarot P, JACC Cardiovasc Imaging 2024)*

**ICU Management:**
- **Avoid aggressive diuretics:** Restrictive physiology is preload-dependent
- **Target low-normal heart rate (60 bpm):** Maximize diastolic fill time for stiff ventricle
- **Gentle inotropic support if CI falls:** Dobutamine at low dose (2–5 mcg/kg/min)
- **Rate control if AFib develops:** Slow ventricular response to maintain filling time

**Intervention decision:**
- **High calcium burden (plausible AS) + clinical deterioration:** TAVR outcomes favorable (similar to classical LFGL-AS)
- **Low calcium burden (true mild AS) + symptoms from other cause:** Conservative management; investigate other etiologies of dyspnea/syncope

## Clinical Pearls: ICU-Focused Insights

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls — Aortic Valve Disease
> 1. **The AS + Sepsis Trap:** Severe AS can mimic septic shock (fever, elevated lactate, hypotension from low forward flow). Always obtain urgent echo in febrile critically ill patients with known AS. Blood cultures + TEE differentiate endocarditis from other causes. Premature antibiotics without cardiac imaging can delay necessary TAVR/AVR by hours.
> 2. **IABP Contraindication in AR:** IABP inflates in diastole, directly increasing regurgitant volume into the failing LV. This paradoxically worsens hemodynamics. Use **Impella or ECMO** instead for mechanical support in acute AR — placing an IABP in undifferentiated cardiogenic shock is a common, potentially deadly error.
> 3. **The Low-Gradient AS Pitfall:** AVA 1.2 cm², gradient 35 mmHg, EF 55%, SVI 30 mL/m² can be true severe AS. Obtain **CT aortic valve calcium scoring** — Agatston ≥2,000 AU (men) or ≥1,200 AU (women) confirms severity despite low gradient (updated validated thresholds per Clavel et al. and ACC/AHA guidelines). Failure to recognize paradoxical LFLG-AS leads to delayed intervention and worse outcomes.
> 4. **Urgent TAVR Timing in Cardiogenic Shock:** TAVR within 24–48 hours of AS-related cardiogenic shock onset (30-day mortality 13–28%) outperforms delayed intervention (>40–50% at 5 days). Longer ICU stays before TAVR independently predict worse outcomes. Stabilize with medical bridge while expediting intervention — don't wait for the "perfect" moment.
> 5. **BAV Mortality & 90-Day Window:** BAV restenosis occurs in 50–70% by 6 months. Definitive AVR (TAVR or SAVR) must occur within 90 days — mortality approaches 50% without it. At the time of BAV, commit to a definitive intervention plan.
> 6. **Chronic AR Decompensation & Aorta Size:** Ascending aorta >50 mm warrants surgical AVR ± root replacement regardless of AR severity or EF due to rupture risk. Use lower thresholds (>45 mm) in Marfan, Loeys-Dietz, or other connective tissue disorders.
> 7. **Rate Slowing in AS Is Therapeutic; Inotropes Are Not First-Line:** AF with RVR in severe AS is a hemodynamic emergency — cardiovert or rate-control immediately. Similarly, **inotropes are not first-line in AS cardiogenic shock** — increasing contractility against fixed obstruction raises myocardial oxygen demand without improving output. Dobutamine >5 mcg/kg/min in AS-CS was associated with 100% mortality or recurrent shock (Debry et al., 2018). Use **norepinephrine, phenylephrine, or vasopressin** to maintain SVR; reserve low-dose dobutamine only for the subset with concomitant significantly reduced EF while awaiting TAVR.
> 8. **"AS Angina" Doesn't Require Revascularization:** Troponin elevation and chest pain in severe AS are typically from demand ischemia, not obstructive CAD. Revascularization before AVR is often unnecessary. Focus on expedited TAVR/AVR.

### Landmark Randomized Controlled Trials

#### PARTNER Trial Series

**PARTNER 1 (2010–2012) — *New England Journal of Medicine***
- Compared TAVR to SAVR in inoperable and high-risk operable patients with severe AS
- **Key findings:**
  - TAVR non-inferior to SAVR in operable high-risk patients (30-day mortality: TAVR 3.4% vs SAVR 6.5%)
  - TAVR superior to medical therapy in inoperable patients (30-day mortality TAVR 5% vs medical 15%)
  - Mortality benefits sustained to 5 years
- **ICU relevance:** Established TAVR as standard of care for AS; shifted paradigm toward earlier intervention

**PARTNER 2 (2015–2016) — *New England Journal of Medicine***
- Extended TAVR to intermediate-risk patients
- **Key findings:** TAVR non-inferior to SAVR in intermediate-risk cohort; similar 30-day mortality (TAVR 2.4% vs SAVR 3.6%)
- **ICU relevance:** Broadened TAVR candidacy; intermediate-risk cardiogenic shock patients now preferentially offered TAVR

**PARTNER 3 (2019) — *New England Journal of Medicine***
- TAVR vs SAVR in low-risk patients
- **Key findings:**
  - TAVR superior to SAVR in low-risk patients (mortality TAVR 1% vs SAVR 2.4% at 1 year)
  - TAVR advantages: shorter hospital stay, reduced acute kidney injury, fewer major adverse events
  - Durable outcomes at 3 years
- **ICU relevance:** Validates TAVR as preferred approach across risk spectrum; earlier TAVR in decompensated AS justified

#### EXCEL Trial (2015–2020)

**Early Invasive Revascularization vs Ischemic Symptom–Guided Management for Elderly Patients with Non-ST–Elevation Myocardial Infarction (ISCHEMIA Study cohort comparison)**
- Examined early TAVR in low-risk AS patients
- **Outcomes:** Durable TAVR performance with favorable long-term survival
- **ICU relevance:** Supports aggressive early intervention strategy

### Cardiogenic Shock-Specific Evidence

**Mortality data integrated from recent literature (2020–2026):**
- **TAVR in cardiogenic shock:** 30-day mortality 13–28% (depending on severity and center experience)
- **TAVR in decompensated non-shock AS:** 30-day mortality 4–16%
- **Survivors of first 30 days post-urgent TAVR:** Similar long-term mortality trajectory to non-shock cohorts (2–5% annual mortality year 2–5)
- **BAV alone (without follow-up AVR within 90 days):** ~50% mortality at 90 days

## Key Guidelines

### ACC/AHA 2020 Valvular Heart Disease Guidelines

**Aortic Stenosis Indications for Intervention:**

| Scenario | Recommendation | Class | LOE |
|----------|---|---|---|
| **Severe symptomatic AS** | AVR (TAVR/SAVR) | **I** | A |
| **Severe asymptomatic AS, EF <50%** | AVR | **I** | B |
| **Asymptomatic severe AS, EF >50% + high surgical risk + EF decline >0.3/yr OR AVA decline >0.1 cm²/yr** | Consider AVR | **IIb** | C |
| **Asymptomatic severe AS undergoing other cardiac surgery** | AVR | **I** | C |
| **Moderate AS undergoing other cardiac surgery** | Consider AVR | **IIb** | C |

**Low-Flow Low-Gradient AS:**
- **Recommendation:** DSE or CT calcium imaging to confirm severe AS before intervention
- **TAVR:** Class I if confirmed severe AS and high surgical risk; outcomes superior to SAVR in this cohort

### ESC/EACTS 2021 Valvular Disease Guidelines

**Aortic Stenosis & Aortic Regurgitation Management:**

1. **Severe Symptomatic AS:** Immediate AVR (Class I)
2. **Asymptomatic Severe AS with EF <50% or stress-induced symptoms:** AVR (Class I)
3. **TAVR vs SAVR selection:**
   - **High/prohibitive surgical risk:** TAVR preferred (Class I)
   - **Intermediate surgical risk:** TAVR reasonable (Class IIa)
   - **Low surgical risk, age <60 years (elective):** SAVR preferred for mechanical valve durability (Class IIa)
4. **Acute Severe AR:** Urgent surgical intervention (Class I); medical management temporary only
5. **Chronic AR, EF decline or symptoms:** AVR (Class I)

### Recommendations Specific to ICU/Cardiogenic Shock

- **Early structural heart team involvement:** Shorten time to diagnosis and intervention
- **TAVR timing:** Pursue within 24–48 hours of shock onset if suitable anatomy
- **BAV:** Acceptable bridge if TAVR delayed, but commit to 90-day intervention window
- **Hemodynamic monitoring:** SwanGanz catheter useful for optimization; central lines standard
- **Medical bridge:** Dobutamine + phenylephrine as outlined; avoid diuretics and vasodilators as monotherapy

## Summary: Aortic Valve Disease in the Cardiac ICU

| Component | Key Takeaway |
|-----------|--------------|
| **Recognition** | AS: progressive dyspnea, chest pain, syncope; AR: acute dyspnea, hypotension, tachycardia |
| **Diagnosis** | Echocardiography cornerstone; TEE for endocarditis; CTA for dissection & calcium scoring |
| **AS Hemodynamics** | Preload-dependent, afterload-sensitive; maintain sinus rhythm & slow heart rate |
| **AR Hemodynamics** | Afterload reduction; avoid IABP; inotrope + vasodilator + rate control |
| **TAVR Timing** | Urgent TAVR within 24–48 hours of CS onset = lifesaving (30-day mortality 13–28%) |
| **LFGL-AS** | DSE (classical) or CT calcium (paradoxical) for diagnosis; TAVR superior to SAVR |
| **IABP in AR** | **CONTRAINDICATED** — use Impella or ECMO instead |
| **BAV** | Bridge therapy only; 90-day window for definitive AVR, or 50% mortality |
| **Drug strategy** | AS: vasopressors first (phenylephrine/NE); inotropes **not first-line** — fixed obstruction limits benefit; use only for concomitant reduced EF as bridge. AR: norepinephrine preferred vasopressor; afterload reduction cornerstone |
