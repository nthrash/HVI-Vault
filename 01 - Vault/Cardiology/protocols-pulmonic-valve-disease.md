## 1. Title & Overview

**Pulmonic Valve Disease** encompasses both **pulmonic stenosis (PS)** and **pulmonic regurgitation (PR)**, two distinct pathologic entities that frequently coexist in adults with congenital heart disease, particularly those with a history of tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) repair or other RVOT procedures. While PS creates an obstruction to right ventricular (RV) ejection leading to pressure overload and RV hypertrophy, PR causes RV volume overload from retrograde flow during diastole. Both lesions can progress to RV dysfunction and failure, but they demand different diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

In the ICU and structural heart setting, pulmonic valve disease is encountered frequently in adults with **adult congenital heart disease (ACHD)**, post-surgical patients, and those with rare acquired etiologies (carcinoid, rheumatic). The advent of **transcatheter pulmonary valve replacement (TPVR)** has transformed the management of PR in patients with suitable anatomy, offering a less invasive alternative to surgical valve replacement and reducing morbidity while preserving RV function.

> [!tip] **Why This Matters**
> Pulmonic valve disease often progresses silently — patients may remain remarkably well-compensated for years before decompensating acutely. In the ICU, iatrogenic volume overload, tachycardia, and pulmonary hypertension can unmask severe chronic PR or precipitate acute RV failure in patients with longstanding PS. Recognition and anticipatory management are critical for these patients.

## 2. Etiology & Risk Factors

### Pulmonic Stenosis (PS)

**Congenital (>90% of cases in children; remains most common in adults):**
- **Isolated valvar PS** — most frequent form; dysplastic valve with reduced commissural separation
- **Noonan syndrome** — dysplastic pulmonic valve, often severe; autosomal dominant RAS/RAF pathway mutations
- **Rubella syndrome** — in utero infection causing valvar hypoplasia; now rare in vaccinated populations
- **Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF)** — PS is one of the four cardinal features; varies in severity from mild to severe
- **Other syndromes** — Williams syndrome, Alagille syndrome, LEOPARD syndrome

**Structural variants:**
- **Subvalvar PS** — muscular outflow tract obstruction (RV hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy in TOF)
- **Supravalvar PS** — stenosis above the valve (rare; associated with Williams syndrome)

**Acquired PS (rare):**
- **Carcinoid syndrome** — serotonin-induced valve thickening and retraction; typically affects PS more than isolated PR in right-sided carcinoid
- **Rheumatic heart disease** — very rare; usually occurs with other valves involved
- **Pregnancy-related** — mechanical obstruction from increased venous return or cardiomyopathy (rare)

**Risk Factors:**
- Family history of congenital heart disease or syndromic PS
- Maternal teratogenic exposure (rubella infection, medications)
- Genetic syndromes with known cardiac involvement

### Pulmonic Regurgitation (PR)

**Post-procedural (most common in ACHD population):**
- **Post-PS repair** — intentional destruction of commissures during balloon pulmonary valvuloplasty (BPV)
- **Post-TOF repair** — transannular patch or outflow tract enlargement leaves incompetent valve
- **Post-RVOT surgery** — pulmonary artery or right ventricular outflow tract procedures
- **Post-TPVR** — residual or progressive PR after Melody, SAPIEN, or other transcatheter valve deployment

**Acquired etiologies:**
- **Endocarditis** — vegetative destruction of leaflets; leading cause of acute severe PR
- **Carcinoid syndrome** — valve retraction from serotonin; bilateral right-sided involvement typical
- **Rheumatic heart disease** — PR often secondary to rheumatic MS with pulmonary hypertension (Carey-Coombs lesion)
- **Pulmonary hypertension** — secondary PR from RV dilation and annular enlargement
- **Trauma** — penetrating or blunt cardiac injury with valve leaflet disruption

**Risk Factors:**
- Prior cardiac surgery or intervention on RVOT
- History of endocarditis
- Chronic pulmonary hypertension
- Unrepaired congenital heart disease (e.g., large ASD, VSD driving secondary RV dilation)

## 3. Pathophysiology

### Pulmonic Stenosis

**Acute phase:**
- Narrowed pulmonic orifice increases **RV-to-pulmonary artery pressure gradient** during systole
- RV develops **concentric hypertrophy** as a compensatory mechanism to maintain forward flow against increased afterload
- Hypertrophied RV maintains normal ejection fraction acutely but at the cost of elevated filling pressures

**Progressive phase (chronic/moderate to severe):**
- Persistent pressure overload leads to **RV diastolic dysfunction** — the hypertrophied ventricle becomes stiff and requires higher atrial pressures for adequate filling
- RV preload becomes increasingly critical; patients develop **dependence on atrial contraction** (loss of sinus rhythm is poorly tolerated)
- Coronary perfusion pressure to RV becomes compromised at the subendocardium (RV ischemia can occur with severe stenosis)

**Decompensation (severe, longstanding):**
- RV hypertrophy eventually leads to **RV systolic dysfunction**
- Tricuspid regurgitation (TR) develops secondary to RV dilation and annular stretch
- RV failure manifests as **hepatic congestion, peripheral edema, and reduced cardiac output**
- Right atrial pressure elevation can cause atrial arrhythmias (atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter)
- In severe PS, **paradoxical septal motion** and leftward bulging of the septum may compress the left ventricle, reducing LV filling

**Hemodynamic thresholds (standardized from catheterization):**
- **Mild PS:** Peak-to-peak transvalvular gradient <36 mmHg; RV pressure <50 mmHg systolic
- **Moderate PS:** Gradient 36–64 mmHg; RV pressure 50–80 mmHg systolic
- **Severe PS:** Gradient >64 mmHg; RV pressure >80 mmHg systolic

### Pulmonic Regurgitation

**Acute phase:**
- Incomplete valve closure during diastole allows **retrograde flow into the RV**
- RV acutely receives an **increased stroke volume** (forward flow + regurgitant volume)
- The RV is compliant and initially tolerates volume overload well; RV dilation may be minimal acutely

**Progressive/chronic phase (the "sleeper" lesion):**
- **RV volume overload persists** day after day, year after year
- RV dilates progressively to accommodate the regurgitant volume (**eccentric hypertrophy**)
- Initially, RV ejection fraction remains normal (dilated but well-compensated)
- Over time, the **RV myocardium fatigues** — wall stress increases, afterload rises, and systolic function declines
- **RV systolic dysfunction develops insidiously**; patient may remain asymptomatic until late in the disease course
- Secondary tricuspid regurgitation develops from RV dilation and annular stretch
- Right atrial enlargement and eventual right heart failure ensue

**Mechanism of decompensation:**
- The critical point occurs when **RV diastolic compliance declines** and wall stress becomes excessive
- Increased **end-diastolic pressure** impairs coronary perfusion and triggers ischemia
- The ventricle exhausts its compensatory capacity → rapid deterioration in function
- Patients may decompensate rapidly once systolic dysfunction begins

**Timing of intervention:**
- Unlike left-sided valve disease, PR tolerance is high and prolonged
- However, **RV remodeling becomes irreversible once significant systolic dysfunction develops**
- Current evidence supports intervention when:
  - RV end-diastolic volume index (RVEDVI) >160 mL/m² (by CMR)
  - RV ejection fraction declines to 45–50% or below
  - Sustained arrhythmia develops (risk marker for functional decline)
  - Progressive exercise intolerance despite normal resting function

### Carcinoid Heart Disease

In patients with carcinoid syndrome (neuroendocrine tumors, typically ileal):
- **Serotonin, tachykinins, and other vasoactive substances** cause **fibrotic thickening and retraction** of right-sided valve leaflets
- Results in **combined PS and PR** (valve becomes stenotic and regurgitant simultaneously)
- Tricuspid valve also affected (common); pulmonic involvement depends on serotonin burden
- Pathology: endocardial fibrosis with **retracted, leaflet-restricted motion** (not dynamic stenosis)
- Medical management (somatostatin analogs, beta-blockers) may slow progression but does not reverse valve changes
- Surgical or transcatheter valve replacement required for significant hemodynamic lesions

## 4. Clinical Presentation

### Pulmonic Stenosis

**Mild-to-moderate PS (often asymptomatic):**
- Many patients with mild PS are discovered incidentally on screening echocardiography
- Exertional dyspnea may be present but is often attributed to deconditioning
- Palpitations from atrial ectopy or paroxysmal atrial fibrillation

**Severe PS or decompensated PS:**
- **Dyspnea** — exertional dyspnea that worsens with activity or pregnancy; orthopnea/PND if RV failure develops
- **Chest discomfort** — atypical chest pain or anginal discomfort (from RV ischemia with severe stenosis); syncope can occur with severe stenosis (RV pressure >100 mmHg, inadequate systemic perfusion with exertion)
- **Fatigue and reduced exercise tolerance**
- **Palpitations** — irregular heart rhythm, presyncope with arrhythmias
- **Signs of right heart failure** — peripheral edema, hepatomegaly, ascites (late finding)

**Physical Exam Findings:**
- **Systolic ejection murmur** — best heard at the left upper sternal border (pulmonary area); crescendo-decrescendo character; radiates to left shoulder and back
- **Murmur increases with inspiration** (Carvallo sign) if RV dilation is present
- **Wide splitting of S2** — from delayed pulmonic component (delayed RV ejection); more pronounced with inspiration
- **RV heave** — palpable RV impulse at the left sternal border if RV hypertrophy is significant
- **Prominent a wave in jugular venous pulse (JVP)** — from forceful RV contraction pushing blood back into RA
- **Hepatomegaly** — from hepatic congestion if RV pressure is markedly elevated
- **Cyanosis** — rare in pure PS; suggests right-to-left shunt (e.g., PFO/ASD with elevated RA pressure in severe PS)

> [!warning] **Red Flags**
> - **Syncope with exertion** in PS patient → suggests critical obstruction or RV ischemia; requires urgent intervention
> - **New onset atrial fibrillation** in PS → rapid ventricular rate may precipitate hemodynamic collapse (RV preload-dependent)
> - **Cyanosis** → suggests shunt-level defect; risk of paradoxical embolism and stroke

### Pulmonic Regurgitation

**Mild-to-moderate PR (often silent):**
- Frequently discovered as an incidental finding on echo during follow-up of congenital disease
- Patients may be completely asymptomatic for decades
- Dyspnea on exertion is typically absent until late in the disease course

**Moderate-to-severe PR (progressive decompensation):**
- **Exertional dyspnea** — develops gradually; often attributed to other causes (deconditioning, anxiety)
- **Reduced exercise tolerance** — unable to keep up with peers or perform activities previously tolerated
- **Palpitations** — from RV hypertension, tricuspid regurgitation, or arrhythmias (atrial fibrillation common)
- **Fatigue and malaise**
- **Syncope or presyncope** — from arrhythmia (supraventricular or ventricular)
- **Signs of RV/right heart failure** — peripheral edema, hepatomegaly, ascites (indicates advanced disease)

**Physical Exam Findings:**
- **Diastolic murmur** — high-pitched, decrescendo murmur best heard at the left lower sternal border (4th–5th intercostal space) with patient leaning forward and breath held in expiration; increases with inspiration if secondary to pulmonary hypertension
- **Prominent P2** (pulmonic component of S2) — if pulmonary hypertension is present
- **RV heave** — from RV dilation and hypertrophy
- **Hyperdynamic RV impulse** — from increased stroke volume
- **Prominent a and v waves in JVP** — from secondary tricuspid regurgitation
- **Hepatomegaly and hepatic pulsatility** — from TR and elevated RA pressure
- **Peripheral edema** — in advanced disease with RV failure

> [!tip] **Clinical Pearl: The "Sleeper" Presentation**
> A 28-year-old with repaired TOF comes to the ED with "just fatigue and some shortness of breath the last few weeks." On exam, you hear a diastolic murmur and note RV heave. Echo shows severe PR with RV ejection fraction of 40%. The patient's own remark: "I didn't think anything was wrong; I just felt a little tired." This exemplifies why PR is dangerous — it silently destroys RV function. Always ask ACHD patients about exercise tolerance and perform careful echo follow-up.

### Carcinoid Heart Disease

- **Flushing, diarrhea, bronchospasm** — systemic manifestations of carcinoid syndrome
- **Dyspnea and fatigue** — from combined PS and PR with RV dysfunction
- **Palpitations** — from RV pressure/volume overload
- **Right heart failure signs** — peripheral edema, ascites, hepatomegaly

**Physical Exam:**
- **Systolic murmur at left upper sternal border** (PS component)
- **Diastolic murmur at left lower sternal border** (PR component)
- **Tricuspid systolic murmur** (common; from TR)
- **RV heave, prominent JVP**

## 5. Diagnostic Workup

### Electrocardiography (ECG)

**Pulmonic Stenosis:**
- **Right axis deviation** — from RV hypertrophy
- **RV strain pattern** — T-wave inversions in right precordial leads (V1–V3), sometimes extending to V4
- **Peaked P waves** — from RA enlargement (chronic elevation of RA pressure)
- **Right atrial enlargement** — tall P waves in II, III, aVF
- **Prolonged PR interval** — nonspecific finding with RV hypertrophy
- **Atrial fibrillation or flutter** — in severe or decompensated PS

**Pulmonic Regurgitation:**
- **Right axis deviation** — from RV dilation and hypertrophy
- **RV hypertrophy pattern** — tall R wave in V1, deep S wave in V5–V6
- **Left axis deviation of T waves** — unusual finding; suggests RV strain
- **Arrhythmias** — atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or ventricular ectopy (especially if RV dysfunction advanced)

### Transthoracic Echocardiography (TTE)

**Key measurements and findings:**

**Pulmonic Stenosis:**
- **Continuous wave Doppler across pulmonic valve** — quantifies peak systolic gradient (peak velocity converted to gradient using modified Bernoulli equation: gradient = 4 × velocity²)
- **RV cavity size** — normal or mildly dilated; assess for paradoxical septal motion (suggests significant RV pressure elevation)
- **RV wall thickness** — measure basal RV free wall; concentric hypertrophy typical
- **RV systolic function** — FAC (fractional area change) or RVEF if using 3D; preserved in uncomplicated PS
- **Valve morphology** — assess for dysplasia, leaflet thickness, degree of doming
- **Diastolic function** — evaluate for RV diastolic dysfunction (restrictive filling pattern common in severe PS)
- **RA size** — enlarged if significant RV hypertension
- **Hepatic vein flow** — assesses RA pressure non-invasively (hepatic vein S/D ratio reversal indicates elevated RA pressure)

**Pulmonic Regurgitation:**
- **Regurgitant jet width and density on color Doppler** — qualitative assessment (mild, moderate, severe); should always be correlated with quantitative data
- **Quantitative PR assessment** — regurgitant volume (mL/beat) and regurgitant fraction (%) by pulsed Doppler or cardiac output comparison method; regurgitant fraction >40% suggests significant PR; CMR is superior to echo for precise quantification (see CMR section)
- **Regurgitant volume and fraction** (by pulsed Doppler or cardiac output calculation) — useful for serial assessment
- **RV end-diastolic volume (RVEDV)** — typically indexed to body surface area (RVEDVI); severe PR often shows RVEDVI >120–160 mL/m²
- **RV systolic function** — RVEF, FAC; critical for timing intervention
- **RV end-systolic volume (RVESV)** — predicts degree of functional impairment
- **RA size and IVC diameter** — markers of RV failure
- **Tricuspid regurgitation** — secondary TR common; TR jet velocity estimates RV pressure (pulmonary hypertension indicator)
- **Atrial strain** — if available, RA strain may predict functional decline
- **Septal motion** — septal "bowing" into LV during diastole (reversed ventricular septal interdependence) suggests significant RV dilation

> [!tip] **Echo Tip: Assessing RV Function in PR**
> In a patient with chronic severe PR and normal resting RVEF, exercise stress echo can unmask declining RV function (failure of RVEF to increase with exercise or decline in global longitudinal strain). This may help identify the optimal timing for intervention before irreversible systolic dysfunction develops.

### Cardiac Magnetic Resonance (CMR)

**Superior for quantification and prognostication in PR:**
- **Gold standard for RV volume and ejection fraction measurement** — most accurate reproducible method
- **RV end-diastolic volume index (RVEDVI)** — cutoffs of 160 mL/m² associated with increased risk of arrhythmia and death
- **RV end-systolic volume index (RVESVI)** — >80 mL/m² suggests significant dysfunction
- **Regurgitant fraction** — CMR flow quantification is superior to echo for accurate regurgitant volume and fraction
- **Myocardial fibrosis assessment** — late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) may predict functional decline and arrhythmia risk
- **Ventricular septal motion** — visualizes septal mechanics and degree of interdependence

**CMR is **recommended** for:**
- Quantifying RV volumes and function in suspected severe PR when echo is inconclusive
- Serial assessment of PR progression (baseline and follow-up at 1–3 year intervals)
- Detecting myocardial fibrosis (may guide intervention timing)
- Pre-intervention planning (anatomy, conduit assessment in complex cases)

### Cardiac Catheterization

**Indications:**
- **Diagnosis and hemodynamic assessment of PS** when noninvasive findings are discordant with clinical presentation
- **Therapeutic intervention** — balloon pulmonary valvuloplasty for suitable PS anatomy
- **Evaluation of RV function and filling pressures** in suspected RV dysfunction
- **Assessment of coronary artery involvement** in carcinoid or complex anatomy pre-intervention
- **Pre-TPVR evaluation** — RVOT anatomy, pulmonary artery dimensions, assessment for left main coronary artery compression risk

**Hemodynamic parameters:**
- **RV systolic pressure** — direct measurement; >80 mmHg indicates severe stenosis
- **RV end-diastolic pressure (RVEDP)** — elevated (>12 mmHg) indicates RV diastolic dysfunction or failure
- **RV-to-PA gradient** — peak-to-peak gradient; used to classify severity
- **RV stroke work index (RVSWI)** — may be reduced in RV failure (cardiogenic shock physiology)
- **Cardiac output** — reduced in severe PS or PR with RV decompensation
- **RA pressure waveform** — prominent a and v waves with rapid y descent indicate tricuspid regurgitation
- **Pulmonary artery pressure and pulmonary vascular resistance** — assess for secondary pulmonary hypertension

**Angiography:**
- **RV ventriculography** — visualizes valve morphology, degree of stenosis, and RV wall motion
- **Pulmonary artery angiography** — assesses PA size, branch anatomy, and degree of regurgitation
- **Selective coronary angiography** — identifies coronary origins and relationship to RVOT (critical pre-TPVR)

### Computed Tomography (CT)

**Cardiac CT is valuable for:**
- **RVOT anatomy and dimensions** — critical pre-TPVR planning; assess diameter, length, and angulation
- **Calcification assessment** — degree of valve calcification affects balloon vs. self-expanding valve choice
- **Identification of conduit material and integrity** — in post-surgical patients
- **Relationship to adjacent structures** — distance to coronary arteries, aorta, and other critical structures
- **Complex anatomy** — in patients with syndromic or post-surgical PS with unusual anatomy

### Exercise Testing

**Role in asymptomatic PS and PR:**
- **Identifies blunted RV output response** — in PS, failure of RV pressure to rise appropriately with exercise or paradoxical increase in gradient
- **Exercise-induced arrhythmia** — ventricular ectopy with exercise may identify high-risk patients
- **Oxygen desaturation** — suggests right-to-left shunt (PFO/ASD) or right heart failure
- **Abnormal blood pressure response** — failure to rise, paradoxical decrease, or excessive rise suggests significant obstruction
- **Dyspnea and functional capacity** — objective measure of exercise tolerance for serial comparison

## 6. Management

### Pulmonic Stenosis

#### Medical Management (Supportive, Pre-Intervention)

**Asymptomatic mild-to-moderate PS:**
- **Observation** — natural history is generally benign; progression uncommon
- **Serial TTE** — every 1–3 years to detect progression; more frequently if gradient >36 mmHg or RV dysfunction develops
- **Restricted activity** — avoid strenuous isometric exercise and competitive sports (risk of syncope with exertion)
- **Avoid dehydration** — maintain preload; dehydration reduces cardiac output and exacerbates gradient
- **Genetic counseling** — for syndromic PS (Noonan, Williams) and family screening

**Symptomatic or severe PS (before intervention):**
- **Diuretics** — cautious use only; excessive diuresis reduces preload and can worsen gradient; use only for pulmonary edema
- **Beta-blockers** — reduce contractility and lower gradient; useful for symptomatic patients awaiting intervention or those with syncope risk; also manage tachycardia and ectopy
  - Example: metoprolol 25–50 mg BID or atenolol 25–50 mg daily
  - Caution: negative inotropic effects may worsen RV function if patient has impending RV failure
- **Avoid NSAIDs and excessive sodium** — maintain euvolemia
- **Antibiotic prophylaxis** — NOT recommended for isolated, uncomplicated native valve PS. Prophylaxis IS reasonable (Class IIa, ACC/AHA 2020; reaffirmed 2025 ACHD guideline) for dental procedures in patients with PS who have: (a) prosthetic valves or prosthetic material, (b) prior endocarditis, (c) unrepaired cyanotic CHD, or (d) repaired CHD with residual shunts/regurgitation adjacent to prosthetic sites

#### Interventional Management

**Balloon Pulmonary Valvuloplasty (BPV)**

**Indications (2025 ACC/AHA ACHD Guideline):**
- **Symptomatic moderate or severe PS** (peak gradient >36 mmHg or peak velocity >3 m/s) — **Class I**
- **Asymptomatic severe PS** (peak gradient >64 mmHg or peak velocity >4 m/s) — **Class IIa**
- **Symptomatic patients with cyanosis** from right-to-left shunt (e.g., PFO/ASD with PS) or significant exercise intolerance — regardless of gradient threshold
- **Pregnancy planning** in women with moderate-severe PS (reduce RV burden before conception)
- **Neonates/infants with critical PS** — bridge to definitive surgery if anatomy unsuitable for BPV

*Note: The ≥40–50 mmHg threshold in older literature reflects pediatric/surgical era norms. The 2025 guideline uses 36 mmHg (moderate) and 64 mmHg (severe) as standardized adult thresholds consistent with echocardiographic severity classification.*

**Procedure:**
- Percutaneous balloon catheterization over guide wire across stenotic valve
- Balloon inflated under fluoroscopy to disrupt fused commissures
- Goal: reduce peak-to-peak gradient to <36 mmHg
- Real-time hemodynamic assessment; repeat balloon dilation if gradient remains >36 mmHg

**Success rates:**
- **Excellent in congenital valvar PS:** >90% procedural success; gradient reduction by 50–70% typical
- **Success lower in dysplastic PS** (Noonan syndrome) due to abnormal leaflet tissue; may be suboptimal or require repeat intervention

**Complications (5–15% of procedures):**
- **Pulmonary regurgitation** — mild-to-moderate PR common after BPV (accepted trade-off; usually well-tolerated)
- **Valve insufficiency progression** — occasional patient develops progressive PR requiring later surgical/transcatheter valve replacement
- **Femoral vein/artery injury** — access site complications
- **Perforation of RV or PA** — rare but serious; may require emergency surgery
- **Atrial arrhythmia** — from RA manipulation; usually transient
- **Paradoxical embolism** — if PFO present

**Long-term outcomes:**
- **Recurrence of stenosis:** 10–15% of patients develop re-stenosis requiring repeat BPV or eventual surgery
- **Freedom from reintervention at 10 years:** ~70–80% in pediatric studies
- **RV function:** generally preserved post-BPV if performed before severe RV dysfunction develops

> [!tip] **BPV Success Pearl**
> BPV is most successful when performed on young patients with isolated congenital valvar PS before significant RV hypertrophy has developed. Once the RV is severely hypertrophied with reduced compliance, the hemodynamic benefit of BPV may be blunted (the stiffened RV still requires high filling pressures even without the gradient). Timing matters.

**Surgical Valvotomy/Replacement**

**Indications:**
- **Failed BPV or re-stenosis** after prior BPV
- **Dysplastic pulmonic valve** (especially Noonan syndrome) — poor BPV results; high rate of suboptimal outcome
- **Subvalvar PS** — muscular obstruction not amenable to balloon dilation
- **Supravalvar PS** — anatomic obstruction above valve; requires surgical repair
- **Severe PS with additional pathology** — e.g., TOF with subvalvar obstruction or multiple levels of RVOT obstruction

**Procedure:**
- **Pulmonary valvotomy** (commissurotomy) — division of fused commissures; preserves valve if anatomy permits
- **Valve replacement** — if valve unsuitable for repair; choose between bioprosthesis (10–20 year durability) or mechanical prosthesis (lifetime durability but requires anticoagulation)
- **Transannular patch** — enlargement of RVOT beyond valve annulus; commonly used in complex PS or TOF, results in significant PR

**Long-term considerations:**
- Bioprosthetic valves in the pulmonary position have excellent durability (lower stress environment than left side)
- Mechanical valves require lifelong anticoagulation (INR 2–3)
- Reoperation rates depend on valve type and age at implantation

#### ICU Management of Acute RV Failure from PS

**Key principles:**
- **Preload optimization** — RV is exquisitely dependent on preload; avoid aggressive diuresis
- **Afterload reduction** — avoid sudden drops in pulmonary vascular resistance (vasodilators can cause hypotension); graduated approach
- **Contractility support** — inotropes may be needed if RV is failing
- **Arrhythmia prevention** — atrial fibrillation poorly tolerated (loss of atrial "kick" critical for RV filling)
- **Minimize hypoxia, hypercarbia, acidosis** — all increase pulmonary vascular resistance and worsen RV afterload

**Specific interventions:**
- **Fluid resuscitation** — cautious; target CVP 10–15 mmHg (higher than usual for RV pathology)
- **Avoid tachycardia** — maintain heart rate <100 bpm; beta-blockers or calcium channel blockers if needed
- **Inotropic support** — milrinone (inotropy + pulmonary vasodilation) preferred over catecholamines if inotropes needed; avoid pure alpha-agonism
- **Pulmonary vasodilation** — inhaled nitric oxide (iNO; 20 ppm) or inhaled epoprostenol for acute pulmonary hypertension
- **Mechanical ventilation strategy:**
  - Permissive hypercapnia acceptable; avoid hyperventilation (↑ PVR from hypocapnia)
  - Moderate PEEP (8–10 cm H₂O) to improve oxygenation without excessive intrathoracic pressure
  - Avoid excessive tidal volumes (plateau pressure <30 cm H₂O)
- **Monitoring** — invasive hemodynamics (Swan-Ganz catheter) if RV failure refractory to initial therapy
- **ECMO consideration** — if cardiogenic shock unresponsive to medical therapy and patient is transplant candidate

### Pulmonic Regurgitation

#### Medical Management

**Asymptomatic mild-to-moderate PR with normal RV function:**
- **Observation** — majority of ACHD patients with post-surgical PR remain well-compensated for years
- **Serial imaging** — TTE every 1–2 years; CMR at baseline and every 2–3 years if significant PR (RVEDVI >100 mL/m²)
- **Activity counseling** — no specific restrictions for asymptomatic patients; encourage normal activity to maintain cardiac conditioning
- **Genetic counseling** — for syndromic underlying disease (e.g., tetralogy of Fallot in family screening)
- **No medications proven to slow RV dilation** in isolated PR; avoid ACE inhibitors/beta-blockers unless other indication

**Symptomatic moderate-to-severe PR or progressive RV dysfunction:**
- **Diuretics** — for volume overload and pulmonary edema (more appropriate than in PS)
  - Loop diuretics (furosemide 20–80 mg daily/BID) titrated to symptoms
  - Monitor electrolytes and renal function
- **Beta-blockers** — may slow RV remodeling and reduce arrhythmia burden; widely used but limited evidence
  - Metoprolol or carvedilol preferred (additional alpha-blocking properties may benefit)
  - Target HR <80 bpm at rest
- **ACE inhibitors or ARBs** — rational but unproven benefit; may help if systemic hypertension present
  - Lisinopril 10–40 mg daily or losartan 50–100 mg daily
  - Avoid if systemic hypotension
- **Anticoagulation** — **NOT routinely recommended** unless:
  - Atrial fibrillation present (stroke risk)
  - Known thromboembolism or hypercoagulable state
  - Mechanical valve prosthesis

**Symptomatic management:**
- **Sodium restriction** — <2 g daily to reduce volume overload
- **Fluid restriction** — 1.5–2 L daily if advanced RV failure with hyponatremia
- **Oxygen supplementation** — for hypoxemia (from right-to-left shunt or pulmonary edema)

#### Interventional Management: Transcatheter Pulmonary Valve Replacement (TPVR)

**Landmark shift in ACHD management:** TPVR has transformed the field by offering a percutaneous alternative to surgical valve replacement for suitable candidates. This is a **major advance** in reducing morbidity and preserving quality of life.

**Indications for TPVR:**
- **Chronic moderate-to-severe PR** with evidence of RV dilation and/or dysfunction:
  - RVEDVI >160 mL/m² (by CMR; or RV diameter >55 mm by echo)
  - RVEF <50% or declining
  - Progressive symptoms (dyspnea, exercise intolerance)
  - Sustained arrhythmia (ventricular ectopy, nonsustained VT)
- **Patients with suitable RVOT anatomy** — diameter 16–27 mm depending on device
- **Failed surgical bioprosthesis** in RVOT — excellent application for valve-in-valve approach

**Contraindications/Relative contraindications:**
- **Active infection or endocarditis** — delay until bacteremia cleared
- **Severe systemic hypertension** — relative; optimize BP control first
- **Severe LV dysfunction** — may be unable to tolerate TPVR hemodynamics
- **Anatomy unsuitable** — RVOT <16 mm or >27 mm for most devices; excessive angulation; left main coronary artery at risk

**Available devices (as of 2026):**

1. **Melody Valve** (Edwards Lifesciences)
   - Balloon-expandable
   - Best for conduits and small RVOTs (16–20 mm)
   - Success rate >95%; 10-year freedom from reintervention ~46% (requires repeat interventions in some)
   - Cost-effective; most experience globally
   - Limitations: limited to 20 mm diameter; stent fracture can occur; endocarditis risk

2. **SAPIEN (Edwards Lifesciences)**
   - Balloon-expandable; newer iterations optimized for RVOT
   - Larger landing zones (22–27 mm)
   - Good initial outcomes; expanding experience
   - Hemodynamically superior to Melody in some studies
   - Still being evaluated for long-term durability

3. **Harmony Valve** (Medtronic)
   - Self-expanding frame; designed for larger native RVOTs
   - Can be retrieved/repositioned before full deployment
   - Growing clinical experience; promising early/intermediate outcomes
   - Landing zone 22–35 mm
   - Lower stent fracture rates than Melody

4. **Venus P-Valve** (Venus Medtech)
   - Self-expanding; newest iteration available in China and Europe
   - Largest landing zones (large native/patched outflow tracts)
   - Procedural success >95%; good hemodynamic outcomes; RV reverse remodeling at 6–12 months
   - 5-year follow-up shows durable performance; low reintervention rates
   - Not yet FDA-approved in US; limited US experience

**Pre-TPVR evaluation:**
- **Imaging protocol:**
  - TTE with Doppler: measure RV volumes, function, quantify PR, assess for complications
  - CMR: precise RV volumes and RVEF; assess myocardial fibrosis
  - CT angiography: RVOT anatomy, diameter, length, angulation; coronary artery relationships (critical: rule out left main compression risk)
  - Catheterization: hemodynamics, RVOT angiography, coronary angiography to exclude coronary at risk
- **Risk stratification:**
  - Left main coronary artery in proximity to RVOT: high risk for compression with valve implant; may require surgical approach or different valve type
  - Severe RV systolic dysfunction (RVEF <30%): associated with higher periprocedural mortality; optimize medical therapy first
  - Pulmonary hypertension: increases RV afterload; optimize PVR-lowering therapy pre-procedure

**Procedure:**
- Percutaneous femoral venous access (or jugular if IVC thrombosis/stenosis)
- Guide wire across pulmonic valve; catheter manipulated into RVOT position
- Valve crimped on balloon (or self-expanding device deployed); inflated under fluoroscopy and TEE guidance
- Goal: competent valve with <10 mmHg residual gradient
- Post-deployment angiography to confirm positioning and rule out coronary compression

**Immediate outcomes (from PubMed recent evidence, 2025-2026):**
- **Procedural success >95%** — valve successfully deployed
- **Hemodynamic improvement:**
  - Reduction in PR (from severe to none/trivial)
  - Restoration of pulmonary valve competence
  - Gradient <10 mmHg expected
  - Reduced RV pressure (if secondary pulmonary hypertension present)
- **RV reverse remodeling:** begins at 6–12 months post-implantation
  - RVEDVI decreases by 10–20% at 6–12 months
  - RVEF improves in majority (by 5–10 percentage points on average)
  - Subjective functional improvement (exercise tolerance) noted by patients

**Long-term outcomes and durability:**

According to PubMed evidence:

- [McCarthy EK, et al. Long-Term Outcomes After Melody Transcatheter Pulmonary Valve Implant in Patients Weighing ≤30 kg. *JACC Cardiovasc Interv*. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcin.2025.10.049):
  - 199 patients, median follow-up 8 years
  - Freedom from reintervention at 10 years: 46% (nearly half required reintervention)
  - Most common reintervention: balloon dilation (20 patients), valve-in-valve (30), explantation (28)
  - Endocarditis occurred in 32 patients (16%); 9 managed medically
  - Freedom from Melody explantation at 10 years: 78%
  - **Clinical implication:** TPVR is a bridge procedure; expect need for reintervention

- [AlNajashi K, et al. Transcatheter Pulmonary Valve Replacement (TPVR): A Saudi Consensus Statement. *J Saudi Heart Assoc*. 2025](https://doi.org/10.37616/2212-5043.1448):
  - Multidisciplinary consensus on TPVR best practices
  - TPVR offers favorable outcomes vs. surgical approach: RV remodeling, reduced hospital stay
  - Complications of concern: tricuspid valve injury, stent fracture, endocarditis
  - Standardized imaging-guided approach with multidisciplinary oversight recommended

- [Ayyash BM, Hijazi ZM. Evaluation of the Venus P-valve for transcatheter pulmonary valve replacement. *Future Cardiol*. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/14796678.2026.2628689):
  - 600+ implantations worldwide with Venus P-Valve
  - Procedural success >95%; immediate hemodynamic competence
  - RV reverse remodeling within 6–12 months
  - Durable performance to 5 years; low reintervention rates
  - Shorter hospitalization and lower morbidity vs. surgical replacement in appropriately selected patients

**Complications (5–25% depending on series):**
- **Valve insufficiency (early)** — malposition or incomplete coaptation; may require valve-in-valve
- **Stent fracture** — Melody: 5–10% at 5 years (less common with Harmony/Venus)
- **Endocarditis** — **significantly higher with TPVR (5.8%) vs. surgical PVR (2.7%); OR 3.1** (Chongmelaxme et al. *PLoS One* 2025; Ribeiro et al. *Ann Thorac Surg* 2020). This is the major long-term concern driving need for indefinite prophylaxis and annual surveillance. TPVR endocarditis is associated with ~75% 1-year mortality.
- **Tricuspid regurgitation worsening** — from right ventricular remodeling (usually mild, improves with RV reverse remodeling)
- **Left main coronary artery compression** — rare but catastrophic; prevented by careful pre-procedural imaging and anatomy assessment
- **Pulmonary artery perforation** — rare; managed conservatively (pericardial effusion) or surgically
- **Arrhythmia** — from manipulation; usually transient
- **Vascular injury** — at access site; femoral vein/artery stenosis
- **Valve thrombosis** — rare; preventable with appropriate anticoagulation perioperatively

**Antibiotic prophylaxis post-TPVR:**
- **Recommended** for dental, GI, GU procedures for **at least 6 months** post-implant (per ACC/AHA/2025 ACHD guideline); many experts recommend **indefinite prophylaxis** given demonstrated 3-fold higher endocarditis risk with TPVR vs. surgical PVR (Chongmelaxme et al. 2025; Ribeiro et al. 2020)
- Annual surveillance echo recommended given endocarditis risk (TPVR endocarditis associated with ~75% 1-year mortality)

#### Surgical Pulmonary Valve Replacement

**Indications:**
- **Failed or contraindicated TPVR** — anatomy unsuitable for transcatheter approach (extreme RVOT size, angulation, or specific anatomic barriers)
- **Need for concurrent procedures** — tricuspid valve surgery, LV repair, other cardiac surgery
- **Severe systemic ventricular dysfunction** — may preclude percutaneous approach
- **Pregnant woman requiring urgent valve replacement** — TPVR generally avoided in pregnancy (radiation, contrast, hemodynamic changes)

**Procedure:**
- Median sternotomy or right thoracotomy
- Cardiopulmonary bypass
- Excision of native or prosthetic valve
- Implantation of new valve (bioprosthetic or mechanical)

**Valve options:**
- **Bioprosthetic:** 10–20 year durability; excellent performance in low-pressure RVOT; no anticoagulation
- **Mechanical:** lifetime durability; requires INR 2–3 anticoagulation
- **Cryopreserved homograft:** growing experience; good outcomes; intermediate durability

**Outcomes:**
- Operative mortality 1–3% in elective cases
- Excellent long-term RV reverse remodeling post-surgery
- Reoperation rates: bioprosthetic 20–30% at 15–20 years; mechanical valve rarely requires replacement

**TPVR vs. Surgical PVR: Comparative Durability (2024–2025 Evidence)**

| Outcome | TPVR | Surgical PVR | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Perioperative mortality** | ~0.5–2% | ~0.5–2% | Similar |
| **Hospital stay** | Shorter | Longer | TPVR advantage |
| **Mid-term reintervention** | Similar | Similar | No significant difference at mid-term |
| **Endocarditis** | 5.8% | 2.7% (OR 3.1) | **Major TPVR liability** |
| **Long-term survival** | Similar | Similar | No mortality difference |
| **RV reverse remodeling** | Favorable in some studies | Standard | Egbe et al. *JACC Cardiovasc Interv* 2024 |
| **Anatomic eligibility** | ~60–70% of candidates | Broader | RVOT size/morphology limits TPVR |

*Clinical implication: TPVR and surgical PVR should be viewed as **complementary strategies throughout a patient's lifetime**, not competing options. Multidisciplinary decision-making should incorporate anatomy, prior surgeries, comorbidities, endocarditis risk tolerance, and institutional expertise (Geva T et al., AHA Scientific Statement 2024).*

#### Timing of Intervention in PR

**Updated 2024–2025 Recommendations: Multiparametric Approach**

Current consensus (2025 ACC/AHA ACHD Guideline; 2024 AHA Scientific Statement on TOF — Geva T et al., *Circulation* 2024) emphasizes a **multiparametric decision framework** rather than reliance on a single volume or function threshold. The 160 mL/m² threshold predicts normalization of RV volumes post-PVR but has **not been shown to improve survival** in isolation.

**Class I (Strongly Recommended):**
- Symptomatic moderate-to-severe PR with RV dilation or dysfunction
  - TPVR preferred over surgery if anatomy suitable
  - Surgical replacement if TPVR not feasible

**Class IIa (Reasonable):**
- Asymptomatic moderate-to-severe PR with **any** of the following:
  - Severe RV dilation: RVEDVI ≥160 mL/m² **or** RVESVI ≥80 mL/m² **or** RVEDV ≥2× LVEDV
  - Progressive RV or LV systolic dysfunction
  - Objective decline in exercise capacity on serial testing
  - Sustained atrial or ventricular arrhythmia

**Class IIb (May Be Reasonable):**
- Asymptomatic with ≥2 of the following criteria:
  - Mild-moderate RV or LV dysfunction
  - Severe RV dilation (as above)
  - RV systolic pressure ≥2/3 systemic (if RVOT obstruction also present)
  - Progressive reduction in exercise tolerance on serial testing

> [!tip] **Multiparametric Timing Caveat**
> The RVEDVI >160 mL/m² threshold alone predicts RV volume normalization post-PVR but has not demonstrated a survival benefit in isolation. No single parameter should drive the decision — integrate symptoms, RV volumes, biventricular function, arrhythmia burden, and exercise capacity. The **INDICATOR risk calculator** (incorporating age, BMI, repair type, RVESVI, and biventricular global function index) may help individualize timing at experienced centers (Geva T et al., AHA Scientific Statement 2024).

> [!warning] **Intervention Timing Pearl**
> The goal is to intervene **before irreversible RV systolic dysfunction develops**. Once RVEF drops below 35–40%, recovery of function post-valve replacement is unpredictable and often incomplete. This is the "window of opportunity" — catch it too late and you've saved the patient from hemodynamic decompensation but not from chronic RV failure.

### Carcinoid Heart Disease

**Medical management:**
- **Somatostatin analogs** (octreotide or lanreotide) — reduce serotonin production and may slow valve fibrosis progression
  - Depot formulations: lanreotide 30 mg IM monthly or octreotide LAR 20–30 mg IM monthly
  - Efficacy limited; does not reverse established valve disease
- **Beta-blockers and diuretics** — symptomatic management for dyspnea and arrhythmias
- **Monitor for carcinoid-related complications** — liver metastases, mesenteric ischemia, other organ involvement

**Interventional/surgical management:**
- **Timing:** intervention similar to acquired PR, but carcinoid valve disease often progresses more rapidly
- **Valve replacement** (surgical preferred) — TPVR not typically first-line for carcinoid (anatomy often complex; valve thickened/calcified)
- **Tricuspid valve replacement often required** — bilateral right-side involvement common
- **Prognosis dependent on extent of metastatic disease** — overall survival in advanced carcinoid limited by tumor burden, not cardiac lesion per se

## 7. Clinical Pearls

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls — Pulmonic Valve Disease
> 1. **Gradient ≠ Symptom Status:** A severe PS gradient in an asymptomatic patient may not require immediate intervention, while a mildly symptomatic patient with moderate PS often benefits from BPV. Symptom status and functional limitation drive timing more than gradient alone.
> 2. **The "Silent Killer" of ACHD:** Severe PR is often missed — patients compensate for years. Always ask ACHD patients about exercise capacity, maintain regular echo surveillance even if asymptomatic, and don't miss the window to intervene before RV dysfunction becomes irreversible.
> 3. **AF in PS is an emergency:** New-onset AF in severe PS can cause rapid deterioration — loss of atrial kick deprives the hypertrophied RV of its critical preload boost. Restore sinus rhythm urgently; consider synchronized cardioversion if hemodynamically unstable.
> 4. **Preload is sacred in RV disease:** Whether PS or PR, the RV is preload-dependent. Avoid aggressive diuresis, maintain adequate CVP, and be cautious with vasodilators. Apparent "pulmonary edema" may actually be RV failure with elevated RA pressure — not left heart failure.
> 5. **TPVR is a bridge, not a cure:** Durability is limited — ~54% of Melody valve recipients need reintervention by 10 years. However, freedom from explantation at 10 years is ~78%, supporting TPVR as a meaningful bridge strategy that delays, rather than avoids, surgical replacement. Counsel patients accordingly.
> 6. **Syndromic PS is different:** Noonan syndrome and other syndromic forms often have dysplastic valves refractory to BPV. Success rates are lower; surgical valvotomy or early valve replacement may be needed. Know the underlying syndrome.
> 7. **Check the septal wall:** Rightward septal bowing during systole (PS) or leftward bowing during diastole (PR) signals significant chamber burden on echo and may influence intervention timing.
> 8. **Endocarditis risk post-TPVR:** TPVR carries a 3-fold higher endocarditis risk than surgical PVR (5.8% vs. 2.7%). Prophylaxis for dental/GI procedures is recommended for at least 6 months post-implant; many experts advocate indefinite prophylaxis given this risk. Annual echo surveillance is essential — TPVR endocarditis has ~75% 1-year mortality.

## 8. Evidence & Landmark Trials

### Landmark Trials and Studies

**Pulmonic Stenosis:**

- **National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) Registry Data on Balloon Valvuloplasty (1980s–1990s)**
  - Population: Over 700 pediatric and adult patients with PS undergoing BPV
  - Intervention: Balloon dilation of stenotic pulmonic valve
  - Outcome: Gradient reduction by 50–70%; <2% major complication rate; excellent long-term freedom from reintervention
  - Impact: Established BPV as first-line therapy for suitable congenital PS; transformed management away from obligatory surgical valvotomy

- **Valvular Heart Disease in Adults (AHA/ACC Guidelines, 1998–2014 editions)**
  - Synthesized evidence on natural history of untreated PS, indications for intervention, and comparative outcomes of BPV vs. surgery
  - Reinforced BPV for most patients; surgery reserved for dysplastic valves, subvalvar obstruction, or failed BPV
  - Informed current practice guidelines

**Pulmonic Regurgitation and TPVR:**

- **MELODY Trial (Khonsari et al., 2009)**
  - Population: 155 patients with dysfunctional RV-to-PA conduits (prior surgical TOF repair or other RVOT procedures)
  - Intervention: Melody transcatheter pulmonary valve implantation
  - Outcome: 96% procedural success; 87% remained free from conduit reintervention at 1 year; marked hemodynamic improvement (PR eliminated, gradients <10 mmHg)
  - Impact: First major multicenter evidence supporting TPVR; fundamentally changed the field by enabling percutaneous valve therapy in failing conduits and established Melody as gold standard for 15+ years

- **COMPASSION Trial (Lurz et al., 2014)**
  - Population: 60 patients with RVOT dysfunction (PR or PS) refractory to medical therapy, suitable for TPVR
  - Intervention: SAPIEN transcatheter pulmonary valve (Edwards; balloon-expandable device optimized for larger RVOTs)
  - Outcome: 100% procedural success; significant PR reduction and pulmonary valve competence restoration; RV reverse remodeling at 3–6 months
  - Impact: Demonstrated TPVR feasibility with second-generation device; expanded candidacy to larger RVOTs (beyond Melody's 20 mm limit)

- **Venus P-Valve Registries (2020–present)**
  - Population: 600+ patients with large native or patched RVOTs unsuitable for prior transcatheter devices
  - Intervention: Self-expanding Venus P-Valve (landing zone 22–35 mm)
  - Outcome: Procedural success >95%; excellent hemodynamic results; RV reverse remodeling; durable at 5 years; low reintervention rates
  - Impact: Expanding TPVR candidacy to the largest RVOTs; newest technology with promising durability profile

### Current Evidence (PubMed Search, 2022–2026)

**Based on articles retrieved from PubMed:**

1. **McCarthy EK, et al. Long-Term Outcomes After Melody Transcatheter Pulmonary Valve Implant in Patients Weighing ≤30 kg. [*JACC Cardiovasc Interv*. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcin.2025.10.049)**
   - **Key finding:** Among 199 small patients with Melody implants (mean 8-year follow-up), freedom from reintervention at 10 years was 46%; however, 78% remained free from valve explantation and 79% from endocarditis, supporting utility of TPVR as bridge to delay surgical replacement in pediatric ACHD.

2. **AlNajashi K, et al. Transcatheter Pulmonary Valve Replacement (TPVR): A Saudi Consensus Statement. [*J Saudi Heart Assoc*. 2025](https://doi.org/10.37616/2212-5043.1448)**
   - **Key finding:** Multidisciplinary consensus endorsed TPVR as safe and effective for selected patients with PR or PS from congenital disease; emphasized role of imaging-guided approach and multidisciplinary assessment; identified stent fracture and tricuspid valve injury as ongoing concerns warranting further device refinement.

3. **Ayyash BM, Hijazi ZM. Evaluation of the Venus P-valve for Transcatheter Pulmonary Valve Replacement in Patients with Right Ventricular Outflow Tract Dysfunction. [*Future Cardiol*. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/14796678.2026.2628689)**
   - **Key finding:** Narrative review of Venus P-Valve in 600+ patients showed procedural success >95%, immediate pulmonary valve competence, RV reverse remodeling within 6–12 months, durable performance to 5 years, and hemodynamic outcomes comparable to surgical replacement with reduced hospitalization and morbidity—supporting role as frontier device for large native/patched RVOTs.

4. **O'Donnell-Smith MB, et al. Harmony Transcatheter Pulmonary Valve Housing Compression: Bench Testing and Case Report. [*Catheter Cardiovasc Interv*. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/ccd.70382)**
   - **Key finding:** Bench testing and clinical case demonstrate Harmony valve tolerates compression in smaller landing zones without hemodynamic deterioration, expanding candidacy for patients with restrictive RVOT anatomy (e.g., after high-pressure balloon valvuloplasty with intended fracture).

## 9. Key Guidelines

### AHA/ACC Guidelines on Valvular Heart Disease (2020 Edition — VHD-Specific)

**Pulmonic Stenosis:**
- **Mild PS (peak gradient <36 mmHg):** Observation; periodic echo surveillance; no activity restriction
- **Moderate PS (gradient 36–64 mmHg, RV <80 mmHg systolic):** Observation unless symptoms develop; echocardiography every 1–3 years
- **Severe PS (gradient >64 mmHg, RV >80 mmHg systolic):** Intervention (BPV preferred; surgical valvotomy if dysplastic or subvalvar component)
  - Class IIa: Asymptomatic severe PS may be considered for intervention due to RV hypertrophy burden
- **Antibiotic prophylaxis:** NOT recommended for native valve PS (isolated lesion)

**Pulmonic Regurgitation:**
- **Asymptomatic, normal RV function:** Observation with regular echo follow-up
- **Asymptomatic with progressive RV dilation (RVEDVI ≥160 mL/m², RVESVI ≥80 mL/m²) or declining RVEF:** Consider intervention — 2025 ACHD guideline classifies as Class IIa if any one major criterion met; Class IIb if ≥2 minor criteria
- **Symptomatic or RVEF <45–50%:** Intervention strongly recommended (Class I, 2025 guideline)
  - TPVR preferred over surgery in suitable anatomy
  - Surgical valve replacement if TPVR not feasible

### ACHD Guidelines (2025 ACC/AHA/HRS/ISACHD/SCAI and 2018 AHA/ACC)

> [!warning] **Guideline Update (December 2025)**
> The **2025 ACC/AHA/HRS/ISACHD/SCAI Guideline for the Management of Adults With Congenital Heart Disease** (Gurvitz M, Krieger EV, et al. *JACC*. 2025. doi:10.1016/j.jacc.2025.09.006) supersedes the 2018 AHA/ACC ACHD guideline for adult CHD management. Intervention thresholds and classification criteria referenced throughout this document reflect the updated 2025 recommendations where applicable.

- **Serial imaging strategy for ACHD with prior TOF repair:**
  - Annual or biennial TTE initially
  - CMR at diagnosis/baseline and every 2–3 years if significant PR
  - Intervention timing based on RV volumes (RVEDVI), function (RVEF), and symptom status
  - Exercise testing valuable for objective assessment of functional capacity

### ESC Guidelines on Congenital Heart Disease (2010)

- **Emphasis on multidisciplinary team care** — involvement of cardiologist, cardiac surgeon, and imaging specialists in intervention planning
- **Importance of standardized imaging protocols** — reproducible measures of RV size and function for serial comparison
- **Long-term antibiotic prophylaxis** post-TPVR for dental/GI procedures
