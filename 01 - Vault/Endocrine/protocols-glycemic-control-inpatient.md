# Glycemic Control in the ICU/Inpatient Setting

## 1. Introduction
-  **Background:** Glycemic control in critically ill patients is a crucial aspect of intensive care management. Hyperglycemia is common in the ICU due to stress responses and can lead to adverse outcomes if not managed properly. The balance between controlling hyperglycemia and avoiding hypoglycemia is delicate, as both extremes can lead to increased morbidity and mortality.
## 2. Pathophysiology
-  **Stress Response:** Stress hyperglycemia occurs as part of the body's response to critical illness, driven by elevated levels of stress hormones such as cortisol, glucagon, and epinephrine. These hormones increase glucose production and cause insulin resistance, further exacerbating hyperglycemia.
-  **Iatrogenic Factors:** In critically ill patients, ongoing nutritional support, especially parenteral nutrition, can contribute to hyperglycemia. Without invasive nutritional support, many critically ill patients would become cachectic, highlighting the complex interplay between nutrition and glucose metabolism in the ICU.
## 3. Importance
-  **Impact on Outcomes:** Hyperglycemia in the ICU is associated with increased mortality, particularly in non-diabetic patients. The NICE-SUGAR trial demonstrated that intensive glucose control (targeting 81-100 mg/dL) increased mortality compared to conventional control (targeting <180 mg/dL), highlighting the risks of overly aggressive glycemic management.
-  **Risks of Poor Control:** Both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia are linked to adverse outcomes. Hyperglycemia can impair immune function and endothelial integrity, while hypoglycemia can cause neurological damage and is associated with increased mortality, especially in diabetic patients.
## 4. Guidelines and Targets
-  **Current Guidelines:** Major guidelines, including those from the ADA and SCCM, recommend a target glucose range of 140-180 mg/dL for most critically ill patients. This range balances the risks of hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia, avoiding the pitfalls of tight glycemic control.
-  **Target Ranges:** For patients with chronic hyperglycemia, such as those with diabetes, slightly higher glucose targets may be appropriate to prevent hypoglycemia and account for baseline glucose variability.
## 5. Methods
### Initial Evaluation
-  **Presentation:** Patient with known diabetes or blood glucose > 180 mg/dL.
-  **Immediate Actions:**
  - Check hemoglobin A1c if not done in the last 3 months.
  - Begin point-of-care (POC) glucose monitoring.
  - Assess for hyperglycemic emergencies (e.g., DKA, HHS).
### Hyperglycemic Emergency Management
-  **Criteria for Hyperglycemic Emergency:**
  - DKA: Blood glucose > 250 mg/dL, pH < 7.3, bicarbonate < 15 mEq/L.
  - HHS: Blood glucose > 600 mg/dL, pH ≥ 7.3, bicarbonate ≥ 15 mEq/L.
-  **Action:** Consult Endocrinology-Diabetes for urgent management.
### Choosing an insulin strategy
Generally three overall strategies: insulin infusion, basal-bolus, and correction insulin-only. 
- When to choose insulin infusion
	- Initiate insulin infusion for 2 consecutive glucose levels > 200 mg/dL within 24 hours or one glucose > 300 mg/dL
	- Indicators
		- Severely elevated glucose
		- Hyperglycemia refractory to SQ insulin
		- Patients with impaired perfusion who may have erratic SQ insulin
- When to choose basal-bolus insulin
	- Indications
		- Type 1 DM
		- Transitioning off insulin gtt
		- Persistent Hyperglycemia despite SQ insulin
- When to choose SSI
	- ⚠️ Sliding scale alone should _never_ be utilized in type-I diabetes (since these patients require basal insulin to prevent DKA).
	- The use of a sliding scale alone is generally frowned upon in patients with insulin-dependent type-2 diabetes since it is a purely _reactionary_ strategy.
	- SSI alone is only recommended for those who weren't treated with insulin before admission

### SubQ Regimen
The SQ basal-bolus regimen has 3 components
- **(1/3) Basal Insulin**
	- Basal insulin exists regardless of caloric intake
	- Basal insulin usually in the form of Glargine Q24hr
	- Usual dosing
		- A typical starting dose is ~0.1-0.3 U/kg/day.
		    - 50 kg: ~5-15 U
		    - 70 kg: ~7-21 U
		    - 90 kg: ~9-27 U
	- For DKA patients who are naive to insulin, 0.25 U/kg/day may be used
	- Titrate to target fasting AM glucose of ~100-130. Adjustments should only be made every 2-3 days based on pharmacokinetics.
		- Fasting glucose <100 mg/dL: reduce basal dose.
		- Fasting glucose 100-130 mg/dL: no change.
		- Fasting glucose 130-159 mg/dL: increase by 2 units.
		- Fasting glucose 160-189: increase by 4 units.
		- Fasting glucose 190-220: increase by 6 units.
		- Fasting glucose >220 mg/dL: increase by 8 units.
- **(2/3) Nutritional Insulin (aka prandial insulin)**
	- Nutrition insulin is required to counter carb intake. Important to separate prandial from basal due to patients intake possibly stopping for procedure, etc.
	- Discrete Meals:
		- Rapid-acting insulin scheduled with meals
		- Starting roughly ~0.05-0.1 U/kg/meal
		- Can used carb counting for patients with DM type I but data is conflicting. If you need to because the patient was doing that prior, then using a ratio of 1 Unit to 15 grams of carbohydrates is typical. 
		- Basal-Plus multicenter RCT found omitting meal-associated insulin for DM type II (control with oral diet, oral agents, or low dose insulin (≦ 0.4 U/kg/day)) resulted in equivalent glycemic control and reduced hypoglycemic incidents.
	- Bolus tube feeding
		- Essentially the same as those who are receiving meals. 
	- Continuous Tube Feeding
		- Regular insulin scheduled Q6hr makes sense pharmacokinetically
		- If tube feedings held, consider D50W infusion or something dextrose containing solution. 
		- This may be held and closely monitored for those with HF and/or those who benefit from less volume with close monitoring
	- TPN
		- Insulin can be added
- **(3/3) Correctional Insulin PRN (aka sliding scale)**
	- General principles of correctional insulin:
		- Correctional insulin is the same _type_ of insulin as is utilized for nutritional insulin.
		- Correctional insulin is given _at the same time_ as the nutritional insulin. The correction dose is added on top of the scheduled nutritional dose, so only a single combined dose is administered.
	- This principle will allow flexible yet reasonable insulin regimen so that they are essentially only getting the basal insulin (glargine) and the nutritional/correctional (rapid acting insulin)

**Initial Construction of basal-bolus SubQ regimen**
- Determine total daily dose (TDD) of insulin. 3 ways to do this
	- (1) Transition from IV to subQ
		- Determine daily IV insulin. Can take IV insulin requirement over the last six hours x 4
		- Multiply daily IV insulin requirement by 0.6-0.8 to get an estimated (TDD) for subQ insulin.
		- If receiving nutrition, then split the TDD 50/50 between basal and nutritional. 
		- If NPO, give 100% as basal
		- Basal insulin given 2 hours prior to insulin infusion has stopped
	- (2) Based on prior insulin prescription before admission
		- Obtain an accurate history of total insulin requirements patient was taking prior (basal, meal, correctional total)
		- If unable then move to section 3
		- Be careful of basal-heavy regimens (>0.6 U/kg/day basal insulin). If the basl insulin is inappropriately functioning to provide some meal coverage as well, then inappropriate basal dosing for a patient with reduced nutritional intake could result in hypoglycemic episodes
	- (3) Fresh start using weight-based estimation of total daily dose of insulin
		- Hypoglycemia risk factors: 0.2-0.3 U/kg/d (e.g., age >70 YO, renal dysfunction, poor PO intake).
	- No hypoglycemic risk factors: 0.4-0.6 U/kg/d (doses >0.6 U/kg/day may increase the risk of hypoglycemia)
- Divide the total daily dose (TDD) into basal and nutritional
	- Usually split 50/50
		- Half basal
		- Half nutritional

**Adjust insulin to Accommodate Steroid Therapy**
- Use of NPH to manage daily AM prednisone therapy
	- General concept
		- Glycemic impact of prednisone taken in the morning typically worsens hyperglycemia during afternoon, peaking in the evening
		- NPH admin concomitantly with prednisone is a common approach
		- Most patients treated with prednisone will be fine and will not require insulin.
	- Estimated initial NPH dose:
		- (Step #1) Start with 0.4 Units NPH/mg prednisone, up to a maximal dose of 20 Units NPH.
		- (Step #2) Divide that dose in _half_ if the patient is not diabetic.
		- (Step #3) Divide that dose in _half_ if the patient is NPO.
- Insulin Adjusts with other steroids
	- Other steroids (e.g., dexamethasone or hydrocortisone) will cause a more persistent therapeutic effect which isn't easily matched by a single insulin dose.
	- Steroids tend to increase post-prandial glucose levels, requiring higher doses of meal-associated insulin (whereas basal insulin may not need to be adjusted). Sometimes 65-70% of the total daily dose of insulin may need to be given as nutritional insulin, with only 30-35% as basal insulin.

### MMH Order Protocols
Below are protocols used at Memorial Herman Hospital ICU
#### ICU Insulin SubQ Orders
**Parental/Enteral/NPO**
Basal Insulin 
- Daily basal insulin recommended for patients with HA1c > 8% or average blood glucose > 200mg/dL
- Start with 0.2 units/kg/day (max 30-40 units)
- If glucose is persistently above target, consider increasing basal dose by 20%.
- Dosed with Glargine (Lantus) every evening or NPH (Isophane) every 8 hours
Correction Insulin
- Dosed with Lispro (HumaLOG) or Regular.
- Typically chose between low, medium, high protocols
- Every 4 hours

**Oral Nutrition ICU**
Basal Insulin
- Daily basal insulin recommended for patients with HA1c > 8% or average blood glucose > 200mg/dL
- Start with 0.2 units/kg/day (max 30-40 units)
- If glucose is persistently above target, consider increasing basal dose by 20%.
- Dosed with Glargine (Lantus) every evening or NPH (Isophane) every 8 hours
Premeal Insulin
- Only used for patients with oral diets
- Starting dose of 0.05 u/kg; begin after 1st basal insulin. Max 10 units.
- Dosed with Lispro (HumaLOG) or Regulin (HumuLIN R, NovoLIN R)
- Given 3 times daily before breakfast, lunch, and dinner only
Correction Insulin
- Dosed with Lispro (HumaLOG) or Regular.
- Typically chose between low, medium, high protocols
- 3 times daily PRN, with meals

## 6. Challenges
-  **Variability in Response:** Individual patient factors, such as insulin sensitivity and nutritional intake, can significantly affect glycemic control. Adjustments to insulin regimens must consider these variables to avoid hypoglycemia.
-  **Iatrogenic Hypoglycemia:** Insulin is a high-risk medication, and hypoglycemia is a common adverse effect. The NICE-SUGAR trial highlighted the increased risk of hypoglycemia with intensive insulin therapy, underscoring the need for careful titration and monitoring.
## 7. Special Considerations
-  **Patient Populations:** Patients with diabetes, renal failure, or those receiving steroids may require tailored glycemic targets and insulin regimens. Steroid therapy, in particular, can increase insulin requirements due to its hyperglycemic effects.
-  **Nutritional Support:** Enteral and parenteral nutrition can impact glucose levels and insulin needs. Continuous tube feeding requires careful insulin management to prevent fluctuations in glucose levels.
## 8. Recent Advances and Research
-  **Landmark Trials:** The pendulum of opinion on glycemic control has shifted over the years, influenced by key trials such as Leuven, VISEP and [[NICE-SUGAR Trial]]. These studies have shaped current practices by highlighting the risks of tight glycemic control.
-  **Emerging Therapies:** New approaches, such as the use of SGLT2 inhibitors, are being explored for their potential benefits in managing hyperglycemia and its complications in critically ill patients.
## 9. Conclusion
-  **Summary:** Effective glycemic control in the ICU requires a balance between preventing hyperglycemia and avoiding hypoglycemia. Current guidelines favor a moderate approach, targeting glucose levels of 140-180 mg/dL for most patients.
-  **Future Directions:** Further research is needed to refine glycemic targets and develop personalized strategies that account for individual patient characteristics and comorbidities.
