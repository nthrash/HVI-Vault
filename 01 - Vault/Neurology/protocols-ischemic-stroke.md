# Ischemic Stroke

## Overview

Ischemic stroke accounts for approximately 87% of all strokes and results from occlusion of a cerebral artery by thrombus or embolus, leading to downstream tissue ischemia and infarction. It remains the fifth leading cause of death in the United States and a leading cause of long-term disability worldwide. The clinical imperative in ischemic stroke is **time** — the concept of "time is brain" reflects the estimated loss of 1.9 million neurons per minute of untreated large vessel occlusion. Early recognition, rapid triage, and timely reperfusion therapy (intravenous thrombolysis and/or endovascular thrombectomy) are the cornerstones of acute management and the primary determinants of functional outcome.

However, the intensivist's role in stroke care is primarily **supportive management** — the stroke neurology team drives decisions about thrombolysis and endovascular therapy, while the ICU team simultaneously focuses on airway assessment, blood pressure control, hemodynamic optimization, electrolyte and glucose management, and DVT prophylaxis. Dividing tasks and maintaining close communication enables rapid, comprehensive care.

> [!tip] Bottom Line
> IV thrombolysis (alteplase or tenecteplase) within 4.5 hours and endovascular thrombectomy for LVO within up to 24 hours in select patients are the two pillars of acute reperfusion therapy. But the ICU team's contribution — meticulous supportive care — is what protects the penumbra and prevents complications. Every detail matters.

## Etiology & Risk Factors

### Etiologic Classification (TOAST Criteria)

Ischemic strokes are classified by mechanism, which directly informs secondary prevention:

| Category | Mechanism | Approximate Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| **Large artery atherosclerosis** | Thrombosis or artery-to-artery embolism from carotid, vertebral, or intracranial stenosis | ~20% |
| **Cardioembolism** | Thrombus from atrial fibrillation, valvular disease, LV thrombus, PFO with DVT | ~25% |
| **Small vessel occlusion (lacunar)** | Lipohyalinosis of penetrating arterioles | ~20% |
| **Other determined etiology** | Dissection, vasculitis, hypercoagulable states, sickle cell disease | ~5% |
| **Cryptogenic / ESUS** | No cause identified after standard workup (embolic stroke of undetermined source) | ~25–30% |

### Non-Modifiable Risk Factors

- Age (risk doubles each decade after 55)
- Sex (men at higher risk, but women have higher lifetime risk due to longevity)
- Race/ethnicity (Black and Hispanic populations have higher incidence)
- Family history / genetics
- Prior stroke or TIA

### Modifiable Risk Factors

- **Hypertension** — single most important modifiable risk factor
- Atrial fibrillation
- Diabetes mellitus
- Dyslipidemia
- Smoking
- Obesity and physical inactivity
- Obstructive sleep apnea
- Excessive alcohol use
- Carotid stenosis
- Hypercoagulable states

### Etiologic Workup Considerations by Age

**Elderly patients** — common causes include local cerebral atherosclerosis, carotid artery atherosclerosis with embolization, and cardioembolic stroke (AF, prosthetic valve, LV thrombus). Workup: carotid ultrasonography, echocardiography, telemetry for AF.

**Younger patients** — additional causes to consider include septic embolism from endocarditis, paradoxical embolization (DVT through PFO), cervical artery dissection, substance use (cocaine/sympathomimetics), inflammatory vasculitis (lupus, primary CNS angiitis), infectious vasculitis (meningovascular neurosyphilis, VZV, Lyme, tuberculosis), acquired thrombophilia (HITT, antiphospholipid syndrome), and RCVS. Additional workup: echo with bubble study, blood cultures, MRA/CTA for dissection, VDRL/RPR, inflammatory markers, coagulation studies as guided by clinical context.

## Pathophysiology

The pathophysiology of ischemic stroke centers on the concept of the **ischemic penumbra** — a critically hypoperfused region surrounding the irreversibly infarcted core.

### Core Infarct vs. Ischemic Penumbra

**Core infarct** refers to tissue that has already been irrevocably damaged — even if the vessel could be immediately opened, the core would not recover. Radiologically, the core is defined by **cytotoxic edema** reflecting neuronal cell swelling, which causes diffusion restriction on MRI (the reference standard). CT techniques can also identify the core. For anterior hemispheric infarctions, a core volume over ~50–70 mL suggests poor responsiveness to endovascular therapy.

**Ischemic penumbra** is the tissue surrounding the core that is malperfused and nonfunctional, yet remains potentially viable if blood supply can be restored. The penumbra is often maintained by a trickle of blood flow via **collateral circulation**. The entire purpose of revascularization is to resurrect the penumbra.

### CBF Thresholds

| Zone | CBF (mL/100g/min) | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | 50–80 | Normal function |
| Oligemia | 20–50 | Reduced flow, normal function |
| **Ischemic penumbra** | **8–22** | **Functionally impaired, structurally viable — salvageable** |
| **Core infarct** | **< 8–10** | **Irreversible necrosis** |

> [!warning] Key Concept — The penumbra threshold does NOT significantly vary with time after stroke onset, but the infarction (core) threshold IS time-dependent. This is why tissue-based imaging selection is increasingly replacing rigid time cutoffs.

### Rapid Progressors vs. Slow Progressors (IBCC)

The natural history of untreated stroke is for the penumbra to gradually necrose — the penumbra disappears while the core expands. The velocity at which this occurs varies widely:

- **Rapid progressors** — poor collateral flow → complete infarction within hours
- **Slow progressors** — robust collaterals → large penumbras persist for many hours or even days → remain candidates for revascularization well beyond traditional time windows (>> 4.5 hours)

This is the physiologic basis for moving from **time thresholds to tissue thresholds**. A rigid 4.5-hour cutoff made sense in the 1990s, but modern neuroimaging can rapidly determine the amount of salvageable tissue, making tissue viability the primary driver of treatment decisions.

### Sequence of Cellular Injury

1. **Arterial occlusion** → thrombus obstructs blood flow to a vascular territory
2. **Energy failure** → ATP depletion within minutes → failure of Na+/K+-ATPase → cytotoxic edema
3. **Excitotoxicity** → glutamate release → intracellular calcium influx → activation of destructive enzymatic cascades (phospholipases, proteases, endonucleases)
4. **Inflammation and oxidative stress** → reperfusion paradoxically triggers additional injury through reactive oxygen species and inflammatory cell infiltration
5. **Blood-brain barrier breakdown** → contributes to vasogenic edema (typically ~3–6 hours) and risk of hemorrhagic transformation

## Clinical Presentation

### Detailed Stroke Syndromes by Vascular Territory

#### Anterior Cerebral Artery (ACA)

**Either side:** contralateral leg weakness and sensory loss, frontal lobe dysfunction (poor judgment, apraxia, abulia/flat affect, imitation behavior, incontinence), alien hand sign or hemiballismus.

**Dominant hemisphere:** transcortical motor aphasia (similar to Broca's but with preserved repetition).

**Non-dominant hemisphere:** acute confusional state, contralateral hemineglect.

**Bilateral ACA occlusion** (azygous ACA variant): bilateral leg weakness + incontinence mimicking a spinal cord lesion, executive dysfunction with abulia or akinetic mutism (awake unresponsiveness).

#### Middle Cerebral Artery (MCA)

**Superior trunk occlusion (either side):** contralateral face and arm hemiparesis, hemisensory loss (face/arm), conjugate ipsilateral eye deviation. *Dominant:* Broca's aphasia, bilateral apraxia. *Non-dominant:* neglect, anosognosia.

**Inferior trunk occlusion (either side):** contralateral superior quadrantanopia. *Dominant:* Wernicke's aphasia (patients may appear confused or psychotic — can be difficult to differentiate from primary psychiatric event or metabolic encephalopathy). *Non-dominant:* severe left hemineglect with anosognosia, motor neglect, sensory neglect.

**Proximal MCA (M1) occlusion:** contralateral hemiplegia (face/arm > leg), hemisensory loss, hemianopia, ipsilateral eye deviation. *Dominant:* global aphasia. *Non-dominant:* hemispatial neglect, drowsiness, eyelid-opening apraxia.

**Deep territory (lenticulostriate arteries):** contralateral hemiparesis (may mimic lacunar syndrome). Larger infarcts may functionally cut off the cortex and mimic cortical deficits.

**MCA anatomy refresher:** M1 = horizontal (lenticulostriates); M2 = insular segment (before superior/inferior split — M2 occlusions may be amenable to EVT); M3 = inferior cortical branches; M4 = superior cortical branches.

#### Anterior Choroidal Artery

"Triple H": contralateral hemiparesis + hemisensory loss + hemianopia. Absence of cortical signs (e.g., no neglect) may differentiate from MCA stroke.

#### Posterior Cerebral Artery (PCA)

**Either side:** hemianopia or superior quadrantanopia, thalamic involvement → contralateral sensory loss or reduced arousal.

**Dominant:** alexia without agraphia (splenium infarction cuts off visual input from language centers), visual agnosia, transcortical sensory aphasia, thalamic aphasia, inferomedial temporal infarction → global amnesia or agitated delirium.

**Non-dominant:** prosopagnosia (inability to recognize faces).

**Bilateral PCA:** Anton syndrome (cortical blindness with unawareness → patient confabulates vision), Charles Bonnet syndrome (release visual hallucinations), agitated delirium.

#### Thalamic Syndromes

| Thalamic Region | Frequency | Supply | Key Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anterior** | 12% | Tuberothalamic arteries (from PComm) | Disorientation, euphoria, apathy, anterograde memory impairment, transcortical aphasia (left) |
| **Paramedian** | 35% | Paramedian arteries (from P1/PCA); Artery of Percheron variant → bilateral | Impaired arousal (hours to days), mood/behavioral changes. *Bilateral:* stupor/coma, amnesia with confabulation, vertical gaze paresis |
| **Inferolateral** | 45% | Thalamogeniculate arteries (from P2/PCA) | Central pain syndrome (Dejerine-Roussy), hemibody sensory loss, ataxic hemiparesis |
| **Posterior** | Rare | Posterior choroidal arteries | Homonymous quadrantanopia, hemisensory loss, transcortical aphasia |

#### Basilar Artery

**Proximal basilar:** impaired consciousness (somnolence → coma), quadriplegia, "crossed" paralysis, abnormal movements, oculomotor abnormalities (horizontal gaze palsy, INO, one-and-a-half syndrome, skew deviation), pinpoint pupils, bulbar symptoms (dysphagia, dysarthria, facial weakness, palatal myoclonus).

**Mid-basilar (locked-in syndrome):** quadriplegia + facial paralysis, horizontal gaze palsy (vertical gaze/blinking may remain intact), anarthria, dysphagia, vertigo.

**Top of the basilar:** ischemia of midbrain/thalamus/occipital lobes (NOT pons). Impaired consciousness, pupillary abnormalities (variable — dilated, mid-position, or small), vertical gaze impairment, hemianopia or cortical blindness, amnesia/agitation/hallucinations, ataxia/tremor if cerebellum involved.

> [!warning] Basilar occlusion may often result from in situ atherosclerotic plaques with superimposed thrombosis — can present as a stuttering process. The natural history without intervention is very poor. These patients may be more amenable to thrombolysis than other stroke types due to fresher thrombus.

#### Lacunar Syndromes

| Syndrome | Symptoms | Localization |
|---|---|---|
| **Pure motor hemiparesis** | Isolated contralateral face/arm/leg weakness | Corona radiata, posterior limb internal capsule, cerebral peduncle, anterior pons |
| **Pure sensory stroke** | Hemibody sensory loss of all modalities | Thalamic VPL/VPM (thalamoperforators) |
| **Ataxic hemiparesis** | Ipsilateral weakness + limb ataxia disproportionate to weakness | Same as pure motor |
| **Dysarthria-clumsy hand** | Dysarthria, facial weakness, hand clumsiness | Same as pure motor |
| **Sensorimotor stroke** | Combined thalamic lacune + pure motor hemiparesis | Thalamocapsular |

### History Pearls

- **Establish last known well (LKW)** — the single most critical historical data point
- Wake-up strokes: MRI DWI-FLAIR mismatch can identify patients within the treatment window (WAKE-UP trial)
- Ask about **anticoagulant use** (affects thrombolysis eligibility)
- Ask about **ACE inhibitor use** (risk factor for thrombolytic-induced angioedema)
- Screen for **stroke mimics**: seizure with Todd's paralysis, hypoglycemia, complicated migraine, conversion disorder
- CT perfusion can sometimes reveal a seizure mimic (shows *increased* regional perfusion instead of decreased)

### Physical Exam — NIHSS

The **NIHSS** is the standardized quantitative assessment that drives treatment decisions. Key components:

| Domain | Scoring |
|---|---|
| Level of consciousness | 0 (alert) to 3 (unresponsive) |
| LOC questions (month, age) | 0 (correct) to 2 (both wrong/aphasic) |
| LOC commands | 0 (correct) to 2 (both failed) |
| Gaze | 0 (normal) to 2 (complete palsy) |
| Visual fields | 0 (normal) to 3 (bilateral hemianopia) |
| Facial palsy | 0 (none) to 3 (complete unilateral) |
| Motor — each limb | 0 (normal) to 4 (no movement). Arms: drift before 10 sec. Legs: drift before 5 sec |
| Ataxia | 0 (none) to 2 (two+ limbs) |
| Sensory | 0 (normal) to 2 (severe loss) |
| Language | 0 (no aphasia) to 3 (mute) |
| Dysarthria | 0 (normal) to 2 (severe/mute) |
| Extinction/inattention | 0 (normal) to 2 (profound hemi-inattention) |

> [!warning] NIHSS Limitations
> - Does not correlate well with functional outcomes (e.g., severe aphasia = 2 points, same as bilateral subtle arm weakness)
> - **IV thrombolysis is NOT recommended for non-disabling stroke with NIHSS 0–5**
> - NIHSS is **misleading in posterior circulation strokes** — basilar occlusion can present with deceptively low scores

> [!warning] Red Flags Requiring Immediate Action
> - NIHSS ≥ 6 with LVO concern → activate thrombectomy team
> - Rapidly declining level of consciousness → consider basilar occlusion or malignant MCA edema
> - Cerebellar stroke with brainstem compression → neurosurgical emergency

## Diagnostic Workup

### Immediate (Do Not Delay Treatment)

- **Non-contrast CT head** — rule out hemorrhage; the only imaging test required before IV thrombolysis
- **STAT fingerstick glucose** — the only lab required before thrombolysis (rule out hypoglycemia as mimic)
- **CT Angiography (CTA) head and neck** — identify LVO and guide thrombectomy decision; obtained in parallel, not sequentially. Never delay definitive imaging for "contrast nephropathy" concerns

### Admission Lab Panel

- CBC with platelets
- Electrolytes including Ca/Mg/Phos
- Liver function tests
- Coagulation studies: PT, PTT, fibrinogen; **anti-Xa level for patients on oral Xa inhibitors**
- Blood cultures x2 if concern for endocarditis (fever, IVDU history)
- Pregnancy test as appropriate
- Troponin

### NCCT Interpretation

Beyond ruling out hemorrhage, the NCCT provides important information:

1. **Hyperdense vessel sign** — clot within an artery. Dense MCA sign: ~33% sensitivity, 95% specificity for MCA occlusion. Hyperdense MCA > 8 mm predicts poor recanalization after thrombolysis. Can also be seen in ACA, PCA, basilar. Differentiate from atherosclerosis, polycythemia, or streak artifact by comparing to other vessels.
2. **Loss of grey-white matter differentiation** — can appear within 1 hour; reflects irreversibly infarcted tissue. Look for: disappearing basal ganglia sign, insular ribbon sign, cortical ribbon sign. Use narrow windowing (width 8–35, level 32–35 HU) to accentuate differences. The **ASPECTS score** systematically quantifies the extent of MCA/ICA infarction (normal = 10, subtract 1 per affected region).
3. **Vasogenic edema** — frank hypodensity within ~3–6 hours reflects irreversible infarction. May cause midline shift and compression (malignant MCA syndrome).
4. **Signs of cerebral venous sinus thrombosis** — seek especially in younger patients.

### Advanced Imaging

**CT Perfusion (CTP)** — identifies core vs. penumbra mismatch. Common approach:
- Core infarct: CBF < 30%
- Core + penumbra: Tmax > 6 seconds
- Key parameter: **mismatch ratio** (penumbra/core). Ratios > 1.2–1.8 suggest benefit from EVT (EXTEND-IA used > 1.2; SWIFT PRIME used > 1.8)
- Core volume < 50–70 mL → favorable prognosis following intervention
- Note: CTP evaluates supratentorial (hemispheric) stroke only — not designed for posterior circulation. It is also a snapshot at one point in time and may not reflect integrated tissue status.

**MRI DWI/FLAIR:**
- DWI hyperintensity appears within ~10 minutes with high specificity — **reference standard for core infarct**
- T2/FLAIR hyperintensity doesn't emerge until ~4.5–6 hours
- DWI positive + FLAIR negative = **hyperacute stroke** (within ~4.5 hours) → basis for treating wake-up strokes
- GRE/SWI: detects acute thrombus (deoxyhemoglobin → dark signal) and hemorrhagic transformation

### Etiologic Workup (Post-Acute)

- Transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) ± bubble study
- Prolonged cardiac monitoring (≥ 48 hours, or implantable loop recorder if cryptogenic)
- Lipid panel, HbA1c
- Hypercoagulability panel (if age < 50 or no traditional risk factors)
- Vessel imaging if not already obtained

## Management

### Acute Management — Intravenous Thrombolysis

**Alteplase (tPA)**
- Dose: 0.9 mg/kg IV (max 90 mg); 10% as bolus, remainder over 60 minutes
- Window: within **4.5 hours** of last known well
- Extended criteria (3–4.5 hour window): age > 80, NIHSS > 25, oral anticoagulant use regardless of INR, and history of both diabetes and prior stroke are no longer absolute contraindications per AHA/ASA 2026 guidelines

**Tenecteplase (TNK)** — emerging standard
- Dose: **0.25 mg/kg IV bolus (max 25 mg)** — this is the STROKE dose, NOT the MI dose
- Advantages: single bolus (vs. 1-hour infusion), simpler administration, ideal for drip-and-ship models
- **TRACE-2 (2023):** non-inferiority to alteplase for 90-day functional outcomes
- **ORIGINAL (2024):** confirmed non-inferiority (JAMA)
- AHA/ASA 2026: Class 2a recommendation as alternative to alteplase
- For patients with LVO who are eligible for thrombolysis, tenecteplase 0.25 mg/kg is now recommended *over* alteplase (ESO expedited recommendation)

> [!warning] TNK Dosing Caution (IBCC)
> With increasing use of tenecteplase, it is often impossible to stop administration of thrombolysis (since it's a single bolus, not an infusion). Additionally, TNK packaging may reflect MI dosing — **do NOT use MI dosing for stroke**.

**Key Contraindications to IV Thrombolysis:**
- Hemorrhage on CT
- Platelets < 100,000 (if known)
- INR > 1.7 or aPTT > 40 (if on anticoagulants)
- Recent major surgery or trauma (within 14–21 days)
- Active internal bleeding
- Blood glucose < 50 mg/dL
- DOACs taken within 48 hours with abnormal coagulation studies (within 24 hours if impaired renal function)
- IV thrombolysis is NOT recommended for non-disabling stroke with NIHSS 0–5

### Acute Management — Endovascular Thrombectomy (EVT)

**Standard Window (0–6 hours):**
- Indicated for LVO (ICA, M1, and in select cases M2 and basilar) with disabling deficit (NIHSS ≥ 6)
- ASPECTS ≥ 6 on NCCT (landmark trials: MR CLEAN, ESCAPE, EXTEND-IA, SWIFT PRIME, REVASCAT)

**Extended Window (6–24 hours):**
- **DAWN (2018):** clinical-core mismatch 6–24 hours; NNT 2.8
- **DEFUSE 3 (2018):** perfusion mismatch 6–16 hours

**Large Core Infarcts (ASPECTS 3–5):**
- **SELECT2 (2023)** — EVT superior to medical management for ASPECTS 3–5 within 24 hours
- **ANGEL-ASPECT (2023)** — EVT for large core confirmed benefit
- **TENSION (2023)** — EVT for large infarcts on NCCT (Lancet)
- **LASTE (2024)** — EVT for large infarcts of unrestricted size (NEJM)
- Pooled analysis of 6 RCTs (3,897 patients): OR 2.19 (95% CI 1.81–2.64) for functional independence

**Post-Procedure Management:**
- Reperfusion graded by **TICI score**: 0 (no perfusion) → 1 (minimal) → 2A (<2/3 territory) → 2B (complete, slower) → 3 (normal). TICI 2B or 3 = successful reperfusion
- Monitor groin puncture site for **limb ischemia** or **retroperitoneal hematoma**
- Blood pressure management (see below)

**Thrombectomy alone vs. combined with thrombolysis:** Recent RCTs suggest EVT alone may be non-inferior to combined IV thrombolysis + EVT in some patients — this is an evolving area.

### Blood Pressure Management

> [!warning] Hypertension is a normal, physiologic response that improves brain perfusion. In most cases it should be left alone for the first ~24–48 hours. BP will generally decrease on its own. 

**Patients NOT receiving any intervention:**
- BP reduction indicated ONLY if: (1) hypertensive emergency with target organ damage (MI, nephropathy, pulmonary edema), or (2) BP > 220/120 (even then, benefit is uncertain — Class IIb, AHA 2026)
- If reduction is needed: **gentle** — ~15% reduction during the first 24 hours

**Patients receiving thrombolysis:**
- Pre-thrombolysis: BP < 185/110
- Post-thrombolysis: BP < 180/105 for 24 hours
- **ENCHANTED RCT:** lowering SBP to 130–140 within 1 hour of thrombolysis didn't affect functional outcomes but DID cause fewer ICH (15% vs 19%). For patients at higher hemorrhage risk, lower targets might be reasonable.

**Patients receiving EVT:**
- Pre-procedure: maintain flat position to improve perfusion; reasonable to maintain BP < 185/110. **Avoid hypotension during intubation** — even small reductions (>10% decrease) may correlate with worse outcomes
- Post-EVT: Most guidelines recommend < 180/105. A **reasonable goal is SBP 140–180** (AHA 2026). RCTs demonstrated **worse outcomes with more intensive BP control targeting SBP < 140**

**Preferred agents:** nicardipine or clevidipine infusions (IBCC preference over labetalol)

**Hypotension management (IBCC):**
- Hypotension is potentially dangerous — promotes cerebral hypoperfusion, associated with worse outcomes
- Evaluate for etiology (hypovolemia, etc.) and treat causes
- Discontinue antihypertensives
- Vasopressors are not evidence-based but may be reasonable in specific scenarios (e.g., perfusion-dependent patient whose exam worsens with low BP and improves with pressors)

### Antiplatelet & Anticoagulant Therapy

**Antiplatelet therapy (noncardioembolic stroke):**
- **Aspirin 325 mg PO loading** (or 300 mg rectally), then 81 mg daily. Aspirin allergy → clopidogrel 300 mg load
- This is one of the most strongly evidence-based therapies for acute stroke (reduces recurrence and mortality: 8.2% vs 9.1%, p = 0.001)
- If thrombolysis given: delay 24 hours, start after repeat CT confirms no hemorrhage
- If possible decompressive craniectomy planned: may need to delay

**Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT):**
- Indicated for noncardioembolic high-risk TIA (ABCD2 ≥ 4) or minor stroke (NIHSS ≤ 5), with no indication for long-term anticoagulation
- Aspirin + clopidogrel × 21 days, then single agent (CHANCE, POINT)
- Intracranial stenosis: DAPT for 90 days (SAMMPRIS)

**Anticoagulation for cardioembolic stroke:**
- Two competing considerations: anticoagulation increases hemorrhagic conversion risk BUT decreases recurrent stroke risk
- General recommendation: start oral anticoagulation **~2–14 days** after stroke
- **Early anticoagulation (< 48 hours) does NOT reduce neuroworsening or improve outcomes — not recommended** (Grade III, AHA/ASA 2026)
- Bridging with heparin is not generally beneficial
- For larger strokes with higher hemorrhagic risk → delay closer to ~14 days
- The 1-3-6-12 day rule is a practical heuristic, not a hard guideline

**DVT prophylaxis:**
- Post-thrombolysis or successful EVT: delay chemical prophylaxis 24 hours
- Intermittent pneumatic compression for patients who cannot receive chemical prophylaxis
- **LMWH is preferred** for DVT prophylaxis (not UFH)

### Supportive ICU Care — The 7 Core Topics

#### [1] Volume Management

- **Euvolemia** is the target — both hypovolemia and hypervolemia are harmful
- Hypovolemia compromises perfusion of ischemic tissue and can cause labile BP
- Consider IV fluids for: hypotensive patients, NPO patients for extended periods, patients with labile BP and hypovolemia
- Follow I/O balance carefully

#### [2] Respiratory Support

- Target SpO2 > 94%
- Supplemental O2 is NOT recommended for non-hypoxemic patients
- **Exception: for patients planned to undergo EVT, elevated FiO2 before EVT may be reasonable (e.g., 10 L/min via NRB)** (Class IIb, AHA/ASA 2026)
- Indications for intubation: respiratory failure (especially hypercapnia exacerbating ICP), bulbar dysfunction with inability to protect airway, procedural sedation for EVT, impaired consciousness with neuroworsening
- Anterior strokes usually don't impair airway protection unless edema compresses other areas (malignant MCA syndrome) — need for airway protection in an anterior stroke should trigger consideration of decompressive craniectomy
- Brainstem and thalamic strokes may directly affect consciousness and respiratory centers → intubation often necessary

#### [3] Temperature Management

- Elevated temperature (> 38°C) should be investigated and treated aggressively
- Workup for infection (blood cultures, UA, CXR)
- **Scheduled acetaminophen 1g q6hr** is preferred over PRN dosing for consistent temperature control
- INTREPID trial: enforced normothermia using automated surface devices doesn't improve functional outcomes
- Unlike ICH, ischemic stroke is **unlikely to cause neurogenic fever**

#### [4] Glycemic Control

- Hyperglycemia correlates with worse outcomes, but tight control causes harm
- **SHINE trial:** targeting glucose 80–130 mg/dL caused increased severe hypoglycemia and worse outcomes
- Target **140–180 mg/dL**; avoid hypoglycemia. Hypoglycemia is especially detrimental to injured brain
- 2026 guidelines emphasize hyperglycemia management as a quality metric

#### [5] Seizure Management

- Seizure incidence < 10%. Risk factors: higher stroke severity, cortical involvement (especially multilobar), hemorrhagic conversion
- **In the context of AIS, a seizure may reflect hemorrhagic transformation** — maintain low threshold for CT
- **Seizure prophylaxis is NOT recommended** (Class III, AHA 2026)
- If seizures occur, treat as usual and continue maintenance AED at least short-term
- Consider continuous EEG for: fluctuating neuro deficits, unexplained coma/AMS, post-seizure persistent AMS, seizures requiring active therapy, paralysis precluding neuro exam

#### [6] Nutritional Support & Dysphagia

- **All patients must receive bedside dysphagia screening before any diet** (supervised water swallow by nurse or SLP). Failure → comprehensive evaluation
- Intubated patients: enteral nutrition within 24–48 hours
- Non-intubated with significant dysphagia: enteral nutrition within < 7 days (earlier if pre-existing malnutrition). Small-bore feeding tube is often adequate for weeks while swallowing recovers
- Persistent dysphagia > 2–3 weeks → consider PEG tube
- **FOOD3 trial:** early PEG tube placement had *worse* outcomes vs. nasogastric tube

#### [7] Statin Therapy

- High-intensity statin (atorvastatin 40–80 mg or rosuvastatin 20–40 mg) for all non-cardioembolic strokes
- Target LDL < 70 mg/dL (TST: LDL < 70 superior to 90–110; SPARCL: atorvastatin 80 mg reduced recurrent stroke by 16%)

## Specific ICU Management Situations

### Neuroworsening in AIS

**Differential diagnosis (most common considerations):**
- Hypoglycemia
- Infarct extension, reocclusion, or additional embolic events
- Hemorrhagic transformation (even without thrombolysis or EVT)
- Edema (malignant MCA syndrome)
- Elevated ICP (e.g., cerebellar stroke obstructing cerebral aqueduct)
- Blood pressure: PRES (from hypertension) or brain malperfusion (from hypotension)
- Medication effects (procedural sedation)
- Seizure (including nonconvulsive seizures and postictal state)

**Approach:**
1. STAT fingerstick glucose
2. If hypotension suspected → raise BP with vasopressors, repeat exam
3. STAT CT scan (hemorrhage, edema, elevated ICP)
4. EEG (especially for unexplained altered mental status despite normal neuroimaging)

### Thrombolytic-Induced Angioedema

Occurs in ~2% of thrombolysis cases — a **bradykinin-mediated** class effect that may occur with any thrombolytic.

**Diagnosis:**
- Onset ~30–120 minutes after tPA infusion (later than anaphylaxis, which is usually < 30 min)
- **Isolated orolingual swelling** (especially tongue), often asymmetric and contralateral to the CNS lesion (ipsilateral to affected body)
- Risk factors: ACE inhibitor use, C1-esterase deficiency, MCA territory strokes involving insular cortex

**Differentiate from anaphylaxis** (urticaria, flushing, pruritus, wheezing, GI symptoms) and **lingual hematoma** (ecchymosis, dusky/purple tongue, firm/tense swelling).

**Management:**
1. Stop thrombolytic infusion (moot with TNK since it's a bolus). Stop all bradykinin-inducing medications (ACE inhibitors, DPP-IV inhibitors/gliptins, aliskiren, sirolimus/tacrolimus/everolimus)
2. Consider **icatibant 30 mg SC** or **C1-esterase concentrate 20 IU/kg** (expensive, limited evidence — a few case reports, referenced in AHA/ASA 2026). Reasonable if it might avoid intubation
3. Observe airway — **most cases are mild and self-limited** (~only 10% cause upper airway obstruction). Thrombolytics have short half-lives, so improvement is expected
4. If airway threatened → intubate (prepare for difficult airway — fiberoptic with double-setup and cricothyrotomy readiness). Often can extubate next day

> [!warning] Steroids, epinephrine, and antihistamines are NOT effective for bradykinin-mediated angioedema. If given unnecessarily, epinephrine may cause dangerous BP spikes and steroids cause hyperglycemia. Only use these if anaphylaxis is genuinely suspected.

### Hemorrhagic Transformation

A very broad term encompassing three entities:
1. **Petechial hemorrhages (HI-1, HI-2)** — most common, usually clinically insignificant
2. **Asymptomatic hematomas (PH-1)** — < 30% of stroke volume, mild mass effect
3. **Symptomatic hematomas (PH-2)** — > 30% of stroke volume, substantial mass effect. Occurs in ~2% without thrombolysis, up to ~6% with thrombolysis

**Risk factors:** large infarct size (> 1/3 MCA territory), coagulopathy, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, older age, diabetes/hyperglycemia, uncontrolled HTN, endocarditis.

**Management of symptomatic hemorrhagic transformation:**
- Stop thrombolytic and anticoagulants immediately
- Aggressively reverse coagulopathies (especially post-thrombolysis)
- Baseline coagulation labs (INR, PTT, fibrinogen, CBC, type & cross) — but don't delay reversal of known agents
- Manage as intracranial hemorrhage

### Malignant MCA Syndrome

Proximal MCA infarction → marked edema → herniation and death. **Edema usually peaks after 3–4 days** (reperfusion may accelerate this).

**Risk factors:** younger patients (less atrophy = less space), dense MCA sign (large territory at risk), > 50% MCA territory involved, multi-territory infarction, DWI volume > 82 mL within 6 hours, mass effect on imaging, rapid edema (frank hypodensity within 6 hours, early encephalopathy).

**Key concept (IBCC):** patients with malignant MCA syndrome can have **normal ICP** — deterioration results from **tissue shifts** rather than globally elevated ICP. Therefore, invasive ICP monitoring is usually not helpful.

**Management:**
- Clinical exam + interval CT scanning (q24h + PRN) to track progress
- Medical therapies for edema (hypertonic therapy) as temporizing bridge to craniectomy
- AHA guidelines: decrease in level of consciousness attributed to brain swelling is a reasonable trigger for decompressive craniectomy
- **Decompressive hemicraniectomy** in patients ≤ 60 years with deterioration (DECIMAL, DESTINY, HAMLET: NNT 2 for survival)

### Cerebellar Stroke & Posterior Fossa Syndrome

Limited posterior fossa space makes cerebellar infarction dangerous:
1. **Noncommunicating hydrocephalus** — compression of cerebral aqueduct or fourth ventricle
2. **Brainstem compression** — if more severe

**Risk factors for deterioration:** PICA territory (largest cerebellar supply), cerebellar vermis involvement.

**Management:**
- **Ventriculostomy** for obstructive hydrocephalus (Class I, AHA/ASA 2026). Use **conservative CSF drainage** — aggressive drainage risks upward transtentorial herniation of cerebellum
- **Suboccipital craniectomy** — not necessarily required at time of ventriculostomy; based on infarct size, clinical condition, brainstem compression, response to medical management. If neurological deterioration from brainstem compression or volume > 35 mL → decompressive craniectomy (Class I)
- Outcomes tend to be **better than MCA decompressive craniectomy** (smaller stroke, less eloquent tissue — 35–40% achieve functional independence)

### Basilar Artery Thrombosis

- May result from in situ atherosclerotic plaques with superimposed thrombosis → **stuttering clinical course**
- Natural history without intervention is very poor
- Fresher thrombus may make these patients more amenable to thrombolysis
- Recent studies support endovascular therapy for basilar strokes

### Moyamoya Disease

Chronic, progressive, idiopathic occlusive disease involving ICA, proximal ACA, and proximal MCA. Hypertrophied small collateral vessels create "puff of smoke" appearance on angiography.

**Epidemiology:** peak incidence in 40s, more common in women of East Asian descent.

**Presentations:** ischemic stroke (most common), ICH (~1/3, typically basal ganglia from fragile collaterals), subarachnoid hemorrhage, recurrent headaches.

**MRI findings:** T2 flow voids in basal ganglia/basilar cistern, FLAIR sulcal brightness from slow pial collateral flow.

**Management:** surgical revascularization (STA-MCA bypass), caution with anticoagulation due to fragile collateral vessels, aspirin commonly prescribed for ischemic symptoms.

## Clinical Pearls

> [!tip] Pearl 1
> **The only test needed before IV thrombolysis is a non-contrast CT and a fingerstick glucose.** Don't wait for coagulation studies unless the patient is known to be on anticoagulants. Time lost to unnecessary labs is time lost to reperfusion.

> [!tip] Pearl 2
> **Tenecteplase is rapidly replacing alteplase.** A single IV bolus is far simpler than a 1-hour infusion, particularly for drip-and-ship. But remember: TNK packaging may reflect MI dosing — the stroke dose is 0.25 mg/kg (max 25 mg), NOT the MI dose. And since it's a bolus, you can't stop it once given.

> [!tip] Pearl 3
> **NIHSS can be misleading in posterior circulation strokes.** A basilar occlusion can present with isolated vertigo and dysarthria (low NIHSS) and then rapidly progress to coma. Always get a CTA when posterior stroke is suspected.

> [!tip] Pearl 4
> **Large core infarcts (ASPECTS 3–5) are no longer automatic exclusions from thrombectomy.** SELECT2, ANGEL-ASPECT, and TENSION changed the paradigm in 2023. LASTE (2024) extended this further. Discuss with your neurointerventionalist.

> [!tip] Pearl 5
> **Permissive hypertension matters.** Hypertension is a normal, physiologic response that improves brain perfusion. If a patient is not a thrombolysis candidate, aggressively dropping their BP can collapse the penumbra. Leave it alone unless SBP > 220, DBP > 120, or there's target organ damage. Even in those cases, reduce only ~15% in 24 hours.

> [!tip] Pearl 6
> **Post-EVT: do NOT target SBP < 140.** Multiple RCTs showed worse outcomes with intensive BP control. A reasonable target is SBP 140–180.

> [!tip] Pearl 7
> **Think "tissue, not time."** Different patients progress at different speeds — a slow progressor with robust collaterals may still have salvageable penumbra at 12+ hours, while a rapid progressor may have completed their infarct at 3 hours. Perfusion imaging determines candidacy, not the clock alone.

> [!tip] Pearl 8
> **Hypotension is the enemy.** Even small BP drops (>10% decrease) during intubation for EVT correlate with worse outcomes. Avoid dropping BP during RSI — use push-dose pressors if needed.

> [!tip] Pearl 9
> **A seizure in an AIS patient may be the first sign of hemorrhagic transformation.** Low threshold for STAT CT. Don't just treat the seizure and move on.

> [!tip] Pearl 10
> **Dysphagia screening before anything oral — including medications.** Use scheduled acetaminophen (1g q6hr) rather than PRN dosing for fever. FOOD3 trial showed early PEG tubes are worse than NG tubes. A small-bore feeding tube can sustain a patient for weeks while swallowing recovers.

> [!tip] Pearl 11
> **Thrombolytic-induced angioedema is bradykinin-mediated, NOT histamine-mediated.** Steroids, epinephrine, and antihistamines don't work. Consider icatibant. Most cases are mild and self-limited (~90%). Prepare for difficult airway if intubation is needed.

## Evidence & Landmark Trials

### Foundational Thrombolysis Trials

| Trial | Year | Key Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **NINDS tPA** | 1995 | IV alteplase within 3 hours improved functional outcomes at 90 days (NNT 8); established tPA as standard of care |
| **ECASS III** | 2008 | Extended the alteplase window to 4.5 hours; OR 1.34 for favorable outcome |
| **WAKE-UP** | 2018 | MRI DWI-FLAIR mismatch can identify patients eligible for alteplase in wake-up strokes with unknown onset |
| **TRACE-2** | 2023 | Tenecteplase 0.25 mg/kg non-inferior to alteplase within 4.5 hours; single bolus vs. 1-hour infusion |
| **ORIGINAL** | 2024 | Confirmed tenecteplase non-inferiority to alteplase; JAMA 2024 |

### Foundational Thrombectomy Trials

| Trial | Year | Key Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **MR CLEAN** | 2015 | First positive RCT for EVT in LVO stroke within 6 hours; shifted global practice |
| **ESCAPE** | 2015 | EVT with emphasis on rapid workflow; median door-to-groin 51 min; NNT 4 |
| **EXTEND-IA** | 2015 | EVT with CTP selection; 71% achieved reperfusion; dramatic functional benefit |
| **SWIFT PRIME** | 2015 | EVT with modern stent retrievers; NNT 4 for functional independence |
| **REVASCAT** | 2015 | EVT beneficial up to 8 hours with appropriate imaging selection |
| **DAWN** | 2018 | EVT beneficial 6–24 hours using clinical-core mismatch; NNT 2.8 |
| **DEFUSE 3** | 2018 | EVT beneficial 6–16 hours using perfusion mismatch on CTP |

### Large Core Thrombectomy Trials (2023–2024)

| Trial | Year | Key Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **SELECT2** | 2023 | EVT superior to medical management for large core (ASPECTS 3–5); NEJM |
| **ANGEL-ASPECT** | 2023 | EVT for large core in Chinese population; confirmed benefit |
| **TENSION** | 2023 | EVT for large infarcts identified on NCCT; Lancet 2023 |
| **LASTE** | 2024 | EVT for large infarcts of unrestricted size; NEJM 2024 |

### Other Key Trials

| Trial | Year | Key Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **CHANCE** | 2013 | DAPT (aspirin + clopidogrel) × 21 days superior to aspirin alone for minor stroke/TIA |
| **POINT** | 2018 | Confirmed DAPT benefit for 21 days in minor stroke/high-risk TIA |
| **SAMMPRIS** | 2011 | Aggressive medical therapy superior to intracranial stenting for intracranial stenosis |
| **SPARCL** | 2006 | High-dose atorvastatin reduced recurrent stroke risk by 16% |
| **TST** | 2020 | LDL target < 70 mg/dL superior to 90–110 mg/dL for secondary stroke prevention |
| **NASCET** | 1991 | CEA highly beneficial for symptomatic carotid stenosis ≥ 70%; NNT 6 at 2 years |
| **CLOSE** | 2017 | PFO closure superior to medical therapy for cryptogenic stroke in young adults |
| **DECIMAL/DESTINY/HAMLET** | 2007 | Decompressive hemicraniectomy for malignant MCA infarction: NNT 2 for survival in age ≤ 60 |
| **BP-TARGET** | 2022 | Intensive BP lowering (SBP 100–129) post-EVT showed trend toward less ICH but no functional benefit |
| **ENCHANTED** | 2019 | Intensive BP control post-thrombolysis (SBP 130–140) → no functional benefit but fewer ICH (15% vs 19%) |
| **SHINE** | 2019 | Tight glycemic control (80–130 mg/dL) in AIS caused harm — increased severe hypoglycemia |
| **FOOD3** | 2005 | Early PEG tube placement worse than nasogastric feeding in dysphagic stroke patients |
| **INTREPID** | 2024 | Enforced normothermia via automated surface device did not improve functional outcomes |
| **BASICS** | 2021 | EVT for basilar artery occlusion — did not show superiority over medical management, but debated |

### Recent Evidence (2023–2026)

- **Mechanical Thrombectomy for Large Ischemic Stroke: A Critical Appraisal of 6 RCTs** (Stroke, 2025) — pooled analysis of 3,897 patients showing OR 2.19 for functional independence with EVT.
- **Efficacy and Safety of Tenecteplase in Acute Ischemic Stroke: Meta-Analysis of RCTs** (PMC, 2025) — confirms non-inferiority of tenecteplase across multiple trials.
- **Antithrombotic Therapy for Primary and Secondary Prevention of Ischemic Stroke: JACC State-of-the-Art Review** (JACC, 2023) — comprehensive review of antiplatelet and anticoagulant strategies.
- **2026 AHA/ASA Guideline for the Early Management of Patients with Acute Ischemic Stroke** — most current comprehensive guideline; expands thrombolysis and thrombectomy eligibility, endorses tenecteplase, updates hyperglycemia and dysphagia management.

## Key Guidelines

### AHA/ASA 2026 Guideline for the Early Management of Acute Ischemic Stroke

Most current comprehensive guideline (published 2026, based on literature through March 2025). Key updates:

- **Tenecteplase** is now a Class 2a alternative to alteplase
- **Expanded thrombectomy eligibility** to include large core infarcts and broader time windows
- **Modified thrombolysis contraindications** — several previously absolute contraindications reclassified
- **Hyperglycemia management** emphasized as a quality metric
- **Dysphagia screening** mandated before any oral intake
- **Pediatric stroke** considerations included for the first time
- **Seizure prophylaxis is NOT recommended** (Class III)
- **Early anticoagulation (< 48 hours) for cardioembolic stroke is NOT recommended** (Grade III)

### AHA/ASA 2024 Guideline for Primary Prevention of Stroke

- Emphasizes BP control, AF detection, statin therapy, and lifestyle modification
- Highlights health equity and social determinants as key factors in stroke prevention

### Additional Guidelines

- **AHA/ASA 2021 Secondary Prevention Guidelines** — comprehensive guidance on long-term risk factor management post-stroke
- **ESO (European Stroke Organisation) Guidelines** — largely concordant with AHA/ASA; notably endorsed tenecteplase earlier
- **Australian Stroke Foundation Clinical Guidelines for Stroke Management** — referenced by Deranged Physiology; incorporated large core thrombectomy recommendations in July 2023
