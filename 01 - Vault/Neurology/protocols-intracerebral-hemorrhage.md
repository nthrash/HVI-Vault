## Overview

**Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH)** is non-traumatic bleeding directly into the brain parenchyma. It accounts for roughly 10–15% of all strokes, yet is disproportionately lethal — only half of patients survive to one year, and only ~20% achieve functional independence at one year. ICH is distinct from subarachnoid hemorrhage and hemorrhagic conversion of ischemic stroke; the term "hemorrhagic stroke" should be avoided because it lumps these very different entities together.

The condition is most commonly driven by small vessel disease — either hypertensive lipohyalinosis or cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) — but macrovascular lesions (aneurysms, AVMs, dural fistulas) and other causes must be actively excluded in the right clinical context. Management is time-sensitive: hematoma expansion occurs most dangerously in the first 6–24 hours and is the primary driver of early neurological deterioration.

> [!tip] **Why This Matters**
> ICH is one of the few acute neurological conditions where early aggressive intervention — BP control, anticoagulation reversal, surgical decision-making — can meaningfully change outcomes. The window is narrow. Know the approach cold.

### ICH vs. Other Intracranial Hemorrhage Types

| Type | Location | Classic Cause | Hallmark Feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ICH** (this topic) | Within brain parenchyma | Hypertension, CAA | Focal deficits; progresses over minutes–hours; hyperdense intraparenchymal blood on CT |
| **SAH** (subarachnoid hemorrhage) | Subarachnoid space (between pia and arachnoid) | Ruptured aneurysm (~85%) | Thunderclap "worst headache of my life"; blood in cisterns/sulci on CT; no focal deficits initially |
| **SDH** (subdural hematoma) | Between dura and arachnoid | Bridging vein tear — often trauma, elderly on anticoagulants | Crescent-shaped hyperdensity following brain contour; may be chronic (hypodense) with insidious presentation |
| **EDH** (epidural hematoma) | Between skull and dura | Middle meningeal artery tear — almost always traumatic | Biconvex (lens-shaped) hyperdensity; classically a "lucid interval" after head trauma; often temporal location |
| **Aneurysm (unruptured)** | Vessel wall (usually circle of Willis) | Congenital/acquired vessel wall weakness | Incidental or causes mass effect (e.g., CN III palsy with posterior communicating artery aneurysm); rupture → SAH ± ICH |

> [!tip] The key distinguishing feature for bedside orientation: **ICH = blood in the tissue itself**, causing focal deficits. SAH = blood in the cisterns, causing headache. SDH/EDH = blood between layers, often trauma-related with CT shape clues (crescent vs. biconvex).

## Etiology & Risk Factors

### Small Vessel Disease ("Primary ICH")

**Hypertensive intraparenchymal hemorrhage** is by far the most common cause, accounting for ~80% of all ICH. The underlying mechanism is arteriolar lipohyalinosis — the same process that drives lacunar infarcts — causing small vessel fragility and rupture.

Characteristic locations mirror those of lacunar disease:

| Location | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Basal ganglia / external capsule | 60–65% |
| Thalamus | 15–20% |
| Pons / deep cerebellar nuclei | ~10% |

Risk factors include advancing age, chronic hypertension, and diabetes. Imaging often reveals chronic white matter changes and microhemorrhages on GRE/SWI MRI sequences as corroborating evidence.

**Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA)** is discussed in detail below. It is the predominant cause of lobar hemorrhage in normotensive elderly patients, but accounts for only about half of lobar ICH in this population.

### Macrovascular Etiologies

These should be actively sought, particularly in younger patients. They are the most common cause of ICH in young adults and often require definitive interventional or surgical management to prevent rebleeding.

- **Aneurysmal rupture** — almost always has a subarachnoid component; often frontal (ACA/AComm) or temporal (MCA); younger patients or family history of aneurysm should raise suspicion.
- **Arteriovenous malformation (AVM)** — most common macrovascular cause in young adults.
- **Cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT)** — associated with hypercoagulable states, oral contraceptive use, peripartum state, cranial infection; unique imaging features (bilateral hemorrhages, excess edema, gyriform pattern).
- **Cavernous malformation** — characteristic "popcorn" appearance on T2 MRI; may be occult on CT and CTA.
- **Dural arteriovenous fistula (dAVF)**.
- **Mycotic aneurysm** — complication of endocarditis or bacteremia.
- **Moyamoya disease**.

### Other Etiologies

- **Malignancy** — primary or metastatic (especially renal cell carcinoma, melanoma, choriocarcinoma).
- **Hemorrhagic conversion of ischemic stroke**.
- **RCVS** (reversible cerebral vasospasm syndrome).
- **Coagulopathy** — therapeutic anticoagulation, thrombocytopenia, liver disease.
- **Sympathomimetic drug use** — cocaine, amphetamines.
- **Vasculitis** — polyarteritis nodosa, lupus, ANCA vasculitis, rheumatoid arthritis, sarcoidosis, drug-induced, Henoch-Schönlein purpura.

## Pathophysiology

### Hematoma Expansion

Up to one-third of patients whose CT is obtained within 3 hours of ictus will demonstrate hematoma expansion. This is the primary driver of early neurological deterioration and a key therapeutic target.

- Expansion is most common within the **first 6–24 hours** (and may occur up to 48 hours in anticoagulated patients).
- Risk factors for expansion: active anticoagulation, shorter time from symptom onset, CT signs of active bleeding (spot sign, swirl sign, black hole sign, blend sign).
- The mechanism involves ongoing bleeding from the ruptured vessel and coagulopathy-mediated failure of clot formation.

### Perihematomal Edema (Cytotoxic Edema)

Cytotoxic edema develops surrounding the hematoma and worsens over several days, typically peaking around **5–6 days** after ictus. This is distinct from vasogenic edema and, critically, **does not respond to corticosteroids**. Steroids have no role in ICH management.

### Intracranial Pressure (ICP) Elevation

ICP rises through several mechanisms:
1. Direct mass effect of the hematoma.
2. Perihematomal edema compounding mass effect over days.
3. Intraventricular extension of blood causing obstructive hydrocephalus.
4. Mass effect on ventricular outflow tracts causing hydrocephalus.

### Terminology Note

"Intracerebral hemorrhage" refers to primary or secondary non-traumatic hemorrhage into the brain parenchyma. Avoid the term "hemorrhagic stroke," as it conflates ICH with subarachnoid hemorrhage and hemorrhagic conversion of ischemic stroke — all of which have distinct management pathways.

## Clinical Presentation

### Signs & Symptoms

ICH classically presents as the **abrupt onset of focal neurological deficits** with associated headache, nausea/vomiting, and often hypertension. Unlike ischemic stroke, symptoms typically progress over minutes to hours rather than being maximal at onset, reflecting ongoing hematoma expansion.

**Location-specific findings:**

| Location | Typical Deficits |
|---|---|
| Basal ganglia / putamen | Contralateral hemiplegia, hemisensory loss, gaze deviation toward lesion |
| Thalamus | Contralateral hemisensory loss, may have gaze deviation toward lesion, hypersomnia |
| Pons | Quadriplegia, pinpoint pupils, horizontal gaze palsy, coma; high mortality |
| Cerebellum | Ataxia, nausea/vomiting, headache, ipsilateral dysmetria; may have no focal motor deficit early |
| Lobar | Variable, depending on lobe affected; seizures more common |

### Key History Points

- **Exact time of symptom onset** — critical for management decisions.
- **Anticoagulant/antiplatelet use** and timing of last dose.
- **Antihypertensive medications**.
- History of hypertension, coagulopathy, malignancy, drug use, or prior hemorrhage.
- Prior functional status and frailty (relevant to prognosis and surgical candidacy).

### Physical Exam Pearls

- **Altered consciousness** disproportionate to CT findings → consider seizure, hydrocephalus, or expanding hematoma.
- **Pupillary asymmetry or fixed pupils** → herniation.
- Cerebellar hemorrhage may present with **minimal motor deficits early** despite a life-threatening hematoma.

> [!warning] **Red Flags Requiring Immediate Action**
> - Declining GCS or any sign of herniation (blown pupil, Cushing's triad).
> - Cerebellar hemorrhage with ANY neurological deterioration — these patients can deteriorate catastrophically within hours.
> - Evidence of brainstem compression.
> - Rapidly progressive deficit suggesting ongoing hematoma expansion.

## Diagnostic Workup

### Initial Evaluation

**History:** Establish exact symptom onset time, anticoagulant/antiplatelet use (with timing of last dose), and pertinent medical history.

**Initial laboratory panel:**
- Electrolytes, CBC (platelet count critical).
- INR, PTT, fibrinogen.
- Anti-Xa level or apixaban level for patients on oral Xa inhibitors.
- Toxicology screen if clinically indicated.
- Pregnancy evaluation as appropriate.
- ABG/VBG if intubated.

### Imaging Strategy

#### Noncontrast CT — First-Line

The **noncontrast CT head** is the standard initial test for acute ICH. Blood clots within minutes to hours, rendering the hematoma hyperdense.

> [!warning] In anticoagulated patients, hematomas may appear **isodense or hypodense** — this is an important clue to coagulopathy and should not be mistaken for a subacute hemorrhage.

**Hematoma Volume — ABC/2 Method:**

Volume (mL) = (A × B × C) / 2

- **A** = largest hematoma diameter on any axial slice (cm)
- **B** = largest perpendicular diameter on the same axial slice (cm)
- **C** = number of CT slices with hemorrhage × slice thickness (cm)
  - Only count slices with ≥75% of hematoma volume; slices with 25–75% count as 0.5

**Volume interpretation:**

| Volume | Clinical Significance |
|---|---|
| < 30 mL | Relatively favorable prognosis |
| > 30 mL | Moderate-to-severe ICH (ICH score threshold) |
| > 60 mL | Predictor of poor outcome |
| > 70 mL | Previously considered uniformly lethal; surgical outcomes now possible |

> [!tip] **Location trumps volume.** 20 mL in the pons is far more dangerous than 40 mL in the frontal lobe.

**CT Signs of Hematoma Expansion (Noncontrast):**

| Sign | Description | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Swirl sign** | Blood of heterogeneous (mixed) density within hematoma | Indicates unclotted/active bleeding |
| **Black hole sign** | Hypodense region within hematoma, not contiguous with brain | 32% sensitivity, 94% specificity for expansion |
| **Blend sign** | Distinct hyperdense and hypodense regions within hematoma | Predictor of growth |
| **Fluid-fluid level** | Layering of blood products | Highly specific for coagulopathy or anticoagulant use |
| **Island sign** | ≥3 separate hemorrhages separate from main hematoma | Predictor of growth |

**Edema:** Not visible initially. Extensive or irregular edema raises concern for CVT, hemorrhagic conversion of ischemic stroke, or intratumoral hemorrhage.

#### CT Angiography (CTA) — Nearly Universal

CTA should be obtained for **nearly all patients** unless imaging and epidemiology are strongly consistent with hypertensive ICH. Mandatory scenarios include (2022 AHA/ASA):

- Lobar ICH in a patient **<70 years old**.
- Deep/posterior fossa ICH in a patient **<45 years old**.
- Deep/posterior fossa ICH in a patient **45–70 years old without hypertension**.

**Spot sign on CTA:** Contrast extravasation into the hematoma. Predicts expansion with 51% sensitivity and 85% specificity. Must not be in anatomic continuity with adjacent vessels.

#### CT Venography (CTV) — Selective

Obtain when CVT is suspected. Clues:

- **Epidemiological:** Young age (20–50), hypercoagulability (OCP, pregnancy, APS, sickle cell, dehydration), cranial infection (meningitis, mastoiditis).
- **Imaging:** Lobar or gyriform hemorrhage, bilateral hemorrhages, edema exceeding expectations.
- **Clinical:** Gradual onset of headache and deficits.

CTA and CTV can often be obtained together (CTAV) with a single contrast bolus.

#### Serial CT Scanning

In stable patients with preserved consciousness: **follow-up CT at ~6 hours and at 24 hours** is generally adequate to evaluate for expansion and final hematoma volume (2022 AHA/ASA). More frequent scanning is appropriate for neurological deterioration or patients unable to be examined (e.g., sedated).

#### MRI

Generally not obtained immediately due to logistics. If gadolinium is desired, administer within **2 days of ictus** — after this, blood becomes T1 hyperintense and may obscure underlying enhancement.

MRI is superior for detecting:
- Tumor with intratumoral hemorrhage.
- Ischemic stroke with hemorrhagic transformation.
- CAA — GRE/SWI reveals microhemorrhages at gray-white junction.
- Cavernous malformations (most sensitive modality; may be occult on CT and CTA).
- Hypertensive microangiopathy (white matter lesions, lacunar infarcts).

Non-gadolinium MRI is sufficient if searching for amyloid angiopathy.

#### Invasive Angiography

Definitive study for aneurysm, dural AVF, and vasculitis. Can be both diagnostic and therapeutic (embolization). Indicated when CTA cannot identify a cause in (2022 AHA/ASA):

1. Lobar ICH, patient <70 years old.
2. Deep/posterior fossa ICH, patient <45 years old.
3. Deep/posterior fossa ICH, patient 45–70 without hypertension or small vessel disease on imaging.
4. Primary intraventricular hemorrhage.

## Management

### Approach Overview

ICH management follows a structured sequence. Time to intervention matters — the most dangerous period for hematoma expansion is within the first 3–6 hours.

### [1] Coagulation Optimization

**Do not delay reversal while awaiting laboratory results.** Empiric reversal should begin immediately in patients on anticoagulants (2022 AHA/ASA).

**Anticoagulant reversal:**

| Agent | Reversal Strategy |
|---|---|
| **Warfarin** | 4-factor PCC (preferred over FFP for speed/volume) + IV Vitamin K |
| **Apixaban / Rivaroxaban / other -xabans** | Andexanet alfa (if available) or 4-factor PCC; check anti-Xa or apixaban level |
| **Dabigatran** | Idarucizumab (specific reversal agent); dialysis if unavailable |
| **Thrombolytics (tPA)** | Cryoprecipitate (fibrinogen replacement); antifibrinolytics (tranexamic acid, aminocaproic acid); FFP |
| **Heparin (UFH)** | Protamine sulfate |

**Antiplatelet agents:**
- Platelet transfusion is **not recommended** (PATCH trial showed harm). (27178479)
- **DDAVP** (desmopressin) may be considered for functional platelet impairment; RCT evidence is limited (DASH pilot trial). (37353276)

**Thrombocytopenia:** Target platelet count > 100,000/μL if possible.

**Tranexamic acid:**
- Reduces hematoma expansion at 24 hours (OR 0.82, 90% CI 0.70–0.97) but has not translated into improved mortality or functional independence (mirroring TICH-2 results).
- **May be considered** for hematoma expansion reduction (ESO/EANS 2025).
- TICH-3 trial is ongoing for further evaluation.

### [2] Blood Pressure Management

> [!warning] **BP control must be started immediately** — the benefit is greatest in the first hours when expansion risk is highest.

**2022 AHA/ASA targets:**

| Presenting SBP | Target |
|---|---|
| 150–220 mmHg | Lower to **130–150 mmHg** (reasonable) |
| < 130 mmHg goal | **Potentially harmful — avoid** |
| > 220 mmHg | Target **140–180 mmHg** |

Additional considerations:
- SBP drops of **>70 mmHg within the first hour** may cause harm (ESO/EANS 2025).
- After >24 hours in stable patients, transition to oral antihypertensives may be appropriate.
- **Treat pain and anxiety before initiating antihypertensives** — especially in intubated patients, hypertension is often a manifestation of undertreated pain.

**Preferred IV agents:**

| Agent | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Clevidipine** | Ultrashort half-life (~1 min), most titratable, smoothest control |
| **Nicardipine** | Widely available, effective; monitor closely for overshoot hypotension |
| Labetalol | Less titratable but commonly used |

**Rationale and risks of BP reduction:**
- Rationale: Reducing SBP should decrease hematoma expansion, particularly in the immediate post-hemorrhage period.
- Risk: Excessive reduction may impair perfusion of the penumbra surrounding the hematoma, worsening edema and ICP — a cascade of secondary brain insults. Renal failure can also result from excessive hypotension.

### [3] Surgical Management

#### Supratentorial Hematoma Evacuation

**Evidence from STITCH trials:**

| Trial | Population | Key Result |
|---|---|---|
| **STITCH-I** (2005) | 1,033 pts, medical vs. early surgery (median 30h to OR) | No difference overall; trend toward benefit for lobar hematoma <1 cm from cortex with GCS 9–12 |
| **STITCH-II** (2013) | 601 pts with superficial lobar ICH, conscious, no IVH | No significant outcome difference between early surgery and conservative management |

**Bottom line:** Routine early craniotomy is not supported. However, crossover from medical to surgical arm in these trials suggests surgery may benefit selected deteriorating patients.

**Minimally invasive surgery** (e.g., MISTIE III) is an area of active investigation and may be more promising than open craniotomy.

**Features favoring craniotomy:**

- Lobar hematoma superficial (<1 cm from brain surface).
- No intraventricular extension.
- Worsening trajectory with preserved brainstem reflexes ("window of intervenability").
- No severe comorbidities or coagulopathy.
- Good rehabilitation potential.
- Large hematoma with significant midline shift or ICP refractory to medical management.
- Underlying lesion (AVM, tumor) requiring resection.

**Features arguing against craniotomy:**
- Stable hematoma.
- Prior poor functional status or frailty.
- Coma.
- Loss of upper brainstem reflexes (Wijdicks 2025).

#### Cerebellar Hematoma

> [!warning] **Cerebellar ICH is uniquely dangerous** due to the enclosed posterior fossa — brainstem compression and obstructive hydrocephalus can develop rapidly (often within 12–24 hours). These patients need close monitoring and a low threshold for surgery.

Key features:
- Cerebellar hematomas have **better overall prognosis** than other ICH locations — but can deteriorate catastrophically.
- Upward transtentorial herniation of the cerebellum is a feared complication.
- **Ventriculostomy alone without decompression is NOT recommended** — it increases risk of upward herniation. The EVD pressure may also not accurately reflect posterior fossa pressure.

**Indications for immediate surgical evacuation (AHA/ASA 2022):**
- Hematoma volume **>15 mL**.
- Neurological deterioration.
- Brainstem compression.
- Hydrocephalus.

#### Intraventricular Hemorrhage (IVH) & External Ventricular Drain (EVD)

IVH occurs in **>40% of ICH patients** and worsens prognosis. Complications include obstructive hydrocephalus, elevated ICP, and neurogenic fever.

**EVD indications (AHA/ASA 2022 / ESO-EANS 2025):** Ventricular drainage should be performed when hydrocephalus is contributing to **reduced level of consciousness**, to reduce mortality.

Important caveat: EVD placement improves survival but does **not** improve good neurological outcomes — survivors often have poor functional status. This should factor into goals-of-care discussions.

**Intraventricular tPA (CLEAR III trial):**
- 500 patients randomized to intraventricular tPA vs. saline.
- Result: Improved survival; **no increase in good functional outcomes**.
- Selected centers use intraventricular tPA when IVH is the predominant pathology (not massive parenchymal hemorrhage) and when thrombus dissolution might resolve hydrocephalus.

### [4] Seizure Management

**Incidence:** Convulsive seizures occur in ~10% of patients; subclinical seizures are substantially more common.

**Risk factors for seizures:**
- Cortical involvement (lobar hemorrhages).
- Coexistent subarachnoid or subdural hemorrhage.
- Rebleeding.

**EEG Monitoring — Indications:**
- Impaired consciousness disproportionate to CT findings.
- Unexplained fluctuations in mental status.
- Any witnessed or suspected seizure activity.

EEG may also detect LPDs (lateralized periodic discharges), GPDs (generalized periodic discharges), SIRPIDs, and FIRDA, all of which may have clinical significance in ICH.

**Management:**
- **Seizure prophylaxis is NOT recommended.** Prophylactic phenytoin has not shown benefit and has significant side effects.
- Antiepileptic therapy is indicated for witnessed seizure, electrographic seizure, or nonconvulsive status epilepticus.

### [5] ICP and Sodium Management

- Avoid **hyponatremia or rapid sodium drops** — both can worsen cerebral edema and ICP.
- Routine hypertonic saline is not supported by evidence.
- **Hypertonic saline boluses or hypertonic bicarbonate** can be used as a bridge for acutely elevated ICP pending more definitive therapy (EVD, surgery).
- Mannitol may also be used for acute ICP management.

### [6] Fever and Temperature Management

- **Treat fever with acetaminophen** (analgesic benefit is an added bonus in this population).
- **Neurogenic fever** may occur, particularly with intraventricular extension, but remains a diagnosis of exclusion — rule out infection first. Refractory neurogenic fever can be treated with **bromocriptine**.
- The **INTREPID trial** found that enforced normothermia using automated surface temperature management devices does **not** improve functional outcomes. Routine targeted temperature management is not recommended.

### [7] DVT Prophylaxis

- **Initial therapy:** Intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC) devices — start immediately.
- **After 24–48 hours with stable ICH on serial imaging:** Low-dose unfractionated heparin (UFH) or low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) prophylaxis may be initiated (AHA/ASA 2022).
- Patients with coagulopathy or evidence of ongoing hemorrhage expansion require individualized management.

## Prognostication

### Overall Prognosis

ICH carries a poor prognosis:
- ~50% mortality at 1 year.
- ~70% mortality at 5 years.
- Only ~20% achieve functional independence at 1 year.

### Predictors of Poor Outcome

- Hematoma volume >60 mL.
- GCS <8.
- Deep or infratentorial location.
- Intraventricular extension.
- Advancing age.

### ICH Score

The ICH Score is the most widely used risk stratification tool. Higher scores correlate with higher 30-day mortality.

| Component | Points |
|---|---|
| GCS 3–4 = 2 pts; GCS 5–12 = 1 pt; GCS 13–15 = 0 pts | 0–2 |
| Age ≥80 years = 1 pt | 0–1 |
| ICH volume ≥30 mL = 1 pt | 0–1 |
| IVH present = 1 pt | 0–1 |
| Infratentorial origin = 1 pt | 0–1 |
| **Total** | **0–6** |

**Important limitations of the ICH Score:**
- Transient neurological impairment (sedation, hypercapnia) inflates the score falsely.
- Untreated hydrocephalus may cause low GCS that is reversible with EVD — prognostication before EVD is unreliable.
- No differentiation between small vs. massive IVH.
- No differentiation between pontine vs. cerebellar hemorrhage (very different surgical options).
- No age gradation within the ≥80 category (an 18-year-old is scored the same as a 79-year-old).
- No accounting for pre-existing frailty or functional status.

> [!warning] **Avoid early, definitive prognostication in ICH.** Self-fulfilling prophecy is a well-documented phenomenon in neurological injury — early withdrawal of care based on scores like the ICH score contributes to mortality without necessarily reflecting true biological ceiling.

### FUNC Score

The FUNC Score estimates the probability of functional independence at 90 days. Components include ICH volume, age, ICH location, GCS, and pre-ICH cognitive impairment. It shares many of the same limitations as the ICH Score.

## Special Topics

### Primary Intraventricular Hemorrhage

Primary IVH is hemorrhage into the ventricles without associated parenchymal ICH. It is rare, accounting for only ~3% of nontraumatic intracranial hemorrhage.

- **~90% are idiopathic.**
- ~10% have identifiable causes: hypertension, AVM/AVF/aneurysm, Moyamoya, coagulopathy, choroid plexus tumor or ependymal lesion.
- Investigation includes CT/CTA; if no parenchymal hemorrhage is identified, **catheter intra-arterial digital subtraction angiography is recommended** to exclude a vascular cause (AHA/ASA 2022).
- Management mirrors secondary IVH; EVD for acute obstructive hydrocephalus; intraventricular fibrinolytic therapy may benefit selected patients.

---

### Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy (CAA)

**Basics:** CAA results from deposition of beta-amyloid within small-to-medium cortical arteries, white matter arterioles, and leptomeningeal vessels. Clinical manifestations include lobar ICH, ischemic stroke, dementia, and reversible leukoencephalopathy.

**Epidemiology:**
- Prevalence ~5% in patients >65 years old.
- Accounts for ~15% of spontaneous ICH overall.
- **Most common cause of lobar ICH in normotensive elderly patients** — but note that only half of lobar ICH in the elderly is attributable to CAA.
- Associated with Alzheimer's disease.
- Often provoked or worsened by anticoagulation or thrombolysis.

**Radiology:**
- Hemorrhage usually lobar, preferentially occipital and parietal lobes.
- May have finger-like projections, or extension into subarachnoid, subdural, or intraventricular spaces.
- **GRE/SWI MRI** reveals microhemorrhages at gray-white junction and cortical convexities, with hemosiderosis.
- Clinical hint: elderly patient with cognitive decline + lobar/cortical hemorrhage + small cortical subarachnoid blood = likely CAA.

**Clinical implications:**
- Short-term prognosis after the acute hemorrhage is often favorable.
- High risk of **recurrence (~7.5%/year)** with subsequent cognitive decline.
- **Do NOT restart anticoagulation** in patients with confirmed CAA — recurrence risk is prohibitive.

#### Inflammatory CAA (CAA-ri)

A rare but important variant in which perivascular beta-amyloid triggers CNS vasculitis. Also known as amyloid beta-related angiitis (ABRA).

- Mean onset in the 70s.
- Presentation: acute or subacute headache, reduced consciousness, behavioral change, focal deficits, seizures.
- Radiology: vasogenic edema (subcortical white matter, with mass effect) ± leptomeningeal enhancement. MRI should show features of underlying CAA (microhemorrhages). MRA may show medium artery thickening/stenosis.
- Differential: PML, PCNSL, glioma, CVT, cerebritis/toxoplasmosis, tumefactive MS, AHLE, PRES.
- Treatment: corticosteroids → cyclophosphamide.

### Differential Diagnosis of Multiple Microhemorrhages on MRI

| Etiology | Distribution Clue |
|---|---|
| Cerebral amyloid angiopathy | Gray-white junction, cortical; spares basal ganglia |
| Hypertensive microangiopathy | Basal ganglia, thalami, external capsule, pons, deep cerebellar nuclei |
| DAI (diffuse axonal injury) | Gray-white junction, dorsolateral brainstem, corpus callosum splenium |
| Hemorrhagic metastases | Variable; renal cell carcinoma, melanoma |
| PRES | Posterior predominant |
| Fat embolism / gas embolism | Diffuse, often watershed |
| Septic emboli (endocarditis) | Cortical and subcortical |
| Radiation vasculopathy | Within radiation field |
| Vasculitis | Variable |
| Cavernous malformation (type 4) | Variable |
| CADASIL / Moyamoya | Deep structures |
| Thrombotic microangiopathies | Diffuse |

## Clinical Pearls

> [!tip] **Pearl 1: Treat pain before starting antihypertensives.**
> In intubated ICH patients, hypertension is often a manifestation of pain. Adequate analgesia and sedation may substantially lower BP and reduce the need for, or dose of, IV antihypertensives.

> [!tip] **Pearl 2: Cerebellar hemorrhage is a neurosurgical emergency in disguise.**
> A patient with a 16 mL cerebellar hematoma and intact brainstem reflexes can be talking to you one hour and herniating the next. The posterior fossa is unforgiving. Get neurosurgery involved early and watch these patients relentlessly.

> [!tip] **Pearl 3: Don't prognosticate too early.**
> The ICH score and FUNC score are population-based tools with major limitations. Undertreated hydrocephalus, sedation, and metabolic derangements can all mimic a devastated exam. Early withdrawal of care in ICH contributes to self-fulfilling mortality. Reassess after reversible factors are treated.

> [!tip] **Pearl 4: Isodense or hypodense blood = coagulopathy until proven otherwise.**
> When the hematoma doesn't look as bright as expected on noncontrast CT, think about anticoagulation or coagulopathy. A fluid-fluid level is highly specific for this and should prompt an immediate anticoagulant workup.

> [!tip] **Pearl 5: Don't give steroids for ICH.**
> Perihematomal edema in ICH is cytotoxic, not vasogenic. Steroids are ineffective and may cause harm (hyperglycemia, infection risk). This is a common pitfall.

> [!tip] **Pearl 6: Get CTA on almost everyone.**
> Even in a patient whose clinical and imaging profile suggests hypertensive ICH, a vascular malformation or aneurysm can hide beneath the hematoma. The consequences of missing a treatable structural lesion are enormous. CTA is fast, widely available.

> [!tip] **Pearl 7: CAA patients should not be anticoagulated.**
> Once CAA is confirmed or strongly suspected as the cause of ICH, anticoagulation should not be restarted. The ~7.5%/year recurrence rate makes the hemorrhagic risk prohibitive in most cases, even for atrial fibrillation.

> [!tip] **Pearl 8: EVD in IVH saves lives, not function.**
> Be prepared to counsel families on this nuance. EVD placement for hydrocephalus-related reduced consciousness will improve survival but survivors often have poor neurological outcomes. Goals-of-care discussions should happen early and explicitly.

## Evidence & Landmark Trials

### Foundational Trials

**STITCH-I (2005)** — Mendelow et al., *Lancet*
- 1,033 patients randomized to early surgical evacuation vs. initial medical management for spontaneous supratentorial ICH.
- Median time to surgery: 30 hours.
- **Result:** No outcome benefit for early surgery overall. Trend toward benefit for lobar hematoma extending within 1 cm of cortex (GCS 9–12). Trend toward harm for deep ICH or GCS <9.
- Impact: Established that routine early craniotomy is not indicated.

**STITCH-II (2013)** — Mendelow et al., *Lancet*
- 601 patients enriched for those expected to benefit from surgery (conscious, superficial lobar ICH <1 cm from surface, volume 10–100 mL, no IVH).
- **Result:** No significant benefit from early surgery vs. conservative management.
- Impact: Further solidified conservative approach as standard; leaves room for surgery in deteriorating patients.

**INTERACT2 (2013)** — Anderson et al., *NEJM*
- 2,839 patients with acute ICH and elevated SBP randomized to intensive BP lowering (target <140) vs. standard care (target <180).
- **Result:** No significant improvement in primary outcome (death or major disability), but functional outcomes slightly favored intensive treatment. Intensive lowering appeared safe.
- Impact: Supported aggressive BP management; informed subsequent guidelines.

**ATACH-II (2016)** — Qureshi et al., *NEJM*
- 1,000 patients randomized to SBP target <140 vs. <180 within 4.5 hours of symptom onset.
- **Result:** No reduction in death or disability with intensive BP lowering. Renal adverse events were more common in the intensive arm.
- Impact: Raised concern about targeting SBP <130; current guidelines recommend a floor of 130.

**TICH-2** — Tranexamic acid in ICH
- Tranexamic acid reduced hematoma growth but did not improve functional outcomes at 90 days.
- Impact: Supports use of tranexamic acid to limit expansion but does not endorse it for improving outcomes; TICH-3 ongoing.

**PATCH Trial (2016)** — Baharoglu et al., *Lancet*
- Platelet transfusion vs. standard care in antiplatelet-related ICH.
- **Result:** Platelet transfusion was associated with *worse* outcomes.
- Impact: Platelet transfusion is now actively discouraged in antiplatelet-related ICH.

**CLEAR III (2017)** — Ziai et al.
- 500 patients with IVH and EVD randomized to intraventricular alteplase vs. saline.
- **Result:** Improved survival with tPA; no improvement in functional outcomes.
- Impact: Selected use of intraventricular tPA in centers where IVH is dominant pathology.

**INTREPID Trial**
- Enforced normothermia with surface temperature management device vs. standard care.
- **Result:** No improvement in functional outcomes.
- Impact: Routine targeted temperature management is not recommended in ICH.

### Recent Evidence

The following recent publications reinforce or refine current practice:

- **Griffin M et al.** (2025). *Expert Rev Neurother* — "The latest therapeutic advances with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage." Comprehensive review of emerging therapeutic strategies including minimally invasive surgery, neuroprotection, and hemostatic agents.

- **ESO/EANS 2025 Guidelines** — Steiner T et al., *Eur Stroke J* — Most recent European guidelines covering BP management, hemostasis, surgical intervention, and post-acute care. Highlights: BP drops >70 mmHg in first hour may cause harm; tranexamic acid may be considered for expansion prevention; DVT prophylaxis recommendations.

- **Magid-Bernstein J et al.** (2022). *Circ Res* — "Cerebral Hemorrhage: Pathophysiology, Treatment, and Future Directions." Reviews current understanding of perihematomal edema, hematoma toxicity, and novel neuroprotective targets.

## Key Guidelines

**2022 AHA/ASA Guideline** — Greenberg SM, Ziai WC, Cordonnier C, et al. *Stroke* 2022.
- Most current North American guideline for spontaneous ICH management.
- Key recommendations: SBP 130–150 for presenting SBP 150–220; avoid SBP <130; CTA for nearly all patients; early IPC for DVT ppx with LMWH after 24–48 hours if stable; cerebellar ICH >15 mL or with deterioration/brainstem compression → emergent surgery; EVD for hydrocephalus with LOC.

**2025 ESO/EANS Guideline** — Steiner T et al. *Eur Stroke J* 2025.
- Comprehensive European update. Aligns broadly with AHA/ASA 2022 with nuanced additions on BP rate of change, tranexamic acid, and surgical criteria.

**Prior 2015 AHA/ASA Guideline** — Hemphill JC et al. *Stroke* 2015.
- Historical reference; largely superseded by 2022 update but established many foundational principles.
